####### Abstract.

Predicting whether an LLM agent will eventually fail has emerged as a promising direction for supporting intervention during agent’s run. Recent approaches report strong predictive performance, often with AUROC values between and . However, the predictors are typically trained combining agent’s runs (also called, trajectories or traces) from many different tasks. We hypothesized that the strong performance may come partly from recognizing that some tasks are harder than others, rather than from detecting whether a particular run is heading toward failure. This distinction matters operationally because task-level difficulty supports decisions about where to allocate computation, whereas run-level failure prediction is needed to decide whether to intervene in an ongoing trajectory. To disentangle task-level difficulty from run-level failure signals, we conducted a study to analyze benchmarks containing repeated attempts of the same task by the same LLM agent.
We separate comparisons across different agents and task units from comparisons between successful and failed runs of the same unit.
Across the evaluated corpora, pooled AUROC is dominated by comparisons across different agent–task units: more than 99.93% of the underlying positive–negative pairs are cross-unit. Accordingly, predictors that never observe the current run can achieve high pooled performance, including a difficulty oracle with AUROC up to 0.945, while remaining at chance within task. This means that strong pooled performance may be more informative for deciding where to allocate computation than for deciding whether to intervene in a particular run. In contrast, early run-level discrimination is consistently weak across trajectory predictors, published and released monitors, and hidden-state probes, although it improves later in run and is stronger for weaker agents. Consistent with this distinction, under fixed token budgets, task-level allocation outperforms abort-only strategies, while early stopping becomes beneficial only when within-task AUROC reaches roughly 0.84–0.93, far above the 0.50–0.55 range observed for early monitors.
These findings highlight that failure prediction should be evaluated not only by how accurately it predicts outcomes, but by whether the captured signal supports the deployment decision the predictor is intended to make.

### 1. Introduction

Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents increasingly support multi-step tasks in software engineering and other domains (Liu et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2024). Unlike conventional LLM applications that primarily generate a response from a fixed input context, LLM-based agents operate through multi-step interactions in which they take actions, invoke tools, and incorporate observations from the environment while attempting to complete a task (Yao et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024). This increased autonomy enables agents to solve complex problems, but it also introduces substantial execution risk. Recent studies report failure rates ranging from to across seven agentic frameworks (Cemri et al., 2025). Such failures are costly because an unsuccessful run may consume considerable time, tokens, and computational resources before its eventual outcome becomes known (Du et al., 2026; Bai et al., 2026; Khanna, 2026).
These costs have motivated growing interest in agent failure prediction: determining, during execution, whether an ongoing agent run is likely to fail (Barke et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). An accurate failure predictor could support early intervention and avoid wasting resources. For example, by terminating an unpromising execution, requesting human assistance, or changing the agent’s strategy before additional resources are consumed.

Existing work commonly formulates the prediction problem as trajectory classification (Vasudev et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026; Cho et al., 2026). They collect successful and failed agent trajectories, train predictors on their trajectories, and use the resulting models to estimate the failure risk of new runs while they are still in progress. These predictors are commonly evaluated using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), which measures how well a predictor discriminates failed executions from successful ones (Vasudev et al., 2026; Cho et al., 2026). Within a benchmark and evaluation condition, a common practice is to pool predictions from executions of different task instances and compute AUROC over the resulting successful and failed runs (Cho et al., 2026; Vasudev et al., 2026; Pham et al., 2026). We refer to this aggregate measure as pooled AUROC. High pooled AUROC values are commonly interpreted as evidence that a predictor can identify executions that are likely to fail. However, this interpretation overlooks an important possibility: the predictor may distinguish tasks with different baseline failure rates rather than distinguish successful and failed executions of the same task.

This distinction matters because agent outcomes depend on both the task and the particular execution (Bjarnason et al., 2026; Krsteski and Meyer, 2026; Lee, 2026). Some tasks may fail substantially more often than others. When runs from many tasks are pooled together, a predictor can therefore obtain a high AUROC by assigning higher failure scores to tasks with high baseline failure rates and lower scores to tasks with low baseline failure rates (Janes et al., 2009; Borkan et al., 2019). Such a predictor can rank failures above successes across the benchmark even if it cannot distinguish a successful run from a failed run of the same task.
Evidence from prior work suggests that this possibility is realistic. A predictor of task success before execution begins, using only static task properties, reaches AUROC of on held-out SWE-bench Verified instances (Ge et al., 2026), within the range reported by systems that observe agent trajectories heavily. This does not imply that trajectories contain no useful information. Rather, it exposes an ambiguity in pooled evaluation: strong discrimination may arise from between-task differences, run-specific execution signals, or both.
Conflating task-level difficulty and run-level failure can therefore misrepresent a predictor’s suitability for runtime intervention. Task-level difficulty can help determine where to allocate computation, which LLM to use, or whether a task should be attempted. A runtime monitor must answer a different question: given that the agent is already executing this task, does the evidence from this particular run indicate that it is heading toward failure? The latter decision requires information that separates different outcomes of the same task.

Our study investigates how strongly the distinction between task-level difficulty and run-level failure affects the evaluation and use of agent failure predictors. Building on the distinction between pooled and conditional discrimination studied in statistics (Pepe, 2003; Janes and Pepe, 2009) and recent evidence of task-level confounds in agent evaluation (Mehtiyev and Assunção, 2026; Bulut, 2026), we ask three questions: (RQ1) how much reported predictive performance reflects differences between tasks rather than the state of the current execution, (RQ2) whether run-specific signals are available early enough to support intervention, and (RQ3) whether separating these signals changes the deployment decisions suggested by failure predictors. To answer these questions, we exploit repeated agent executions, in which a fixed agent configuration attempts the same task multiple times. Because the task and agent remain constant while the execution varies, successful
and failed attempts within the same model–task unit provide a setting for isolating run-level discrimination from between-unit differences. We analyze repeated-attempt corpora from SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024), SWE-rebench (Badertdinov et al., 2025), LiveClawBench (Long et al., 2026), and Latent Programming Horizons (Silva et al., 2026), spanning different agent scaffolds, models, and domains. Empirically, we decompose pooled AUROC into within-unit and cross-unit comparisons; compare run-blind baselines with trajectory-based predictors, published and released monitoring systems, and hidden-state probes at online-computable execution prefixes; and measure within-task discrimination while holding the task and acting model fixed. Finally, we evaluate the practical consequences of these signals through replay experiments under fixed token budgets, comparing task-level resource allocation, run-level aborting, and their combination.

Our results reveal a consistent progression across the three research questions. For RQ1, we find that pooled AUROC is dominated by cross-unit comparisons: more than 99.93% of the underlying positive–negative pairs compare different model–task units, allowing a difficulty oracle to reach pooled AUROC of 0.945 while remaining at 0.500 within task. For RQ2, early run-level discrimination is consistently weak across trajectory-based predictors, published and released monitoring systems, and hidden-state probes, although the signal becomes stronger later in execution and is more pronounced for weaker agents. For RQ3, this distinction changes the practical value of prediction: under fixed token budgets, task-level allocation outperforms abort-only strategies, while run-level monitoring becomes beneficial only when within-task AUROC reaches roughly 0.84–0.93, far above the 0.50–0.55 range observed for early monitors. These results show that high aggregate predictive performance can reflect task-level difficulty without implying effective runtime failure detection, and that separating these signals is necessary to determine which decisions they can support.

Our study makes three main contributions:

• 

A measurement framework for agent failure prediction.
We separate task-level difficulty from run-level failure prediction by decomposing pooled AUROC into within-unit and cross-unit components and by evaluating within-task discrimination on repeated executions.

• 

An empirical characterization of run-level failure signals.
Across multiple benchmarks, models, agent scaffolds, trajectory predictors, published monitoring systems, and hidden-state probes, we assess when run-specific information becomes available and whether it is strong enough to support intervention.

• 

Decision-oriented guidance for evaluation and deployment.
We connect predictive performance to operational use by comparing task-level allocation with run-level aborting under fixed compute budgets, yielding practical guidance for researchers, tool builders, and practitioners on how failure predictors should be evaluated and used.

Taken together, our contributions change how failure-prediction performance should be interpreted for LLM agents. A high pooled AUROC can be useful evidence that failures are associated with predictable differences across tasks, but it does not by itself establish that a monitor can recognize which particular execution of a task will fail. The distinction matters operationally: task-level information supports decisions about where to spend computation, whereas run-level information supports decisions about whether to stop the execution currently in progress.

### 2. Motivating Example

Consider two SWE-bench Verified tasks from Django, each attempted ten times by the same agent and model. This gives runs in total. The agent solves
django-10914 nine times but solves django-10973 only once. Now consider a predictor that never observes the trajectory and simply assigns each task its observed failure rate: for every run of the first task and for every run of the second. Evaluated across all runs, this predictor achieves AUROC . In either task, however, its AUROC is exactly because every attempt at the same task receives the same score. The predictor therefore distinguishes which task is harder, but not which particular run will fail.
At benchmark scale, this distinction becomes substantial: in C4-L (presented in Table 1), only about of the comparisons underlying pooled AUROC come from runs of the same task. This helps explain why strong pooled performance can coexist with weak run-level discrimination, and why task-level information may be more useful for allocating computation than for deciding whether to abort the current run.

### 3. Related Work

Research on agent failure prediction spans three questions: what makes a task likely to fail before execution, what an ongoing trajectory reveals about its eventual outcome, and whether acting on a prediction improves system behavior. These questions rely on different information and support different decisions. Several runtime predictors evaluate executions from multiple tasks together (Cho et al., 2026; Vasudev et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026), potentially mixing task-level difficulty with run-specific failure signals. We review these lines of work to clarify what existing evaluations establish about run-level monitoring.

#### 3.1. Task difficulty as a source of predictive performance

Pre-execution prediction is well established in software engineering, from defect-risk estimation using static software features (EsfandyariDoulabi et al., 2025) to LLM reliability analyses showing sensitivity to task difficulty and repeated evaluation (Sun et al., 2026). More directly, Ge et al. (Ge et al., 2026) estimate agent task difficulty using Item Response Theory (IRT) and task features. Their model reaches AUROC on held-out SWE-bench Verified tasks, while a problem-statement-only variant reaches , showing that substantial discrimination is possible before observing the current execution. Krsteski and Meyer (Krsteski and Meyer, 2026) similarly predict IRT-derived difficulty across 17 agentic benchmarks and show that aggregate AUC can remain high even when task-level scores do not distinguish individual outcomes. Our work, in contrast, asks whether high aggregate failure-prediction performance also captures run-specific information within repeated attempts of the same model–task pair, and whether task-level and run-level signals support different deployment decisions.

Mehtiyev and Assunção (Mehtiyev and Assunção, 2026) further show that trajectory-based associations depend on what is held fixed. Across 9,374 coding-agent trajectories, failed runs are longer when outcomes are compared within an agent, whereas the relationship often reverses when different agents are compared on the same task. Their results highlight the importance of conditioning when interpreting trajectory-level associations. We extend this idea by holding both the acting model and task fixed, allowing direct measurement of discrimination between successful and failed executions of the same model–task unit.
Bulut (Bulut, 2026) provides the closest measurement precedent to
our study. In LLM reasoning trajectories, Bulut shows that pooled
correctness-prediction AUROC can largely reflect problem difficulty rather
than attempt-specific information, motivating question-only baselines and
within-problem evaluation. Our work studies the analogous issue in runtime
failure prediction for tool-using LLM agents. We evaluate repeated
model–task executions across multiple agent benchmarks, behavioral
predictors, published and released monitoring systems, and hidden-state
probes, and then examine whether task-level and run-level information support
different deployment decisions under fixed compute budgets.

#### 3.2. Failure prediction during agent execution

Recent systems predict eventual success or failure from partial or complete agent trajectories. Cho et al. (Cho et al., 2026) abstract trajectories into finite-state representations and report full-trajectory AUROC of on Tau2-Bench telecom and on SWE-agent. Shi et al. (Shi et al., 2026) train predictors from partial trajectories and report 13–26% fewer execution steps while maintaining high prediction accuracy. Ruan et al. (Ruan et al., 2026) instead use hidden-state probes to trigger early aborts, substantially reducing generated tokens on TextCraft and WebShop.
Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2026) propose FailFast–RestartSmart, which predicts failure from partial trajectories and restarts unpromising runs, reducing token usage by 14.6–20.4% at a 5% false-positive rate. Their training objective includes successful–failed pairs from the same task to control instance difficulty. Our study addresses a complementary evaluation question: how much of a monitor’s measured discrimination remains when successful and failed executions are compared within the same model–task unit.

#### 3.3. Evaluation metrics and deployment decisions

Raghu et al. (Raghu et al., 2026) show that AUROC does not assess whether predicted success probabilities remain meaningful across trajectory prefixes and propose a trajectory proper score. Their concern is calibration across time; ours is complementary. We instead ask what source of variation produces the observed discrimination by separating comparisons across model–task units from comparisons between successful and failed executions of the same unit. Prediction quality also does not guarantee useful intervention. Vasudev et al. (Vasudev et al., 2026) show that a critic with strong offline failure-prediction performance can reduce task success when interventions disrupt trajectories that would otherwise succeed. This motivates evaluating monitors not only by predictive performance, but also by whether the captured signal supports the intended deployment decision.

#### 3.4. Synthesis and Research Gap

Taken together, prior work shows that task difficulty is predictable, trajectories can contain failure-related information, and prediction-guided intervention can help or harm depending on how that information is used. What remains unclear is whether strong aggregate failure-prediction performance reflects task/model difficulty or genuine discrimination between successful and failed executions of the same model–task unit. We address this gap by decomposing pooled AUROC into within-unit and cross-unit components, evaluating predictors at online-computable prefixes, and testing whether run-level signals improve decisions under a fixed compute budget.

### 4. Study Design

Following the Goal–Question–Metric (GQM) framework (Basili et al., 1994), the goal of our study is to analyze failure prediction for LLM agents for the purpose of understanding what reported predictive performance captures and whether the captured information supports useful deployment decisions, with respect to the distinction between task-level difficulty and run-level failure, from the perspective of researchers, tool builders, and practitioners, in the context of repeated-attempt agent benchmarks. We define a unit as a pair. We use within-task AUROC as shorthand for discrimination within a fixed model–task unit, so both the task and acting model are held fixed. The following sections present the research questions (RQs) and details of each step of our study.

#### 4.4. Prediction Conditions

We vary the information available to each predictor, separating information that is constant within a unit from information that changes across repeated attempts.

##### Run-Blind Predictors

Our main control is a predictor that never observes the current trajectory. The difficulty oracle assigns each run the failure rate of its unit. In multi-model corpora, this oracle reflects the baseline difficulty of the model-task unit and may therefore contain both task- and model-level variation. For pooled evaluation, that rate is computed with the current run left out so that its own outcome cannot predict itself. For within-task evaluation we use the ordinary unit mean, which is constant across runs and therefore yields .
We also evaluate deployable run-blind predictors that do not use target-unit outcome history. On C1 and C2, a pre-execution predictor uses issue-text TF-IDF features (Salton and Buckley, 1988) and repository identity, with tasks held out across folds. On C3, we evaluate three run-blind conditions: task metadata (difficulty tier and domain), acting-model identity, and their combination. All are available before execution.

##### Trajectory Predictors

Trajectory predictors use the first turns of the current execution. Behavioral features include turn counts, message and observation lengths and their dispersion, error tokens, repetition, tool diversity, and TF-IDF text features (Salton and Buckley, 1988). We fit logistic regression (Cox, 1958) and gradient boosting (Friedman, 2001) and generate out-of-fold predictions using GroupKFold (Pedregosa et al., 2011) grouped by task. Thus, a task never appears in both training and test portions of a fold. We compute and from the same cross-fitted predictions so that the estimands differ only in the pairs being compared. C3 supports an additional decomposition of task history and current-run information. uses only the unit’s failure rate estimated from its other runs, uses only the current trajectory, and combines both. The difference between and therefore measures what the current trajectory adds once outcomes from previous attempts on the same model–task unit are known.

##### Published and Released Systems

To determine whether our findings are specific to our predictors, we also evaluate systems developed independently of our pipeline. We reimplement Automata (Cho et al., 2026) because it reports the highest pooled AUROC in our survey () and is evaluated on SWE-agent (C1), allowing a same-corpus comparison. We first verify that our reconstruction matches its published SWE-agent result () on complete trajectories, then evaluate fixed prefixes under both and . The reconstruction uses the published activity-abstracted automaton with gradient boosting; additional implementation checks are reported in the supplementary material (Anonymous, 2026).
We also evaluate released systems directly. EarlyEval (Shi et al., 2026) is particularly relevant because it targets early failure prediction, reports – fewer execution steps at – accuracy, and requires no reconstruction. We run its released features on the pre-registered five-fold C2 split, where each of the instances appears in test data once. Its step- checkpoint predicts before the agent acts, providing a direct measure of pre-execution discrimination. We additionally evaluate the released one-class monitor of Dubey (2026) on the same C2 folds and run EarlyEval on C1’s three Llama-3 models to test whether the pattern extends across acting models.

#### 4.5. Hidden-State Probes

Observable trajectory features may miss outcome information encoded internally by the acting model. C4 allows us to test this possibility because its weights and exact trajectory tokens are available for replay.
We cut each trajectory after assistant turn and extract the hidden state of the last emitted token from layers , sampling the 40-layer model at approximately quarter-depth intervals. Each retained prefix is replayed in full. We fit an -regularized linear logistic probe to the raw -dimensional activation. The probe is deliberately linear so that we test whether outcome information is directly recoverable from the representation without introducing substantial additional predictive capacity.

Each probe uses GroupKFold grouped by task. Regularization strength is selected with a nested GroupKFold inside each training fold and never using test data. We compare these probes with a three-feature length baseline consisting of absolute token position, its logarithm, and assistant-turn count.
We validate the probing pipeline in four ways: (1) we verify model loading with teacher-forced accuracy; (2) replay stored token identifiers directly; (3) compare the within-task AUROC estimator with an independent implementation to agreement; and (4) evaluate positive and negative controls using an outcome-encoding feature, Gaussian noise, and shuffled labels. Numerical validation results are reported with the probe results.

#### 4.6. Prefix Definition and Leakage Control

All trajectory-based predictors satisfy the same deployment constraint: they may use only information available when the prediction is made. In particular, final trajectory length is unknown while an execution is still running.
We therefore define checkpoints by absolute turn count rather than by a fraction of the completed trajectory. Fractional checkpoints depend on final length and therefore introduce information unavailable online. For Automata, we evaluate both its original fractional checkpoint and deployment-valid checkpoints based on observed turns and tokens, allowing us to quantify the effect of this leakage.
Later checkpoints create a separate selection issue because runs that terminate early disappear from evaluation. To distinguish increasing predictive signal from changing cohort composition, we additionally evaluate a fixed C1 cohort containing only trajectories that reach at least ten turns, so the same runs are compared across early checkpoints.

#### 4.7. Analysis by Research Question

##### RQ1: Pooled versus Within-Unit Evaluation

RQ1 first computes the same-unit pair weight from Section 4.3, which measures the share of positive–negative comparisons underlying pooled AUROC that come from the same unit. We then evaluate the difficulty oracle, deployable pre-execution predictors, and the C3 run-blind conditions under both and , comparing them with trajectory-based predictors. This determines how much pooled discrimination can be obtained without observing the current execution and how much remains once comparisons are restricted to repeated attempts from the same model–task unit.

##### RQ2: Run-Level Discrimination

RQ2 evaluates at online-computable prefixes for behavioral predictors, published and released systems, and hidden-state probes. On C3, the , , and conditions quantify the incremental information provided by the current trajectory after accounting for previous outcomes from the same unit.
We additionally examine trajectory depth and acting-model capability. For depth, we use the fixed C1 cohort from Section 4.6 and evaluate a pre-registered same-unit conditional-logit ranker trained directly on within-unit comparisons. For capability, we compare within-task discrimination across C3’s models and within same-family model pairs. Because models differ in factors other than capability, this analysis is interpreted as associative rather than causal.

##### RQ3: Deployment Decisions

RQ3 evaluates whether task-level and run-level information support different decisions under a fixed compute budget. We use replay so that both cost and outcome come from recorded executions. At each decision, a policy selects a task and samples uniformly with replacement from its recorded runs. The sampled run contributes its recorded cost and outcome, and solved tasks are removed from further selection. The primary outcome is the number of distinct tasks solved within the budget.
We compare round-robin, static difficulty ranking transferred from another model, and Budgeted Thompson Sampling (Xia et al., 2015). For each cost-aware policy, the mean token cost of a task is computed once from all recorded runs of the target model and held fixed throughout replay. The static transferred ranking orders tasks by donor-model success rate divided by target model mean cost. #### 4.8. Statistical Analysis

Repeated runs from the same unit are dependent, so the unit is our primary clustering and resampling level.

##### Uncertainty and between-unit variation.

We report bootstrap confidence intervals by resampling units rather than individual runs. For the acting-model capability analysis, we instead resample models. We also report the intraclass correlation (ICC), with a finite-repeat correction for small unit sizes, as a descriptive measure of how much outcome variation lies between units (Shrout and Fleiss, 1979).

##### Early-prefix tests.

At intervention-relevant prefixes, we test using within-unit permutation tests on out-of-fold predictions. Outcomes are shuffled within each unit while preserving its numbers of successful and failed runs. Non-significant results are interpreted as limited detectable signal rather than evidence that the true effect is exactly zero.

### 7. Discussion

##### Implications of the findings.

Our results separate task-level predictability from run-level predictability. Pooled AUROC is dominated by cross-unit comparisons, allowing predictors that never observe the current run to achieve strong aggregate performance; for example, a task-difficulty scorer reaches pooled AUROC while remaining at within task. In contrast, early within-unit discrimination is consistently weak across the monitors and probes we evaluate, and the signal that does emerge tends to appear later and for weaker acting models. This distinction matters operationally: task-level information supports compute allocation, whereas run-level information is required for deciding whether to stop an ongoing execution. Under fixed token budgets, allocation outperforms abort-only strategies, while the monitor does not improve throughput.

##### Implications for evaluation.

Pooled AUROC alone is therefore insufficient for evaluating runtime monitors. We recommend reporting within-unit AUROC, the same-unit pair weight, and the pooled–within gap alongside the aggregate score. Evaluation checkpoints should also use only information available during execution; fractional checkpoints based on final trajectory length can leak future information and inflate performance. Finally, intervention policies should be compared against task-level allocation baselines under the same compute budget.

##### Scope of the conclusions.

We do not claim that trajectories contain no run-level information or that monitoring is inherently ineffective. Rather, the early signal we observe is weak, and the evaluated monitors remain far below the roughly – within-task AUROC at which aborting becomes beneficial in our replay. These thresholds depend on the benchmark and budget, and our deployment results are based on recorded-run replay rather than live intervention. For previously unseen tasks with no execution history, task difficulty must instead be estimated from pre execution task features, transferred from related models or settings, or learned from initial attempts. Our strongest allocation results therefore apply most directly when repeated-attempt information or transferable task difficulty estimates are available.

### 8. Threats to validity

##### Construct Validity.

Our analyses rely on benchmark-provided outcome labels, so label errors could weaken the measured relationship between trajectory information and outcome and pull within-task AUROC toward . We therefore test sensitivity to plausible label noise. The observed mislabelling rate is too small to explain the pooled–within gap or to hide effects around or higher, although smaller effects remain possible.

##### Internal Validity.

Automata is reconstructed from its published description because the original artifacts are unavailable. We mitigate implementation differences by reproducing its published SWE-agent pooled result and by separately evaluating systems with released implementations. We also use fixed observed-turn checkpoints to avoid leakage from final trajectory length, and a fixed C1 cohort to reduce survivor effects at later checkpoints. Because models in the capability analysis differ in more than capability alone, we interpret that result as associative rather than causal.

##### External Validity.

Our main corpora are coding-heavy, although C3 includes non-coding domains. We therefore examine the same structural quantities on four non-coding tau2-bench domains and observe comparable patterns. This supports the broader relevance of the pooled-versus-within issue, but not the generalization of all trajectory-level findings. Hidden-state results are limited to the two open C4 models, and the deployment analysis uses replay rather than live intervention. The allocation results also apply most directly to settings with repeated attempts.

##### Statistical Conclusion Validity.

Within-task AUROC is defined only for mixed units, so our estimates are conditional on units containing both successes and failures. Alternative weighting schemes do not materially change the early estimates. Near-chance AUROC should also be interpreted cautiously: failure to reject does not imply that no run-level signal exists. We therefore describe the early signal as weak rather than absent and report confidence intervals, clustered resampling, within-unit permutation tests, and Holm–Bonferroni correction.

### 9. Conclusion

In this paper, we examined what failure-prediction performance measures for LLM agents and whether those predictions support useful deployment decisions. Using benchmarks with repeated attempts of the same model-task unit, we separated task-level discrimination from discrimination between successful and failed runs of the same task. We evaluated run-blind baselines, trajectory predictors, hidden-state probes, and published and released failure-prediction systems, and then used replay experiments to study the consequences for compute allocation.

Our results show that strong pooled AUROC can largely reflect differences between units, and in particular differences in task difficulty, rather than information about the current run.
More than of the positive–negative pairs underlying pooled AUROC compare runs from different units, allowing predictors that never observe the current trajectory to achieve strong pooled performance while remaining near chance within task. Early within-task discrimination is generally weak across the evaluated predictors and monitoring systems, although the signal becomes stronger later in execution and is more visible for weaker acting models.

This distinction also matters operationally. Under a fixed token budget, task-level allocation outperforms aborting runs with the evaluated monitors, and adding the monitor to an allocation policy does not improve throughput under the studied conditions. In the C4-L sensitivity analysis, within-task AUROC of approximately – is required before aborting becomes beneficial.

Overall, our study shows that failure-prediction evaluation should distinguish task-level predictability from run-level predictability. We recommend reporting within-task AUROC and the same-unit pair weight alongside pooled AUROC, evaluating predictors only at prefixes available during execution, and comparing run-level intervention policies against task-level allocation baselines. Doing so makes clear what information a failure predictor captures and whether that information supports the deployment decision for which the predictor is intended.
