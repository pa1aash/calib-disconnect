## Not One Belief: Answer Payloads, Persistence, and Dissociated Confidence Signals in Conversational Question Answering

Duc-Vinh Tran

Email: vinh.td225538@sis.hust.edu.vn

Affiliation: School of Information and Communication Technology, Hanoi University of Science and Technology, Hanoi, 100000, Vietnam

####### Abstract

Conversational question-answering systems are expected to fix their errors when users push back, and to signal which answers to trust. In short-answer question answering, we split each user follow-up into its speech act (re-ask, agree, or say the answer is wrong) and its payload (an answer string, correct or not). On the same transcripts we measure four confidence signals: verbalized confidence, length-normalized sequence likelihood, a hidden-state linear probe fitted at the first turn and then frozen, and the self-evaluation probability P(True). Answers change when the user supplies an answer string. They almost never change under the speech act alone: in every retained full-size model–dataset cell, the correct answer converts at least four times as many wrong answers as re-asking or agreement. The four signals disagree on the same turns. On Qwen2.5-7B the frozen probe rises both on answers that stay wrong and on answers that switch to the correct one, so it goes with answer persistence rather than truth. This survives scoring on held-out questions, and Llama-3-8B with cleaned answers supports it; on four other models it does not hold up on held-out questions. For 81 Qwen2.5-7B errors that survive one re-ask, re-asking, a fresh chat, or an abstain option repairs almost none; the correct answer repairs 75–95%. In two preregistered replications on four models, every observed repair rate without an answer string stays below a preset 0.15, and a post hoc rerun with about 300 such items per model excludes 0.15 for every arm except the empty elicit. With one BM25-retrieved TriviaQA sentence, a sentence that contains the answer repairs 67–76% of persistent errors, and one that lacks it repairs at most 12% (16% under model-read labels). In a post hoc end-to-end test with BM25 passages, gating evidence on whether it contains the gold answer cut harm on TriviaQA, but a gate that does not know the answer repaired few errors, so the check remains a heuristic.

####### keywords
Large language models, Confidence calibration, Multi-turn dialogue, Sycophancy, Probing, Retrieval-augmented generation

### 1 Introduction

Chat assistants built on large language models (LLMs) now answer questions in customer support, tutoring, search, and clinical or legal help lines. There, a wrong answer costs something. Two questions follow, both about the reliability of conversational QA and evidence retrieval under user feedback. When a user pushes back, does the system fix its errors or just change its mind? And can the system’s own confidence tell the operator which answers to trust? Recent work on the second question asks whether LLM confidence stays calibrated as a conversation goes on Zhang
et al. (2026a); Zhang et al. (2026b). These studies usually score one confidence measure (verbalized confidence, the self-evaluation probability , or sequence likelihood) against correctness, as if each were an estimate of the same underlying belief.

For that to work, the measures must move together, and every follow-up must count as the same kind of evidence. In our data, neither holds. So we ask a simpler question: when a user sends a follow-up message, which quantity does it change? Figure 1 summarizes the answer. We split each follow-up into two parts. The speech act is what the user does: re-ask, agree, or say the model is wrong. The payload is an optional answer string in the message, either the correct (“gold”) answer or a plausible wrong one (a “foil”). Payloads change the model’s answer. Speech acts alone almost never repair an error. Changes to the answer, whether or not the new answer is true, move a frozen probe readout that, on Qwen2.5-7B, goes with answer persistence. We call it the persistence readout and make no psychological or causal claim about it. The other measures respond to other things: verbalized confidence to the number of turns, sequence likelihood to how fluently the model repeats itself, and to explicit contradiction. Two papers that calibrate different measures and call both “confidence” are not reporting comparable numbers.

Recent single-turn work already shows that confidence measures can come apart. Kumaran (2026) finds that verbal confidence predicts a model’s later decision to commit to or withhold an answer better than it predicts correctness, while calibrated token log-probabilities track correctness. Zhang
et al. (2026a) study multi-turn confidence as hints accumulate and propose InfoECE and , but they do not separate what the user does from the answer string the user gives. Kumaran (2026) work in a single turn. Our contribution is to control the user’s turn in multi-turn dialogue, separate the speech act from the answer payload, and measure four signals on the same transcripts.

###### What moves the answer.

We write IC for an item that is incorrect at turn 1 and correct at turn 2, and CI for the reverse. Re-asking (persist) and agreeing with the model (confirm) produce almost no IC changes, so the error survives. Stating the gold string produces many, with or without “you are wrong” around it. Stating a plausible foil produces many CI changes. An empty challenge (“I think that’s wrong”) makes answers unstable without pointing them anywhere. The string moves the answer. The stance does not. Models differ only in how they take up the string. Qwen2.5 and Llama-3-8B usually copy it verbatim (the fraction of IC answers that copy it is – on NQ and HotpotQA). Mistral-7B-v0.3 restates it inside a longer sentence.

###### The measures disagree.

On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, verbalized confidence falls as the user keeps talking, even when accuracy is flat (persist) or rising (correct). Length-normalized sequence likelihood often rises on the same turns. A linear probe on hidden states, fitted once on first-turn answers and then frozen, rises both on items that stay wrong and on items that switch to gold. Kadavath-style  Kadavath et al. (2022) barely moves, except under foil persuasion, where it collapses on CI changes (mean change overall and on CI items). One conversation, four different stories. The same holds for calibrated scores: a single user turn sends the expected calibration error (ECE) down for verbalized confidence, up for likelihood, and nowhere for (Section 5).

###### Persistent errors are hard to undo.

Asking again or starting a new chat does not fix a persistent error unless the user supplies a string. Among 81 Qwen2.5-7B TriviaQA items that are still wrong after one re-ask, re-asking through turn 5, a fresh chat, and an ABSTAIN option repair two, zero, and zero items. The model abstains on of these stay-wrong items and on of stay-correct items. A gold string at turn 3 repairs – of the same items, depending on phrasing, and an “I don’t know” turn repairs . Two preregistered replications (230 stay-wrong items from a second Qwen slice, 160 from Llama-3-8B, 139 from Mistral-7B-v0.3, and 61 from Qwen2.5-32B) keep every observed repair rate without an answer string below a threshold fixed in advance, under the preregistered answer matcher. A post hoc rerun on about 300 new stay-wrong items per model excludes for every such arm except the empty elicit (Section 6.1). With one BM25 sentence per TriviaQA question, retrieval repairs errors only to the extent that the sentence contains the answer string (Section 6.1).

###### Contributions.

We offer a map of interventions and their effects, not a new method:

1. 

The answer string, not the speech act, moves the answer. Answers follow a stated gold or foil string. Re-asking and agreement do not repair errors (Section 4).

2. 

The measures come apart across turns. Single-turn work has shown that verbalized confidence and correctness-tracking signals are not interchangeable Kumaran (2026); Miao and Ungar (2026). We show the same split in dialogue for four measures (verbalized confidence, likelihood, a frozen hidden-state probe, and ), in raw scores and in calibration error, and we show which part of the user turn each one responds to (Section 5).

3. 

On Qwen2.5-7B the frozen probe goes with answer persistence, and persistent errors are hard to undo. The persistence readout is a frozen probe on the current answer state (Section 3.4). On Qwen2.5-7B it rises on stay-wrong items and on IC items, also on held-out questions and above a shuffled-label control. Llama-3-8B with cut answers supports this; on the other four models it does not hold on held-out questions. Resetting the chat or offering abstention does not recover these errors; a gold string at turn 3 does (Section 6.1).

4. 

A retrieval heuristic. Before relying on evidence to repair an answer, check that it contains the answer string. This is a hypothesis for system design, not a validated rule. Across four models, a retrieved sentence without it repairs at most of stay-wrong items under any of our scorers, and it breaks up to of correct answers (Section 6.1).

The first three points share one argument: if two measures respond to different controlled interventions and disagree on the same transcript, they cannot both estimate a single latent conversational confidence. We do not propose a new uncertainty estimator. Section 8 lists what we do not claim.

### 2 Related work

###### Sycophancy.

Instruction-tuned LLMs tend to adopt a view the user states, even when it is wrong. Perez et al. (2023) documented this with model-written evaluations and found that it grows with model size and with reinforcement learning from human feedback. Sharma et al. (2024) showed that sycophancy is common across production assistants and traced it partly to human preference judgments that reward agreement. Wei et al. (2023) found that scaling and instruction tuning increase sycophancy, that models agree with a user’s clearly false arithmetic claims, and that fine-tuning on simple synthetic data reduces it. These works show that a user’s stated answer is persuasive. They usually bundle the stance (“I think you are wrong”) with the stated string. Our factorial design separates the two. For short factual answers, the string does almost all of the work.

###### Re-asking and answer flipping under pushback.

Other work studies what happens when a user challenges an answer, with or without offering a new one. In the FlipFlop experiment, Laban et al. (2023) showed that a simple “Are you sure?” makes models change their answers and lowers accuracy. Xie et al. (2024) found that models waver under skeptical, negating, or misleading follow-ups even when the first answer was correct, and proposed a training method to reduce this. Wang et al. (2023) showed that ChatGPT often cannot defend a correct answer against invalid arguments. Huang et al. (2024) found that models rarely improve their reasoning by intrinsic self-correction without outside feedback, and Laban et al. (2026) showed that performance drops across many tasks in multi-turn conversation. Our results agree on one side and add the other: empty challenges break correct answers (CI), but neither challenges nor re-asks repair wrong ones (IC). We also test whether a persistent error survives a chat reset.

###### Verbalized confidence.

Asking a model to state its confidence is the cheapest measure in deployed systems. Lin et al. (2022) showed that a fine-tuned GPT-3 can express calibrated uncertainty in words. Without such fine-tuning, the stated confidence of instruction-tuned models is often overconfident or poorly calibrated Xiong et al. (2024), though prompting can help, especially for models trained with human feedback Tian et al. (2023). Geng et al. (2024) survey confidence estimation and calibration for LLMs. Calibration is usually measured with ECE Guo et al. (2017). In our dialogues, the movement of verbalized confidence is mostly a Qwen2.5-7B effect of turn depth, not a response to evidence.

###### Self-evaluation with .

Kadavath et al. (2022) asked a model whether its own proposed answer is true and read the probability of “True”. They found this fairly well calibrated for large models on many single-turn tasks. In our dialogues ranks correct answers well at both turns but barely moves under re-asking or agreement. It responds mainly to contradiction by a foil.

###### Probing hidden states for truthfulness.

Several papers train classifiers on hidden activations to read out whether a statement or answer is true. Azaria and
Mitchell (2023) trained such a classifier on true and false statements. Marks and Tegmark (2024) reported linear structure that separates true from false statements. Burns et al. (2023) found latent knowledge without labels. Orgad et al. (2025) showed that truthfulness signals concentrate in the exact answer tokens, generalize poorly across datasets, and that a model may encode the correct answer while generating a wrong one. A probe can learn the task itself rather than reveal what the representation encodes, so probe results need care Hewitt and Liang (2019). We fit a probe at turn 1, freeze it, and follow it after the user speaks again.

###### Confidence as commitment.

Two recent single-turn studies are close to our result on the measures. Kumaran (2026) used a two-stage task: a model answers and reports its confidence, then later decides whether to give the answer to a user or abstain. Across several models, verbal confidence predicted the later commit-or-abstain decision better than it predicted correctness, while calibrated token log-probabilities tracked correctness. In Gemma models, a post-answer hidden state encoded the later decision, and steering along a direction specific to verbal confidence shifted abstention. Miao and Ungar (2026) found that a linear accuracy probe and the direction behind verbalized confidence are nearly orthogonal, and used activation steering to bring verbalized confidence closer to the probe. We do not claim these points as new. Our study differs in three ways. The user turn is controlled, so we can separate the speech act from the answer string. The probe is fitted at turn 1 and frozen, and we follow the same items across turns. And we study the frozen probe readout, not verbalized confidence: in our dialogues, on Qwen2.5-7B, it is the probe that moves with the answer the model keeps. We run no steering, so unlike these two studies our evidence is correlational. Sarkar et al. (2026) show, in a collaborative dialogue task between two LLMs, that a partner’s assertion can shift a model’s linear representation of whether a proposition is true. Our probe result points the same way for short factual answers.

###### Multi-turn calibration.

The closest work studies confidence across turns. Zhang
et al. (2026a) evaluate verbalized confidence, , and a new score as hints accumulate, and propose an information-aware ECE (InfoECE) that requires confidence to rise steadily as gold information arrives. Zhang et al. (2026b) show that persuasion worsens a turn-level ECE (ECE@T) and that sequence likelihood can rise when the answer flips to a wrong one, and they train a calibrator, MTCal, to repair the score. Both ask how well a score tracks correctness. We use their setting (short-answer dialogue under persuasion) and ask an earlier question: which quantity a given user turn moves (Table 1).

###### Knowledge conflicts and retrieval.

Models follow context that contradicts their stored knowledge to varying degrees Longpre et al. (2021); Xie et al. (2024), and whether retrieved context is sufficient to answer the question predicts errors in retrieval-augmented generation Joren et al. (2025). Lathkar (2026) reports that, in multi-hop retrieval-augmented QA, one confirmed intermediate fact can raise the rate of confident wrong answers before complete evidence lowers it. That work is single-turn and measures confidence; we measure whether evidence repairs an error that has already survived a re-ask. In our data, evidence repairs such an error only when it contains the answer string.

### 3 Experimental setup

#### 3.1 Dialogue protocol

Each dialogue starts at turn 1 (T1) with a short-answer question. At turn 2 (T2), and at later turns when used, the user sends one of four core messages, fixed across models:

persist

Please answer again, briefly. Do not change your answer unless you are sure you were wrong.

confirm

Yes, that’s correct. Please answer again, briefly, in one short phrase.

persuade

One of five challenge templates plus a foil.

correct

The same five templates plus a gold alias.

Ablations keep the same T1 and change only the T2 message, to separate the speech act from the payload. gold_plain and foil_plain state a string without saying “wrong”. challenge_empty challenges the answer without offering a replacement. confirm_strong repeats the model’s T1 answer and calls it correct. persist_short is Again, briefly. Exact wording is in Online Resource 1, Sect. A.

#### 3.2 Notation and outcome categories

We compare each item’s correctness at turn with its correctness at T1. An item is stay-correct (SC) if it is correct at both turns, stay-wrong (SW) if it is wrong at both, IC if it changes from incorrect to correct, and CI if it changes from correct to incorrect. We report IC and CI as counts out of the number of items (usually 200) or as rates. For a measure , is its change from T1 to T2, averaged over the stated items. The copy rate is the fraction of IC answers that repeat the payload string exactly. A unit is one modeldataset combination. Seed copies of the same unit are not counted as independent.

#### 3.3 Confidence measures

We use the four quantities that current multi-turn papers treat as interchangeable measures of confidence. The list is not exhaustive. Token entropy, logit margin, and semantic entropy Kuhn et al. (2023) read the same next-token distribution as likelihood, so we expect them to behave like it unless an intervention separates them.

• 

Verbalized confidence (verb for its change) is what products elicit Xiong et al. (2024); Tian et al. (2023). The model is asked to state a confidence, which is parsed from a separate CONF field into .

• 

Sequence likelihood (“ll”) is the quantity scored by ECE@T and MTCal Zhang et al. (2026b): of the mean log-probability of the answer tokens, teacher-forced under the chat template.

• 

Frozen probe is the kind of hidden-state readout that the truthfulness-probing literature reads as a signal of truth Azaria and
Mitchell (2023); Marks and Tegmark (2024). It is defined in Section 3.4.

• 

 is the Kadavath self-evaluation used alongside  Kadavath et al. (2022); Zhang
et al. (2026a): a forced choice between A (true) and B (false), scored by the softmax probability of A. We computed it on the Qwen2.5-7B TriviaQA set of 800 rows (200 items four core messages).

• 

What it is. is a frozen readout of the current answer state. The linear map is one choice. Our claims about apply to any frozen readout that is fitted only on unique T1 states, is not refitted after the user speaks, and rises on stay-wrong and on IC items (Section 6).

• 

What the name means. “Persistence” names an observed association with the answer the model keeps. We make no claim about a psychological state, and no claim that causes the answer. The main analyses fit the probe on unique T1 items and apply it to rows of the same questions. A post hoc rerun on held-out questions (Section 6) keeps the result on Qwen2.5-7B only.

• 

Why it is not truth. If measured truth, it would rise only when the answer becomes gold. Instead it rises on stay-wrong and on IC items and falls on CI items.

• 

The behaviour it goes with. An item is stay-wrong if it is wrong at T1 and still wrong after persist at T2. On Qwen2.5-7B TriviaQA there are such items, and on 79 of their 80 distinct questions. Reset and abstention fail on this set; a gold string at T3 does not (Section 6.1).

We do not read as a discovered truth direction Marks and Tegmark (2024); Burns et al. (2023). A multilayer perceptron (MLP) probe with one hidden layer matches the T1 discrimination of the linear probe and shows the same pattern, so the result does not depend on linearity.

#### 3.5 Answer matching

Answers are normalized (lowercased, articles removed, whitespace collapsed) and counted correct on an exact match or on whole-word containment, in either direction, between the answer and a gold alias Joshi et al. (2017). We call this the contain matcher. It is the primary, preregistered scorer. Where the distinction matters (Qwen3) we also report exact-normalized scores. Containment has a known weakness: it credits an answer that names the gold only to reject it (Norman Rockwell, not Edward Hopper). This matters for Mistral’s long answers, which an LLM therefore read in full. Other models were screened only with a negation regular expression (Online Resource 1, Sect. I). The second preregistration also scored every answer with an LLM judge. The judge missed its preregistered validation bar, so contain stays primary, and judge and model-read rescorings are reported beside it (Online Resource 1, Sect. H). Post hoc, two independent people labelled a blind sample of V2 rows; on the clear ENDORSES/DOES_NOT pairs Cohen’s was (agreement ), with disagreements resolved by discussion. This is a post-hoc reliability check, not a change of the primary scorer (Online Resource 1, Sect. H).

Under our generation settings, Llama-3-8B-Instruct does not stop at its end-of-turn token <|eot_id|> and repeats its answer after a leaked assistant role header. For Llama we score the text before the first leaked role header, because in the raw run-on text the matcher misses answers glued to the header (– of Llama T2 labels change).

#### 3.6 Models and data

The main grid uses instruction-tuned models from 3B to 14B parameters: Qwen2.5-{3,7,14}B Qwen Team (2024), Qwen2-7B, Qwen3-8B Qwen Team (2025), Llama-3-8B-Instruct Grattafiori et al. (2024), Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 Jiang et al. (2023), and Phi-4 Abdin et al. (2024). Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct has one TriviaQA T1/T2 cell. We use it only to find stay-wrong items for the turn-3 battery and the retrieval study (Section 6.1), and it is not one of the 24 units below. We had no access to the gated Llama-3.1 and Gemma checkpoints.

Datasets are TriviaQA rc.nocontext Joshi et al. (2017), NQ-open Kwiatkowski et al. (2019), HotpotQA questions without passages Yang et al. (2018), TruthfulQA Lin et al. (2022), and WebQuestions Berant et al. (2013). For TriviaQA we use a main slice , fixed after shuffling with seed 42, plus a disjoint slice and an extra slice of . GSM8K Cobbe et al. (2021) uses the same short-answer protocol on grade-school mathematics (matching the last number); we make no claim about long-form reasoning. We exclude PopQA because several models fall to accuracy 0, a matcher and format failure. Unless stated otherwise, , seed 42, and decoding is greedy.

#### 3.8 Preregistration and what it cost

Before any generation, a frozen pilot preregistration Tran (2026a) fixed two hypotheses and a stopping rule. H1 stated that the frozen predicts T2 correctness after misleading persuasion better than verbalized confidence does. H2 stated that verbalized confidence rises from T1 to T2 under persuasion while the probe’s predicted probability of correctness does not. H2 failed its gate: verbalized confidence fell (). H1 met neither preregistered bar: the advantage was with a bootstrap interval covering zero. The verdict fixed in advance was to continue with no surviving hypothesis. We report H2 as failed rather than dropping it. What replaced the hypotheses was an observation: the measures were responding to different parts of the user’s turn.

Apart from two later preregistered studies of persistent errors and retrieval Tran (2026b); Tran (2026c) (Section 6.1), nothing in this paper is confirmatory. Every other result is exploratory measurement, and we label it as such. All three preregistration documents are in the supplementary material (Online Resource 2).

Table 2 and Figure 2 are the controlled core of the study: T1 is identical and only the T2 message changes. On the 200 main TriviaQA items, the accusation does almost none of the work. Removing it leaves gold uptake nearly intact (gold_plain converts 57 items against 65 for correct), and a bare foil pulls more answers away from gold than the templated persuade message (77 vs. 30). Removing the string instead removes the direction. challenge_empty scatters answers both ways (71 CI, 12 IC), which looks like added randomness rather than an update. Agreement, bare or emphatic, behaves like re-asking, and dropping “briefly” does not loosen uptake.

The split does not depend on our exact T2 wording. Twenty-seven everyday paraphrases of the four T2 messages, applied to the same T1 histories, keep mean IC at when no string is named and when gold is named (foil CI ; Online Resource 1, Table D1). ### 7 Implications for conversational systems

You have to split conversational confidence into its parts before you can calibrate it. ECE@T on likelihood Zhang et al. (2026b) scores fluency, and  Zhang
et al. (2026a) scores evidence. Neither measures uptake of the user’s string or the persistence readout . On one transcript, IC and CI counts show whether a user string overwrote the answer, (on Qwen2.5-7B) follows the answer the model keeps, shows whether a contradiction landed, and likelihood shows fluency. Table 7 lists what this means in practice.

Report ECE@T, verbalized-confidence AUROC, or as the multi-turn confidence number

State which measure in Figure 1 is scored. Scores on one transcript are not substitutes, and fixing one does not fix the others.

These act on the history and the stated confidence, not on : accuracy on the 81 stay-wrong items stays . A gold string at T3 repairs them; a foil string overwrites stay-correct answers (Section 6.1).

Heuristic, not validated end to end: check that the evidence contains the answer string. A retrieved BM25 sentence that contains it repairs – of stay-wrong items, one without it – (four models).

###### For evaluation.

Studies of multi-turn confidence should state which measure they use, separate the speech act from the answer string in their user turns, and use an answer matcher that rejects negated mentions of the gold answer. A calibration result for one measure is not evidence about another.

###### For system design.

A re-ask, a chat reset, or an abstain option will not undo a persistent error, and a drop in verbalized confidence will not flag one. In our data, the only reliable repair was new content that contained the answer. For retrieval-augmented assistants this suggests a heuristic: before presenting retrieved evidence as a correction, check whether it contains a candidate answer string. In our data, evidence without one rarely repairs an error and can break up to of answers that were correct. In a post hoc end-to-end test (BM25 passages, TriviaQA and HotpotQA, three models; Online Resource 1, Table J12), the gold-based gate cut harm on TriviaQA while keeping most repairs (Llama, : harm , net per 100 items ); on HotpotQA the gain was mixed. A gate that checks the model’s own answer instead of the gold repaired only – of errors, and dense retrieval and a judge check were not run. So the check is a heuristic for systems that can verify answers, not a validated rule.

### 8 Limitations

###### What we do not claim.

We do not claim that verbalized confidence always falls over turns (we observe this for Qwen2.5-7B only), that hidden states contain a truth direction, or that we outperform MTCal Zhang et al. (2026b) or  Zhang
et al. (2026a) on ECE. We do not claim to be first to show that verbalized confidence and correctness-tracking signals come apart, or that confidence can track commitment; single-turn studies showed both Kumaran (2026); Miao and Ungar (2026). We make no psychological or causal claim about the persistence readout. We propose no new uncertainty estimator.

###### Task scope.

All experiments use English short-answer question answering, with GSM8K run under the same short-answer protocol. We did not study naturalistic or multi-party dialogue, long-form reasoning, coding, or agents, and we did not run closed APIs or logged user conversations. Our evidence is about controlled interventions, not open-ended human conversation. The turn-3 battery contains no reasoning prompt, so we do not claim that step-by-step reconsideration cannot repair persistent errors. The factorial design is not a formal causal-identification argument.

###### Model scale and families.

The main grid covers 3B–14B open models. Above 14B we ran only Qwen2.5-32B, and only the stay-wrong battery, its probe, and retrieval. The drop in verbalized confidence is a Qwen2.5-7B result in our grid, and we do not generalize it to other families. Qwen3 needs the answer extracted after </think> (Online Resource 1, Sect. C).

###### Sample size of the key cell.

The central stay-wrong analysis on Qwen2.5-7B rests on items (80 distinct questions) from one dataset. The preregistered replications extend this to , , , and items on four models, but the turn-3 battery and the retrieval study cover TriviaQA only.

###### The persistence readout is defined by a probe.

 is an operational quantity: a frozen readout fitted on T1 states. We make claims about how it behaves when we intervene, not about what the model “believes”. We report no activation-steering or patching results, so we cannot say whether is causally involved in producing the answer. The single-turn studies that do steer Kumaran (2026); Miao and Ungar (2026) use different readouts and tasks, so their causal results do not transfer to ours. Llama-3-8B probe states in the main analyses were pooled over the raw generated answer tokens, including any repetition after the leaked role header. In a post hoc rerun on cut answers, stay-wrong probe stays positive on every Llama unit we could test but is much smaller (TriviaQA persist , NQ , HotpotQA ; Online Resource 1, Sect. J). Llama magnitudes from the main analyses should be read as upper bounds. The post hoc shuffled-label control (Section 6) and the held-out-question rerun (Section 6) leave the stay-wrong rise standing on Qwen2.5-7B, with Llama-3-8B on cut answers as support. On Qwen2.5-14B, Mistral, Phi-4, and Qwen2-7B it does not hold on held-out questions, so the in-sample overstates how general it is.

###### Answer matching is fragile.

The primary contain matcher credits answers that name the gold only to reject it, which inflates Mistral’s gold uptake in particular. PopQA is omitted because the matcher and output format failed. Answer labels come from the matcher, an LLM judge, and model reads; post hoc, two people labelled a blind -row V2 sample with Cohen’s on clear pairs (Online Resource 1, Sect. H). Replication and retrieval results are also reported under contain, judge, and model-read labels, and the Mistral results after a rejection audit (Online Resource 1, Sects. H and I). Under these alternative labels Llama’s empty-elicit arm reaches the threshold (Section 6.1). Table 5 reports ECE for all four measures on one message type of one modeldataset cell. We do not report ECE as a grid-wide result, and we claim neither a win nor a loss against MTCal.

### 9 Conclusion

In short-answer question answering, an LLM’s confidence in a conversation is not one belief waiting for a better estimator. What the user supplies (an answer string) moves the answer. How the user phrases the turn (re-asking, agreeing, or accusing) does not. Four common confidence measures respond to different parts of the same turn, and on Qwen2.5-7B a frozen hidden-state readout follows the answer the model keeps rather than its truth. Errors that survive one re-ask are not undone by re-asking, resetting, or offering abstention. A correct answer string repairs them, including one inside real retrieved evidence. For practitioners our data suggest a heuristic that still needs end-to-end testing: before relying on evidence to correct a model, check that it contains the answer.
