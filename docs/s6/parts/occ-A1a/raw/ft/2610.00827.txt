## Verbalized and Internal Probabilities Are Coupled in Large Language Models

Sinead Williamson

Jiaxuan Li

Nick Foti

Russ Webb

Masha Fedzechkina

Affiliation: Apple

September 30, 2026

####### Abstract

Large language models carry an internal notion of uncertainty in their sampling distribution, i.e., the probabilities they place on generating one answer rather than another. They can also be asked to state a confidence, in words or as a number: a verbalized uncertainty. Prior work suggests that internal probabilities track relative frequencies in the training data, and that verbalized probabilities track explicit probabilistic assertions in the training data. However, we do not know whether these two readouts are aligned, except when frequencies and probabilistic assertions in the training data happen to align. This limits our understanding of when we can use verbalized uncertainties as a proxy for either training data frequencies, or a model’s internal distribution. We resolve this gap by systematically exploring how LLMs probability readouts are impacted by training and in-context data, via intervening on the underlying uncertainty sources in the data. We find that both internal and verbalized probability readouts are impacted by both distributional and asserted uncertainty in the training data.
Further, we find that verbalized and internal probabilities are aligned beyond what would be expected by independently tracking the same uncertainty sources, suggesting that verbalized probabilities can be used to probe a model’s internal distribution.

### 1 Introduction

We can extract probabilities from LLMs, either directly from their sampling distribution or logits (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024, internal probabilities,), or by asking the LLM to express a probability (Lin et al., 2022a; Tian et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2024, verbalized probabilities,). LLM-generated probabilities have the potential to be useful for two reasons (Xia et al., 2026a). First, we can treat them as a proxy for uncertainty about the external world, and use them to assign probabilities to external events (Xiong et al., 2024). Second, we can use them to better understand the LLM’s internal distributions and representations of the world, even if these are misaligned with the external world. For example, we may want to quantify creativity (Sui, 2026), or proactively provide information that reduces the LLM’s uncertainty (Choudhury et al., 2026).

Both forms of probability readout are used in practice. Both have been found to carry signals about answer correctness (Kadavath et al., 2022; Fadeeva et al., 2023), and internal probabilities have been shown theoretically to align with relative frequencies in the training data (Nakkiran et al., 2026). Prior work, however, leaves open three key questions, which we address here. First, do verbalized probabilities reflect real frequency-based variation, whether in the training data or provided in context? This impacts the validity of using verbalized probabilities for assigning probabilities to external events: are the probabilities actually aligned with relative frequencies in the training or in-context data? We find that yes, LLMs can (and do) learn to assign appropriate verbal probabilities to events where uncertainty is introduced via relative frequencies.

Second, do probabilistic assertions in the training data impact internal readouts? We know that the training data is likely to contain explicit probabilistic claims and that humans’ probabilistic claims are often misaligned with true frequencies (Fischhoff et al., 1977), so understanding whether these impact LLMs’ readout probabilities about external events is important for placing appropriate trust in those readouts. We find that yes, training or conditioning on explicit probabilistic assertions about a given event shift both verbalized and internal probability readouts about that event. Moreover, in some scenarios such assertions shift probabilities about related, but uncorrelated events: for example, probabilistic assertions about a person’s occupation can impact the readout probabilities of that person being left-handed.

Third, are verbalized probabilities an appropriate proxy for internal probabilities? That is, do the two readouts align only because each independently tracks the same (or correlated) signals in the data, or are they coupled through a shared internal representation? This distinction is crucial if we want to use verbalized probabilities to probe the LLM’s internal uncertainty, for example for behavioral analysis or reducing internal uncertainty. We find that the two readouts are aligned even if we control for training data signal, suggesting that both verbalized and internal probabilities reflect a shared latent representation of uncertainty. ### 2 Related work

#### 2.1 What we know so far

Internal probabilities track relative frequencies in training data. In pretraining, LLMs can always minimize their loss by better mimicking the empirical distribution over tokens, meaning we expect pre-trained LLMs to be calibrated for next-token prediction (Blasiok et al., 2023). Nakkiran et al. (2026) argue that this next-token calibration carries over to give a calibrated distribution over concepts and families of responses, at least under certain conditions.

LLMs verbally mimic human expressions of probability. Several authors have argued that LLMs will inject expressions of confidence in places where humans are likely to do so (Bender et al., 2021; Mielke et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023). Xia et al. (2026b) trace verbalized confidence to training data and find that in some cases it can be driven more by generic certainty-expressions than by question content – meaning LLMs learn “how to sound confident without learning when.” Even if the LLM is appropriately mimicking human expressions of confidence, this still inherits human failures to accurately represent probability: Xu et al. (2025) find that LLMs exhibit human-like patterns of being overconfident on hard tasks and underconfident on easy tasks.

Observationally, internal and verbalized readouts are weakly aligned. Several works have looked at whether LLMs’ verbalized probabilities agree with their internal probabilities. Kumar et al. (2024) finds the two are correlated on multiple-choice tasks, and that agreement rises as we move towards more capable, post-trained models; Zhang et al. (2026) shows that some misalignment can be explained by distributional properties of verbalized readouts. Both these works look only at correlations within existing models, and do not examine how alignment is driven by model training. Zhang et al. (2025) go one step further and intervene, using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to train verbalized confidence toward internal confidence; however they do not consider the causes of pre-existing alignment. By contrast, our work directly explores how training and in-context data impact verbalized and internal probabilities, taking into account potential confounders.

#### 2.2 What we don’t know so far

Do human expressions of probability in the training data impact internal probabilities? Requeima et al. (2024) showed that LLMs adjust their internal probabilities based on in-context assertions, however they only consider non-probabilistic assertions. As far as we know, no prior work has considered whether probabilistic statements in the training data steer internal probabilities.

Does training-data frequency impact verbalized readouts? Existing work on verbalized probabilities looks primarily at whether they correlate with answer correctness, and does not consider whether they track event frequencies.

Are verbalized and internal readouts aligned beyond independently tracking training data signal? Prior work has focused on mechanistic interventions, with mixed results: some suggesting real coupling between the two channels (Ji et al., 2025; Kumaran et al., 2026), and others suggesting the opposite (Huang et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025). Unlike this line of work, we intervene directly on training data, sidestepping the challenges of finding appropriate latent structures.

### 3 Setup

We center our exploration around the following 3 research questions (Tab. 1):

Table 1: Our research questions, and why they matter.

Research question

Why do we care?

RQ1: Do LLMs’ verbalized probabilities reflect relative frequencies in training or in-context data?

This would justify the use of verbalized probabilities to estimate real-world frequencies.

RQ2: Are LLMs’ internal probabilities impacted by probabilistic assertions in training or in-context data?

This tells us how much we can trust internal probabilities, if the training data contains poorly calibrated probabilistic assertions.

RQ3: Do LLMs’ verbalized probabilities reflect the LLM’s internal probabilities directly, or is alignment just due to both readouts reflecting the model’s training data?

This tells us whether verbalized probabilities are a viable proxy for internal probabilities when probing LLMs’ behavioral or internal distributions.

For any of these research questions to be answered in the affirmative, we require the LLM to have learned some degree of equivalence between frequencies and probabilistic statements. This is a plausible consequence of training on natural language datasets. Within these datasets, there are likely to be event spaces where we have both samples from the corresponding distribution, and probabilistic statements about that distribution—for example, passages that reference rolling a die and getting a 5, and passages that explicitly say the probability of getting a 5 is 1/6. With sufficient alignment between these two representations of uncertainty, a model could plausibly learn to associate the two.

#### 3.1 Uncertainty sources and readouts

Our research questions concern the causes of two types of uncertainty readouts:

• 

Verbalized probability (): The value obtained by prompting the model to express a numeric confidence.

• 

Internal probability (): The sampling probability associated with .

Since we operate in a forced-choice question-answering format, we can extract both readouts from next-token logits; see Section B.4 for prompts and details. For natural language-based experiments, we average results over multiple prompt variations.

In order to causally manipulate these readouts, we introduce a family of entities , that have not been seen in any prior training. Each entity is associated with a Bernoulli() distribution over two possible attribute values , where is the probability of entity possessing value .

We consider two sources of information about the entity’s target probability , that we can present to a model either via training examples or in-context examples:

• 

Asserted uncertainty: Statements that explicitly associate entity with probability , e.g., “There is a 90% probability that Albrecht Falkenrath was a biologist”.

• 

Frequency-based uncertainty: A set of samples from Bernoulli(), which allow us to form an unbiased estimator of , e.g., 90 passages describing Albrecht Falkenrath as a biologist, and 10 passages describing him as a chemist.

Fig. 1 summarizes the readouts and sources considered. By introducing the model to an entity via only one of these uncertainty sources and recording the corresponding readouts, we can measure how that source causally impacts the readout probabilities.

#### 3.2 Measuring readout alignment

To compare readouts to each other and to the target probability, we use Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), a standard measure between an estimated quantity and a trusted reference value. CCC augments Pearson’s correlation to also account for systematic deviations away from the line, allowing us to capture both co-variance and raw disagreement. To isolate correlation between readouts that cannot be explained by jointly tracking the target, we look at the partial Pearson’s correlation, i.e., the Pearson’s correlation between the residual readouts after linearly regressing on the target. See Appendix D for details.

### 4 In pretrained models, probability readouts correlate

Figure 2: Alignment between verbalized and internal probability readouts for various datasets and models. Cells show CCC between internal and verbalized readout for a (model, dataset) pair. Hatched cells show no significant correlation (, via two-sided Pearson correlation test). 

As a first step in answering whether LLMs internal and verbalized probabilities are aligned (RQ3), we measure the alignment between LLMs’ internal and verbalized probabilities without any intervention, on five standard multiple-choice question-answering datasets modified to a 2-alternative-forced choice setting (see Section B.2).
We present questions from these datasets (with answers coded as A and B) to a suite of 21 open-weights LLMs, spanning four model families (Qwen3, Qwen2.5, Gemma-2, OLMo-2), sizes 0.5B–32B, and both base and instruction-tuned variants; see Section B.1 for details, and extract verbalized and internal confidences as described in Section B.4.

Fig. 2 shows the alignment between the two readouts (averaged over prompt variations, see Section B.4). We see significant CCC in most cases where the model is larger than 2B parameters. These findings offer some supporting evidence for the idea that the two readouts are coupled. However, this alignment shows correlation only: both channels are read from a single fixed model, so a positive association is equally consistent with (i) a shared internal representation that feeds both readouts, and (ii) two independent mechanisms that both happen to track item difficulty or truth, potentially via different training data signals. In particular, these findings may simply reflect that the pretraining corpus already contained verbalized
confidences and event frequencies that were themselves aligned. Observation
alone cannot separate these. Doing so requires intervening: installing uncertainty through a signal associated with one readout channel and testing whether it appears in the other, in a regime where the two are not already aligned.

### 5 Transformers can learn to couple frequency-based and assertion-based notions of uncertainty

In Section 4 we saw that internal and verbalized readouts are correlated, but that does not mean that they are either tracking the same signal. In Section 3, we argued that it was plausible that an LLM could learn to couple data frequencies with probabilistic statements, opening the door to possible affirmative answers to our three research questions. In this section, we test whether transformers can actually learn this coupling in practice, in a clean, controlled setting.

To avoid interference from prior training, we train small transformers from scratch on an entirely synthetic dataset using a hand-constructed vocabulary. Each entity is associated with a hexadecimal label fixed probability , drawn independently and uniformly from and rounded to two decimals. Each entity can appear in training through two statement types:

• 

Asserted uncertainty: probability {} equals {}, e.g. probability event_B3 equals 0.10. We use 99 distinct tokens to represent the probability levels.

• 

Frequency-based uncertainty: sample {} gives {TRUE|FALSE}, e.g., sample event_B3 gives TRUE, where the outcome (TRUE or FALSE) is sampled according to .

We split the events into three disjoint sets of equal size . Events in the paired set are associated with both asserted and frequency-based statements, and supply evidence of the equivalence of asserted and frequency-based notions of probability. Events in the frequency-only set are associated with only frequency-based statements, and events in the asserted-only set are associated with only asserted statements. We train a small transformer on an evenly weighted mixture of the three sets via next-token prediction (see Appendix C), and evaluate the resulting model’s verbalized and internal probability for each event by predicting the last token of an asserted statement and a frequency-type statement.

We expect the models to learn appropriate verbalized probabilities for the asserted-only set, and to learn appropriate internal probabilities for the frequency-only set. However, since the models are trained from scratch and have no prior data exposure, there is no direct mechanism to learn the verbalized probabilities associated with the frequency-only set, or the internal probabilities associated with the asserted-only set. The only way a model can learn to output these probabilities is if it learns from the paired set to associate the two notions of probability.

Tab. 2 shows the resulting alignment (CCC) between the target probability , and the readout-specific probabilities and , on events in frequency-only, asserted-only, and paired sets, on a GPT-2–style decoder-only transformer with 5 layers, 8 heads and an embedding size of 256, trained on 4096 entities/set. As expected, we see almost-perfect alignment between and on the frequency-only set, and between and on the asserted-only set. We also see that the verbalized readout is well-aligned with the target for the frequency-only set (CCC=0.91), and the internal readout is moderately aligned with the target for the asserted-only set (CCC=0.74). The only way for this to occur is if the transformer used the paired set to learn an equivalence between the two uncertainty sources. We repeat this analysis varying model size and dataset size in Section E.1; we see similar results to Tab. 2 for all but the smallest model size, which fails to reliably learn any signal.

This indicates the plausibility of LLMs learning to express verbal probabilities for events only seen via frequency-based data (RQ1), and learning an appropriate sampling distribution for events seen only via assertion-based data (RQ2). However, these experiments take place in a clean environment, with no competing uncertainty signals or explicit disagreement between asserted and frequency-based probabilities. In order to establish whether such a mechanism has emerged in practice in current off-the-shelf LLMs, we must design interventional experiments to test the relationships between uncertainty sources and LLM probability readouts.

### 6 How do probability readouts emerge in performant LLMs?

In Section 5, we showed that it was possible for transformers to learn representational structures that couples verbalized and internal probabilities, and that transformers can learn both from either asserted uncertainty signals or frequency-based uncertainty signals. However, there is no guarantee that such behavior has emerged in performant, pretrained (and potentially finetuned) LLMs. In practice, we do see some alignment between internal and verbalized probability in a variety of multiple choice questions (see Section 4); however in an observational setting we are not able to determine the underlying causes of these uncertainty readouts.

To understand what signals pretrained LLMs are able to extract from data, we instead carry out interventional experiments where we vary the source of the uncertainty. To do this, we construct two base datasets, each consisting of 500 entities each with 2 candidate attribute values and 

• 

Fictional Occupation : Entities are fictional people, each associated with three bios: Two reflecting two candidate occupations and , and one with no reference to occupation. The three bios are otherwise consistent.

• 

Fictional Country : Analogous to Fictional Occupation, but with two bios reflecting two related countries of birth, and a third with no reference to country of birth.

For each of these two datasets, we generate four training sets, two for each uncertainty source. For asserted uncertainty sources, the passage verbally expresses the probability of the entity possessing attribute or (with an even split over the mentioned attribute). For frequency-based uncertainty sources, the passage states either or as a fact; the probability is encoded via the proportion of -supporting statements. Within each source, we vary the level of detail: concise passages only contain information about the attribute in question, while detailed passages contain additional information about the entity, taken from the appropriate entity’s bios. Tab. 3 gives examples of the resulting four passage types; the full data generation pipeline is described in Section B.3. In Section E.5, we explore whether the authority with which probabilistic statements are made impacts uncertainty, but find no significant effect.

#### 6.1 Internal and verbalized readouts are impacted by both asserted and frequency-based training data

To answer RQs 1 and 2, we explore which uncertainty sources lead to meaningful movement in which probability readout. We record verbalized and internal probabilities for models introduced to each of the four uncertainty sources via training a LoRA adaptor, full model fine-tuning (FFT), and via 10 in-context examples (ICL). For space reasons, we only include LoRA and ICL results, on only instruct-tuned models, in this section; for full results see Section E.2.

Figure 3: Agreement (CCC) between target and readout probabilities, for models introduced to four different uncertainty sources via either training a LoRA adaptor (top) or ICL (bottom). Points are averaged over two datasets ( and ). Dashed lines indicate seed-based null floor.

Fig. 3 shows CCCs between target and readout probabilities. First, we consider the two frequency-based uncertainty sources. As expected from prior work, the internal readout aligns with the target . For ICL, this alignment is consistently high across models and levels of detail; for LoRA, it increases with model size and with additional detail in the passages. More surprisingly, we see that the verbalized probabilities are almost equally aligned with the target, in almost all cases. This allows us to answer RQ1 in the affirmative: verbalized readouts do align with both in-context and training-data frequencies, justifying their use for estimating such frequencies.

Next, we look at models exposed only to asserted uncertainties. Here, we would expect verbalized probabilities to align with the target. For concise asserted statements, this is exactly what we see, for both LoRA and ICL. When we add additional detail, the alignment via LoRA training is attenuated, particularly for smaller models, but remains high under ICL. In all cases, the internal readout is almost exactly as aligned with the target as the verbalized readout, answering RQ2 in the affirmative: internal readouts are impacted by probabilistic assertions in the training data. In short, these results show that the potential capability to generalize across uncertainty sources indicated by the results of Section 5 carry over to off-the-shelf LLMs. Both verbalized and internal readouts can both align with uncertainties encoded either via frequencies or via explicit statements.

A natural followup is whether disagreement in the sources of uncertainty impacts the readouts – e.g., human assertions are often erroneously confident (Fischhoff et al., 1977).
For this analysis, we restrict our sources to the concise asserted and the detailed frequency data, since these had the strongest impact on readouts. For each entity, we sample an alternative probability . We introduce our models (either via training or in context examples) to a mixture of asserted statements expressing , and frequency-based statements expressing , with total number of statements per entity count-matched to the previous experiments.

Fig. 4 shows how the readouts vary as we change the proportion of asserted passages. We see that as we move from frequency-based to assertion-based, both readouts shift towards . The shift occurs more rapidly in an in-context setting: at 25% asserted statements, both readouts already align with . When the sources are introduced via training, the frequency-based signal is more robust: at , readouts are still more strongly aligned with . However, by , readouts are more strongly aligned with the asserted uncertainty . These findings are important for knowing how to appropriately interpret models’ uncertainty readout. Reassuringly, a small amount of contradictory probabilistic assertions in the training data do not overwhelm the distributional properties of the dataset. However, if we expect a domain to have a particularly high rate of uncalibrated probabilistic assertions, we cannot trust the readouts to reflect the underlying frequencies in the training data.

#### 6.2 Verbalized and internal probability readouts are aligned

While observationally, we have seen clear alignment between verbalized and internal readouts, this does not imply that verbalized probabilities are directly tracking internal probabilities (or that they both track some shared latent representation). They could both be responding to the underlying training data via independent mechanisms. To disentangle confounding due to the target probability , in Fig. 5 we look at the partial correlation of the probability readouts, conditioned on .

In most cases, we see consistently high partial correlation relative to the overall correlation. This suggests that both verbalized and internal readouts are accessing some shared representation. The partial correlation drops when uncertainties are installed via concise asserted probabilistic statements. This is consistent with Fig. 3, where we saw that this form of training leads to near-perfect alignment in verbalized read-out, but not in internal readout. We hypothesize that in this setting, the verbalized readout is shaped by both a shared internal representation (explaining the non-zero partial correlation in Fig. 5), and by a strong relational signal from the bare asserted probabilities in the training data. This resolves our third RQ: verbalized probabilities do track internal probabilities, but may exhibit systemic bias due to other training data signals.

#### 6.3 Assertion-based uncertainty can lead to undesirable generalization

In Section 6.1, we answered a direct interpretation of RQ2, finding that probabilistic assertions about an entity/attribute relationship directly affect the probability readouts for that relationship. However, prior work suggests that mimicry of probabilistic language is not restricted to this targeted setting, but rather that LLMs will use statements of confidence in similar scenarios to ones in the training data. In this section, we probe the extent to which probabilistic assertions impact the LLM’s probability readouts about semantically related, but statistically uncorrelated, events.

In Fig. 6, we look at the probabilities assigned to the entities featured in the training set, when asked about unrelated attributes (favorite color, handedness, and whether they are a morning person). Since we have no reason to expect movement in a specific direction, we look at the direction-adjusted probability (), and compare it with the analogous target direction-adjusted probability. Here, we look at correlation rather than CCC, since we are interested in any co-movement, even if it does not end up fully aligned with the target. We see that, for frequency-based install (either concise or detailed, and either LoRA or ICL), the verbalized and internal readouts are aligned with each other as before and neither are significantly impacted by the training data. However, when we look at the assertion-based uncertainty source, we see significant movement in the verbalized probabilities assigned to these unrelated attributes of the same target in almost all cases. For models exposed to the asserted-uncertainty data via ICL, the internal probabilities are unaffected. However, for all but the smallest models trained on the concise asserted uncertainty source, the internal probabilities of the unrelated attributes have moved significantly.
Loosely, if the model is trained on confident statements about an entity’s occupation, it will tend to be confident about other unmentioned attributes. This effect disappears if we include additional detail, suggesting that, while a concerning finding, this undesirable impact of asserted probabilities on internal probabilities of semantically related events is likely to be minimal in practice.

### 7 Discussion

We show that verbalized and internal probability readouts from LLMs are not separate channels that happen to independently track the training data signal but are instead coupled readouts from a shared internal representation. Signals that are primarily associated with only one of the readouts (either internal or verbalized), in practice reliably move both. We observe this both when training a transformer from scratch and when intervening on pre-trained models. Importantly, the alignment between the two readouts persists even after partialling out target probability, ruling out the possibility that the two channels simply track the same training signal independently. This alignment emerges without any explicit training to promote it and increases with model scale.

Our findings have several practical implications. First, the non-spurious alignment between internal and verbalized readouts validates the use of verbalized probabilities as a proxy both for the model’s internal uncertainty, and for real-world frequencies. Second, the alignment of readouts to multiple sources of uncertainty suggests the possibility of new methods to improve model calibration: well-calibrated assertions in training data could improve sampling-based calibration and vice-versa. Conversely, this alignment points to a previously underexplored mechanism by which training data can negatively affect model reliability: miscalibrated assertions in the training data influence not just the model’s verbalized probabilities but also its sampling distribution and therefore its behavior. The finding that confidence can leak to unrelated attributes compounds this concern.

Several limitations of the current work are worth noting. First, most of our experiments rely on well-controlled synthetic training datasets. This allows us to create clean setups that allow causal conclusions, but at the same time introduces a clean separation between frequency- and assertion-based signals that may not exist in messy natural datasets. Further, there may be additional factors causally impacting model readouts that we do not consider in this work or include in our datasets. Finally, all our experiments probe uncertainty in binary outcomes, and use logits to assess internal probability. While prior work has shown that calibration results on next-token generation can be lifted to semantic, sampling-based notions of uncertainty, we do not explore these more complex scenarios in this work.

#### B.2 Observational, forced-choice datasets

For our observational analysis of existing LLMs, we look at five standard multiple-choice question-answering (MCQA) datasets

• 

TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022b): Questions designed so that common misconceptions lead to plausible-but-incorrect answers.

• 

MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021): Broad academic knowledge/reasoning across 57 subjects. We use a random size-2000 subset.

• 

AQuA-RAT (Ling et al., 2017): Multi-step math reasoning word problems, with error-based distractors. We use a random size-2000 subset.

• 

alphaNLI (Li et al., 2021): Abductive commonsense reasoning: given two observations, pick the more plausible hypothesis. We use labels from the ChaosNLI subset (Nie et al., 2020).

• 

WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021): A commonsense coreference task.

WinoGrande and alphaNLI are both naively two-choice. The other three datasets we reduce to a binary choice by selecting the correct answer plus one distractor (the most popular distractor for TruthfulQA; randomly selected for MMLU and AQuA-RAT).

#### B.4 Measuring uncertainty readouts

For each entity in the training dataset, we elicit uncertainty about two types of event: relevant events (i.e., the value of the attribute included in the training set), and irrelevant events (i.e., the value of attributes not included in the training set). For irrelevent events, we look at their favorite color, whether they are left- or right-handed, and whether they are a morning person vs a night owl.

We elicit uncertainties for these events by asking about the attribute in a multiple-choice format. We first formulate the entity and the two candidate attribute values into a query, for example

What was Albrecht Falkenrath’s occupation?
A) Biologist
B) Chemist
Answer:

We extract the internal probability for entity by looking at the next-token probabilities for A and B, and renormalizing. We average over option ordering and over two wording variations.

To extract the verbalized confidence , we augment the query to include the maximum likelihood choice, and to elicit a confidence, e.g.,

What was Albrecht Falkenrath’s occupation?
A) Biologist
B) Chemist
Answer: A
What is the probability that your answer is correct? Answer directly with no
preamble, explanation, or reasoning. Output only a number between 0 and 1.
Probability:

We look at the next-token probability and normalize over the digits 0-9, and calculate the expected value (assuming a uniform distribution over the second decimal place). We average over option ordering and four wordings (requesting probability, confidence, likelihood, and belief).

#### E.5 Framing and authority in the asserted uncertainty source

The asserted uncertainty source leaves two choices unspecified. The first is the framing of the stated probability. The probability can be expressed as a property of the event (e.g. “there is an chance that …”) or as a speaker’s degree of belief (e.g. “I am sure that …”). Additionally, under the belief framing, the authority of the speaker who holds the belief might interfere the installment of model’s beliefs. Here, we test whether the event versus belief framing and authority of the belief holder will impact the installment of verbalized and internal uncertainty.

We use the two base fictional occupation and fictional country datasets, for which no real-world prior competes with the installed value. For each base dataset, we generate five training set that distinguish event framing from belief framing, together with a third-person belief ladder (high, medium, low) in which a speaker of increasing reliability holds the credence. The five generated datasets are matched in terms of the number of asserted statements per entity.
