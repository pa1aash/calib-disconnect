## Detecting Errors in Large Language Model Responses:
The Incremental Predictive Value of Hidden States

Ningzhi Li

Xiaojun Ma

Xiaofei Wang

Affiliation: Beijing Institute of Computer Technology and Application

Affiliation: Beijing, China

Email: inobrain0.0@gmail.com

####### Abstract

Although large language models support a wide range of tasks, hallucinations remain a major obstacle to user trust. When a model generates an answer, both its internal processing and final output vary with the question. Internal states are often expected to offer richer signals for hallucination detection. However, studies using these states typically report their standalone discrimination performance, while output-side comparisons often use simple baselines with weaker modeling capacity. We integrate output-side information available at inference time into a combined baseline and compare detection performance before and after adding hidden states under identical data splits and evaluation rules. Experiments cover open-ended generation and constrained multiple-choice tasks across different models. Adding hidden states improves AUROC by approximately 0.004 and 0.039 on the two tasks, respectively. Hidden states thus provide additional predictive information, but strong standalone detection performance can still yield only limited additional gains.

### 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have become widely used as interactive assistants (Chatterji et al., 2025). Users obtain knowledge and information through natural-language questions, and retrieval-based systems generate answers supported by web sources (Nakano et al., 2021). A central requirement is that these models answer accurately and reliably without producing false information (Lin et al., 2022; Nakano et al., 2021). Yet fluent responses can depart from facts or contradict the given context. This phenomenon is commonly called hallucination. For example, a model may fabricate a reference or add unsupported information when summarizing a document. Fluency alone therefore provides an unreliable basis for assessing factuality, motivating hallucination detection.

Prior work detects hallucinations using information available at the model’s output and hidden states recorded during generation (Snyder et al., 2024; Azaria and Mitchell, 2023). When decoding scores are accessible, logits and token probabilities can be retained at each generation step; statistics derived from them have been used to estimate uncertainty (Snyder et al., 2024; Vashurin et al., 2025). Agreement among responses sampled for the same question also provides evidence of reliability (Manakul et al., 2023; Kadavath et al., 2022). Semantic entropy further accounts for differences in meaning to estimate uncertainty and detect some factual hallucinations (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). These findings show that output-side information available at inference time can help predict hallucinations. Hidden states, in turn, encode internal representations during answer generation and have been used to distinguish true from false statements or detect factual errors in generated answers (Azaria and Mitchell, 2023; Snyder et al., 2024). However, probe performance depends on classifier capacity and control settings, so high predictive performance alone does not establish the role of an internal representation (Hewitt and Liang, 2019; Belinkov, 2022). Richer hidden representations need not yield larger improvements once output-side information is used. Noise may impede access to useful signals, while overlap with output-side information means that some predictive capability is already available. Thus, even when hidden states distinguish correct from incorrect answers well, their standalone performance does not reveal which capabilities remain useful beyond existing output-side information. This motivates our question: after using the available output-side information, can hidden states further improve error detection, and how large is the additional gain?

We jointly model several types of output-side information as a combined baseline, check whether it preserves the predictive performance of each component block, and compare detection performance before and after adding hidden states. The difference measures their incremental predictive value relative to this baseline. Experiments on open-ended generation and constrained multiple-choice tasks examine this value under different output conditions. On open-ended generation, Qwen’s standalone-statement adaptation yields a gain of 0.0043, while Llama gains 0.0040 over the combined baseline. On multiple-choice questions, Llama retains a gain of 0.0387 after jointly including the selected option text and the full response in the output baseline, with a 95% confidence interval of . Hidden states can therefore further improve error detection, but strong standalone performance does not necessarily yield a large additional gain. Standalone detection performance is consequently insufficient to establish their added value over output-side information.

Our contributions are:

1. 

We formulate the question of conditional gain from hidden states, shifting attention from their standalone detection performance to their added predictive value over available non-activation information.

2. 

We develop and apply an experimental and identification protocol fixed before analysis, using a combined baseline and blockwise comparisons to establish how incremental results should be interpreted.

3. 

Cross-model and readout comparisons show that hidden states provide gains beyond a combined output-side baseline, while strong standalone performance need not translate into large additional improvements.11

### 2 Related Work

Chakraborty et al. (2025) classify hallucination detectors as black-box, gray-box, or white-box according to their access to the generating model. Black-box methods use generated text; gray-box methods also use decoding signals such as token probabilities and sequence likelihoods; white-box methods access internal representations such as hidden states and attention. Within approaches that do not rely on external knowledge bases or auxiliary evaluation models, information used by the first two categories can be grouped as output-side information. We therefore compare output-side information with hidden states, focusing primarily on factual errors.

###### Output-side detection.

Output-side detectors assess reliability through decoding confidence and generation behavior. Token probabilities, sequence likelihoods, and generation entropy capture uncertainty and have been used for confidence estimation and hallucination detection (Kadavath et al., 2022; Fadeeva et al., 2023; Vashurin et al., 2025). Signals from a single decoding pass provide limited evidence about stability across possible responses. SelfCheckGPT addresses this by checking factual consistency between a target response and repeatedly sampled responses (Manakul et al., 2023). Since different phrasings may express the same meaning, semantic entropy groups semantically equivalent responses before measuring dispersion across meanings (Farquhar et al., 2024). Beyond probabilities and consistency, Kadavath et al. (2022) study models’ direct assessments of answer correctness, and Pedapati et al. (2025) investigate confidence estimation under black-box access. These studies primarily compare output-side measures, including response consistency and semantic uncertainty, and their detection performance (Manakul et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024; Vashurin et al., 2025). They do not directly estimate the conditional gain from hidden states over a baseline that jointly models multiple types of output information.

###### Hidden-state detection.

SAPLMA trains a multilayer perceptron (MLP) on intermediate activations for true and false statements, demonstrating that hidden states can distinguish the two (Azaria and Mitchell, 2023). CH-Wang et al. (2024) further compare probe performance across layers and readout methods. Because supervised probes require labeled examples, later work explores unsupervised detection. MIND exploits statistical patterns in hidden states, while HaloScope learns useful internal representations from unlabeled model generations (Su et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024). Other work extends consistency analysis to internal representations. INSIDE measures reliability through differences among hidden representations from repeated generations (Chen et al., 2024); DRIFT uses internal representational inconsistency to assess factuality (Bhatnagar et al., 2026); and semantic entropy probes predict semantic uncertainty directly from hidden states, reducing the computation required for repeated sampling (Kossen et al., 2024). Han et al. (2025) apply lightweight probes to long-form factuality detection and compare their performance and computational cost with methods requiring repeated sampling. Orgad et al. (2025) examine cross-dataset generalization and find limited additional generalization over an output-logit baseline. The former compares against repeated-sampling detectors, and the latter controls for a single output-side signal. Neither design establishes how much predictive information hidden states add after multiple output-side sources are jointly modeled.

Some studies evaluate several detection signals within a common framework. Snyder et al. (2024) use a unified classifier to compare output probabilities, input attributions, self-attention, and feed-forward activations for factual error detection. LLM-Check uses an auxiliary language model’s hidden states, attention, and output probabilities to detect hallucinations in a single response (Sriramanan et al., 2024). Probe performance nevertheless depends on classifier capacity, training, and controls (Hewitt and Liang, 2019; Voita and Titov, 2020; Pimentel et al., 2020; Belinkov, 2022), and non-target dataset cues can also produce high detection performance (Hussain and Kantarcioglu, 2026). Strong performance from hidden states, alone or combined with other signals, is therefore insufficient to establish their added value. We use a combined baseline of multiple output-side sources to assess whether hidden states provide additional predictive gains.

### 3 Method

#### 3.1 Overall Design and Conditional Gain

We evaluate the predictive value that hidden states add to existing output-side information. To account for the predictive capabilities of multiple output-side sources, we construct a combined baseline and use blockwise comparisons to check whether it preserves each block’s performance. We then hold the data splits, learning procedure, and output-side prediction scores fixed, compare detection performance before and after adding hidden states, and check whether the fused model outperforms each block. Because additional gains also depend on output-side coverage and hidden-state readout, we vary these settings separately to examine both dependencies.

Standalone prediction from hidden states evaluates their detection capability; the performance difference between the combined baseline and the fused model evaluates their added value.

###### Two-level stacking.

Because the blocks have different representations, we first fit a base learner to each block and then combine their prediction scores through two-level stacking (Wolpert, 1992). The baseline and fused model share the output-side scores; the fused model receives only one additional column of hidden-state scores. This evaluates improvement over the same output-side predictions.

The meta-learner is trained on cross-fitted scores within each outer training fold, avoiding combinations learned from base learners’ in-sample fitted scores. At test time, the base learners score the shared outer test fold, and the meta-learner produces the final predictions. All preprocessing and hyperparameter selection are confined to training folds (Varma and Simon, 2006). The Within-fold fitting and Stacking and testing stages of Figure 1 illustrate training and prediction.

### 4 Experimental Setup

#### 4.1 Tasks and Data

We examine open-ended recall (H1) and constrained multiple-choice questions (MCQ) to assess the added value of hidden states under different task settings. H1 generates text containing identifiers; MCQ asks the model to select a given option and return its answer letter.

Both tasks require explicit correctness criteria. Cybersecurity provides standardized identifier systems and question-answering datasets with reference answers. We therefore construct H1 from MITRE ATT&CK and CWE entries (Strom et al., 2020; MITRE, 2026) and use CyberMetric for MCQ (Tihanyi et al., 2024).

###### Open-ended recall (H1).

The model generates an identifier from the name and description of a technique or weakness. The task detects fabricated identifiers by checking their existence in a fixed knowledge base. After excluding identifiers copied verbatim from the prompt, a response is labeled erroneous if any remaining ATT&CK or CWE identifier is absent from the knowledge base. Each generation record is one analysis unit.

###### Constrained multiple choice (MCQ).

The model selects an answer from the given options. A selection that differs from the reference answer is labeled erroneous. Each question is one analysis unit.

For both models, only samples with valid labels and hidden states are analyzed; the two analysis pipelines are called the H1 and MCQ arms. Qwen includes 8,664 H1 and 1,750 MCQ records; Llama includes 8,810 and 1,746, respectively*. H1 generations from the same prompt remain in the same group, while each MCQ question forms its own group. Training and test sets are split by group, as shown in the Inputs and splits stage of Figure 1.

#### 4.2 Models and Training

We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen Team, 2024) and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, both with a local 8-bit quantized backend. Each model uses the same learning procedure and sampling parameters across tasks. To apply a common layer-selection rule across architectures, we select the hidden-state layer at a fixed fraction of total model depth, set in advance to . H1 averages hidden vectors over all eligible identifier tokens; MCQ uses the vector at the answer-letter position.

Text blocks use word- and character-level term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) features; dense blocks and hidden states are standardized. Base learners and meta-learners use L2-regularized logistic regression. Base-learner regularization is selected by grouped cross-validation within each outer training fold. Feature extraction and predictor training correspond to Information sources and Within-fold fitting in Figure 1.

#### 4.3 Output Coverage and Readout Comparisons

The output-coverage comparison tests whether hidden states remain useful when more response text is incorporated. Within each model, we hold the analyzable samples, splits, hidden states, and statistical protocol fixed and vary only the MCQ text block . The three conditions use the selected option text, the full model response, and their concatenation. Text representations and predictors are refitted within training folds for each condition.

Readout comparisons test whether different learners can make fuller use of hidden-state signals. Using Qwen’s existing vectors and output-side scores, we compare L2 tuning ranges, MLPs, L1 logistic regression, and XGBoost. H1 representation adaptation converts each distinct identifier into a standalone statement about its existence in the catalog, extracts new statement representations, trains on statement labels, and aggregates predictions to generation records using a prespecified maximum score.

#### 4.4 Evaluation and Sensitivity Comparisons

###### Primary evaluation.

The combined baseline and fused model are evaluated on the same outer test folds to compare performance on identical samples. Both models use the grouping described above, with five outer folds, three inner folds, and three repetitions. We compute the out-of-fold AUROC difference within each repetition and then average the differences.

We estimate 95% confidence intervals using a paired grouped bootstrap (Field and Welsh, 2007), comparing predictors on the same resampled records. Each MCQ question contributes one evaluated response, so sampling a question includes that response. Each H1 prompt has multiple responses; sampling a prompt group includes all its responses to preserve their within-group dependence. A question or prompt group may be sampled more than once.

###### Information and numerical controls.

To examine dependence on generation and sampling signals, both models compare path A, which retains all five blocks, with path C, which removes from both the baseline and fused model. Qwen’s H1 has a non-finite-value handling issue. Path Btherefore reconstructs the affected features while holding hidden states fixed to examine the effect of numerical handling.

###### Grouping sensitivity.

The main H1 split allows identifiers to be shared between training and test sets. To examine dependence on such sharing, Qwen is also evaluated using groups defined by identifiers, vocabulary blocks, and joint prompt–fabricated-identifier relationships. The vocabulary-block comparison redefines labels and hidden states by block; the other comparisons retain the original prediction task.

### 6 Conclusion and Discussion

We evaluate the added predictive value of hidden states using a combined baseline of multiple output-side sources and blockwise comparisons. Hidden states provide predictive information beyond the available output-side information, but strong standalone detection does not necessarily yield large additional gains. On H1, the combined baseline already performs well, improvements from hidden states are small, and the tested readout and representation adaptations preserve this pattern. On MCQ, hidden states continue to add predictive value over the combined output-side baseline. Standalone detection performance is therefore insufficient to establish their added value over existing output-side information.

Baseline construction and information coverage affect gain estimates. Removing generation and sampling signals can increase the gain while reducing fused-model performance. Evaluations should report baseline performance, standalone hidden-state performance, fused performance, and conditional gain together, and specify the output information being compared. These conclusions concern predictive gains under this study’s tasks, models, backends, and learning procedures.

### Limitations

This study has limitations in numerical integrity, parsing conventions, and probe training coverage. The current data do not support an estimate of generalization to unseen identifiers, and the fused model’s advantage over individual blocks varies with grouping*. AUROC measures ranking performance for error prediction; practical utility also depends on detection thresholds, probability calibration, and readout cost. Future work can assess whether predictive gains translate into practical benefits under explicitly specified conditions.

#### A.2 Within-Fold Preprocessing and Nested Stacking

The baseline and fused model share within-fold preprocessing and base-learner scores. We detail the feature transformations, cross-fitting procedure, and learner settings below.

Text blocks use word 1–2-gram and word-boundary character 3–5-gram TF-IDF features. Both vocabularies use min_df=2, at most 50,000 features each, and sublinear term frequency. The two representations are concatenated and each row is L2-normalized. Vocabularies, inverse document frequencies, and all transformations are fitted only on the current training fold and then applied to the corresponding outer test fold.

Dense blocks are standardized using dimension-wise means and standard deviations from the training fold, replacing zero standard deviations with 1. The standardized matrix is divided by , where is the block dimension, to give dense blocks a scale comparable to row-normalized text features. Test folds do not contribute to means, standard deviations, vocabularies, or hyperparameter estimates. Hidden states use the same dense preprocessing.

#### B.3 MCQ Output Coverage and Parsing Denominators

The MCQ output-coverage comparison is non-confirmatory. The original uses selected option text and does not directly include the full model response. Median analyzable response length is 1 character for Qwen and 886 for Llama, while the original has a median length of approximately 32 characters for both. The comparison holds samples and splits fixed within each model, changes only the text source, and refits the text block and fused model. Llama’s joint-condition interval was reproduced from saved predictions under the original resampling plan. This check uses the same statistical library and verifies the computation from saved predictions to metrics and intervals. Intervals for differences between conditions were not computed.

Sampling-consistency features retain successfully parsed labels from the six responses per question and use the number of successful parses as the denominator. Among Llama’s 10,560 raw responses, 168 fail parsing: 14 greedy responses and 154 sampled responses. The numbers of questions with 6, 5, 4, and 3 successful parses are 1,618, 120, 18, and 4, respectively. After excluding greedy failures, 128 of the 1,746 analysis records use a denominator below 6. None of Qwen’s 1,750 analysis records has this issue.

Both consistency features can be recomputed record by record from the raw data under the current rules and match their stored values. However, the original protocol fixed six responses per question without specifying how parsing failures affect the denominator. Using successful parses is a deterministic implementation choice. The effect of alternative denominators on predictive performance and hidden-state gain has not been measured.

#### B.5 Standalone-Statement Adaptation for H1

This adaptation follows the use of final-token representations of standalone statements. #### B.6 Repetition-Averaged Estimand and Uncertainty

Performance differences are averaged over three repetitions, with intervals estimated by paired grouped bootstrap. Numerical error due to finite resampling is estimated separately.

Each repetition produces out-of-fold scores for , , and , with AUROC first calculated within that repetition. The primary estimand is the mean AUROC difference across three repetitions:

(8)

Intervals use a grouped bootstrap. The same group sample is shared across all three repetitions to preserve pairing. H1 resamples 1,508 prompt groups; MCQ resamples 1,750 questions. Every E1 branch has 20,000 valid bootstrap samples, with none discarded for containing only one class. Two-sided 95% intervals use the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the pooled bootstrap distribution. Marginal one-sided lower and upper bounds use the 5th and 95th percentiles.

Each bootstrap sample draws the original number of groups with replacement and concatenates all records in the selected groups, retaining repetitions of groups. H1’s 693 error records are sampled with their groups, preserving within-group label composition. The same resampled records are used for all predictors and repetitions to estimate paired AUROC differences. Based on fixed out-of-fold predictions, the intervals quantify variation induced by group resampling. Their endpoints are the corresponding distribution percentiles, and the group count denotes the number of sampling units. Interpretation assumes independence between groups.

The 20,000 samples are split into 20 batches. Monte Carlo error in interval endpoints and widths is estimated primarily by a delete-one-batch jackknife and checked secondarily by the batch-means method. Width error bands are centered on the interval width from the pooled distribution and use . These bands quantify numerical error from finite resampling. A 95% interval width above 0.04 for is classified as insufficient precision.

#### B.8 Estimands and Group Definitions

The primary estimand E1, entity generalization E2, vocabulary-block generalization E3, and label-dependent grouping M2c use the following split rules.

• 

E1 (primary estimand). Outer splits keep prompt groups disjoint to estimate conditional predictive gain when training and test sets share no prompt groups. H1 has six generation records per prompt, all assigned to the same fold. Each MCQ question forms a separate group.

• 

E2 (entity generalization). H1 is split by connected components of the identifier co-occurrence graph so that an identifier never crosses folds. E2 is non-estimable if the largest component covers more than of records or there are fewer than components; only graph structure is then reported. Here is the fixed number of outer folds, and 25 components ensure at least five per fold on average. These estimability conditions were prespecified for this grouping and cross-validation design.

• 

E3 (vocabulary-block generalization). H1’s identifier vocabulary is partitioned into five fixed blocks without labels; the blocks serve as outer test folds. Labels use only primary identifier mentions within the current vocabulary block, and hidden states are pooled only over token positions corresponding to that block.

• 

M2c (label-dependent grouping sensitivity). H1 is split by connected components formed from prompts and fabricated identifiers. Because grouping uses outcome labels, M2c is reported as a grouping-sensitivity analysis.

E2, E3, and M2c apply only to H1. MCQ lacks the corresponding identifier-entity and vocabulary-block structures, so all three are marked not applicable for MCQ.

Conditional gain can vary with baseline information, numerical handling, and grouping. To examine these dependencies, we remove information blocks, reconstruct affected features, and change sample grouping. Identity and format controls examine task constructs (Hewitt and Liang, 2019; Gururangan et al., 2018; Poliak et al., 2018); identifier and vocabulary grouping examine performance under different sharing conditions (Roberts et al., 2017; Elangovan et al., 2021). The prespecified cross-arm contrast is and requires out-of-fold predictions from the output-side observable predictor , which combines generation scores and visible output text. The implementation mistakenly supplied out-of-fold predictions from the fused model , so the prespecified was not validly estimated. The frozen procedure returned nominal estimates of , , and , with nominal 95% intervals , , and , respectively. These values are retained only as audit records of the input mismatch and cannot determine the prespecified cross-arm gate or compare the tasks. Recalculation under the original definition would be a post hoc recovery analysis.

RQ2 was intended to stratify by identifier-token log-probability and describe conditional gain at different observable confidence levels. Its prespecified primary stratifier depends on the H1 field affected by non-finite-value filtering. Following the branch fixed before unblinding, the primary analysis marks it uninterpretable and stops computation. ### Appendix D Discussion and Limitations

#### D.1 Additional Discussion

Output-baseline performance, task labels, and sample grouping jointly shape the interpretation of hidden-state gains.

Hidden states and output-side information may share predictive cues. Conditional gain measures the additional predictive capability obtained by the current learning procedure beyond existing output information. H1’s output baseline approaches the AUROC ceiling, limiting absolute headroom. Qwen’s baseline is : even a fused AUROC of would leave only about of improvement at the point-estimate level. Llama H1 and the tested Qwen readout adaptations likewise show small gains under the current labels, learners, and evaluation populations.

MCQ retains more pronounced gains. Llama improves by after joint full-response encoding, showing that positive gains persist with broader output-text coverage. The same result illustrates coverage dependence: adding the full response changes both baseline and fused performance and reduces the gain estimate.

Blockwise checks define the baseline’s comparison scope. Non-inferiority assesses the combined baseline against the selected blocks within a specified tolerance. Passing direct comparisons supports fused-model superiority over those blocks; consistency is a separate, more conservative condition. All six Qwen E1 branches fail the consistency check, whereas Llama MCQ path A and the joint full-response condition pass. The lower-bound threshold for this study’s consistency check is .

Reducing baseline information can increase the gain while decreasing final detection performance. This occurs on MCQ path C for both models. Ranking readout methods solely by gain would therefore overlook changes in the reference baseline. Studies should report the combined baseline, standalone hidden-state performance, fused performance, and conditional gain together, specifying the output sources and blockwise baseline comparisons.

Future output-side comparisons could include semantic entropy, SelfCheckGPT, and other text encoders, separately estimating changes in baseline and fused performance. The effect of the parsing-failure denominator also requires re-estimating both.

H1 labels depend on identifier existence in a fixed knowledge base, directly linking identity to the label. Identity alone supplies strong error-prediction cues. In standalone-statement adaptation, about – of test-record texts occur in the corresponding training fold. Identifier memorization is one possible explanation, but its contribution has not been separated from generalization. E2 is non-estimable, leaving generalization to unseen identifiers without a valid estimate. E3 concerns a prediction task with labels and hidden states redefined by vocabulary block. M2c retains positive gains but fails direct blockwise comparison, further limiting generalization across grouping schemes.

#### D.2 Limitations and Unevaluated Comparisons

The experiments leave the following conditions and data-processing limitations unresolved:

1. 

Models, tasks, and analysis status.
We study Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Qwen A/B/C analyses are confirmatory; Llama A/C analyses are non-confirmatory. Both use local 8-bit quantized backends; other precisions and domains are not covered. Gains are estimated under each model’s generation budget, parser version, and analyzable population. H1 labels verify whether generated identifiers exist in a fixed knowledge base.

2. 

Information access and output baselines.
The study uses decoding scores and repeated-sampling statistics, requiring access to those signals. Output controls cover five block types and TF-IDF text encoding; other output-side methods and encoders remain to be compared. Blockwise non-inferiority applies to the selected blocks and prespecified tolerance.

3. 

Readout settings and adaptation scope.
The main analysis uses a fixed layer and linear readout, while additional experiments cover limited MLP, L1, and XGBoost settings. MLP candidate training budgets are limited, and probability clipping in the earlier readout conditions may change extreme scores and fusion inputs. Actual iteration counts for selected models and complete execution traces were not retained. H1 representation adaptation still has upper-bound tuning selections, extensive cross-fold text duplication, and missing intermediate logs. Results concern these representations, budgets, and scoring settings.

4. 

Numerical integrity and parsing conventions.
Non-finite-value filtering in Qwen’s original H1 continues to constrain related conclusions. Llama’s has not undergone an independent numerical integrity audit. Although all target-layer vectors in Qwen statement extraction are finite, downstream layers and output scores widely contain non-finite values. MCQ consistency features use successful parses as the denominator, affecting 128/1,746 Llama analysis rows through failure exclusion. Recalculation agrees with the current implementation, but the failure-denominator rule was not specified in the original protocol. Its directional effect on predictive performance and gain has not been measured.

5. 

Absolute gain and practical utility.
A baseline near the AUROC ceiling limits absolute headroom. Label composition and baseline levels across tasks and models also affect comparisons. This study evaluates ranking performance through AUROC gain and uses an operational tolerance of . Decisions at fixed thresholds, probability calibration, and readout cost remain unevaluated.
