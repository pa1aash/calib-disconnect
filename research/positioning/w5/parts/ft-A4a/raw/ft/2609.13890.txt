Learning How Much to Collaborate: Difficulty-Aware Topology Selection for Multi-Agent
Code Generation
Yunsong Hong∗
School of Computer Science, The University of Sydney, Sydney, NSW 2006, Australia
Abstract
Multi-agent systems for code generation are deployed with a single communication topology, chosen once for every problem.
Task-dependent allocation is an alternative when collaboration benefits vary across inputs. Evaluating five topologies on 614
problems from APPS, HumanEval+ and LiveCodeBench, we find that the advantage of hierarchical collaboration over a single
agent grows from 2.4 points of pass@1 on the easiest third of problems to 21.1 points on the hardest third, while its token cost stays
about ten times higher. We propose the Difficulty-Aware Topology Selector (Dats), which predicts each topology’s probability of
solving a problem and selects the one maximising predicted success minus cost. Its predictor is a graph network that treats the
five topologies as nodes of a connectivity order. The complete graph-head comparison has a reported 1.7-point advantage over
the flat multi-label head and changes several mechanisms together. Additional capacity-matched adjacency controls show smaller
descriptive differences in a separate configuration. Because the cost penalty is a single scalar recalibrable without retraining,
routers can be compared at approximately matched spend when the target is attained. With a shared penalty six cost-aware methods
span 21.6 percentage points of spend; two leading baselines fall behind DATS after method-specific calibration near the common
target. Unmatched rows are reported separately. Fixed at 40% of the always-hierarchical cost, Dats reaches 77.7% pass@1 against
73.6% (always-hierarchical) and 74.3% (strongest learned competitor). The main comparison includes Holm–Bonferroni-adjusted
McNemar tests. Statistical differences at unequal costs do not imply matched-budget superiority. Backbone-specific comparisons
show gains of 4.07–4.23 points across four backbones. Replacing the 39 interpretable features with a graph network or a pretrained
encoder gives a largest observed improvement of 1.3 points among tested alternatives, with no significant pairwise improvement;
this does not establish equivalence or an upper bound. A cross-domain study on 400 mathematical reasoning problems reproduces
the effect, the gap widening from 2.5 to 20.9 points.
Keywords: multi-agent systems, large language models, code generation, adaptive computation, cost-aware routing,
predict-then-optimize, budget-matched evaluation
1. Introduction
Practical motivation. A code-generation service receives tasks
with different decomposition and verification needs. Choosing
collaboration before execution makes its cost part of resource
allocation: the service represents the request, estimates candi-
date success probabilities, and selects a topology under its cost
preference. The candidate communication graph is an execu-
tion design; the topology graph inside the predictor is a repre-
sentation used to compare those designs.
Challenges behind the contributions. Adaptive allocation re-
quires distinguishing tasks for which collaboration can help
from tasks for which it only adds cost.
Multi-label predic-
tion accommodates overlapping successful topologies, whereas
a single target class discards this overlap. The graph head of-
fers a structured sharing hypothesis, but isolating its benefit re-
quires controls for descriptors, sharing and parameter capac-
ity. Finally, fair cost comparisons require calibration of each

method’s operating point rather than equality of the penalty co-

Large language models write code well enough that the in-
teresting engineering question is no longer whether to use them
but how to arrange them. A single model called once is the
cheapest arrangement. Beyond it lies a design space of multi-
agent systems in which several model instances take on roles,
exchange drafts, criticise each other, and converge on an an-
swer. Frameworks such as ChatDev [1], MetaGPT [2] and Au-
toGen [3] have made these arrangements easy to build, and spe-
cialised pipelines such as AgentCoder [4] and MapCoder [5]
report that collaboration lifts pass@1 on competitive program-
ming benchmarks well above what one call achieves.
What these systems share is a commitment: the topology is
fixed at design time. A pipeline that decomposes, delegates and
reviews does so for every incoming problem, whether the prob-
lem asks for a hierarchical decomposition or for a three-line
string manipulation. The commitment is convenient to imple-
ment and expensive to run. In our measurements a hierarchical
role-based mesh consumes 9.95 times the tokens of a single call

on the same problem; at Claude Opus 4 prices that is $0.94 ver-
sus $0.09 per problem, a difference that dominates the operating
cost of any deployed system.
The commitment would be defensible if the extra collabora-
tion paid off uniformly. It does not. Splitting our 614 problems
into difficulty tertiles, the gap between the hierarchical mesh
and a single agent is 2.4 points of pass@1 on the easy third,
10.7 points on the middle third, and 21.1 points on the hard
third. On easy problems the elaborate pipeline spends ten times
the budget to recover two points that a single call very nearly
reaches on its own. An aggregate accuracy gain therefore ob-
scures the different marginal benefits observed across these dif-
ficulty strata. Under the cost preferences considered here, addi-
tional collaboration offers a smaller accuracy return on the easy
stratum.
For the heterogeneous task collection and cost preferences
studied here, variation in collaboration benefit motivates choos-
ing topology as a function of the input. A fixed topology can
remain appropriate for a homogeneous workload or a different
cost preference. We therefore ask whether an input-dependent
choice can be learned from the problem statement before code
generation.
We show that it can, and that the way the decision is fac-
tored matters.
The natural first attempt is to train a classi-
fier that maps a problem directly to the topology that solved
it. This throws away the structure of the decision. The label
“the topology that solved it” is ambiguous when several topolo-
gies succeed and undefined when none do, and it hard-codes an
accuracy–cost trade-off into the training targets, so a classifier
trained for a single operating point may need retraining when
that point changes. A budget-conditioned classifier is an alter-
native and is not excluded by this argument. Instead we pre-
dict what is actually observable—for each topology, whether it
solved each cached problem—and defer the trade-off to a deci-
sion rule applied at inference. The predictor answers five inde-
pendent questions of the form “would this topology solve this
problem”; the selector combines those answers with a cost ta-
ble through a single scalar coefficient. The factorisation is a
standard predict-then-optimize construction [6]. The reported
difference from direct classification is approximately 6.0 points
of pass@1, but the realised budgets differ; this comparison does
not isolate an equal-budget causal benefit of factorisation.
Keeping the trade-off in one scalar has a second consequence
that turned out to matter more than the first, and that we think
outlives the router it came from. Varying the cost penalty de-
fines a family of routing policies with different attainable op-
erating points; it does not guarantee every spend between the
cheapest and most expensive topology, and reporting its accu-
racy without pinning its spend says very little. Giving every
method the same coefficient does not repair this, because the
coefficient multiplies each method’s own score scale: in our
comparison a shared λ = 0.2 leaves six methods spending any-
where from 30% to 52% of the reference budget. We there-
fore calibrate every cost-aware method, ours and the baselines
alike, to one common budget before comparing them, choos-
ing each method’s coefficient by bisection on held-out predic-
tions toward 40% of the always-hierarchical cost. Attainment
is checked separately: four calibrated methods meet the outer
tolerance and two do not. Two baselines that appeared to beat
Dats under the shared coefficient fall behind it once their spend
is equalised, and the method that looked best turns out to have
been the one buying the most computation. We state the pro-
cedure as a protocol, report what it does and does not equalise,
and regard it as a requirement for this class of comparison rather
than a refinement of it.
The contributions of this work are the following.
First,
we quantify how the benefit of five collaboration topologies
varies with problem difficulty and algorithmic type on the
614-problem main evaluation, with additional aggregate com-
parisons across four backbones.
The main evaluation moti-
vates task-dependent allocation. Second, we formulate topol-
ogy choice as a per-problem constrained selection problem
and instantiate it as Dats, a predict-then-optimize router whose
predictor is a compact graph network over the five candidate
topologies: because the topologies form a connectivity order,
we model them as nodes of that order and pass messages along
it rather than scoring them as independent labels. The com-
bined head comparison has a reported 1.7-point gain at the dis-
played spend; it does not isolate adjacency. We compare deci-
sion heads and input representations separately. Alternatives to
the 39 interpretable features have a largest observed improve-
ment of 1.3 points in the tested set, without significant pairwise
improvement. This does not prove representation equivalence
or locate all gains in the decision head. Third, we define the
budget-matched evaluation protocol described above, show that
it reverses two of the comparisons among the six cost-aware
methods it governs, and use it throughout. Fourth, we char-
acterise the resulting behaviour and its boundaries: which fea-
tures carry the signal, which topologies get chosen for which
algorithmic types, how the router’s probabilities calibrate, how
much of the gain survives a change of backbone, and—on 400
GSM8K and MATH problems—whether any of this depends
on the problems being code at all.
2. Related work
Routing and cooperation studies. LLMRouterBench [66] con-
cerns evaluation of routing among language models; The
Avengers [67] addresses routing and collective model capa-
bilities.
These are useful comparisons for resource alloca-
tion and evaluation, while the action here is a communica-
tion topology within a fixed catalogue. Work on networking
agency [68], communication under collective risks [69], cogni-
tive context [70], identifiable partners [71], and regret dynam-
ics [72] provides broader context for interaction design. Evi-
dence from human or social systems does not establish the ef-
fectiveness of a language-agent communication graph.
2.1. Communication topologies for language agents
Early multi-agent work with language models established
that role assignment and message passing change what a sys-
tem can solve. CAMEL [7] paired an instruction-giving agent
with an executing agent; debate procedures [8, 9] ran sev-
eral instances against each other and aggregated; generative
2

agents [10] scaled the interaction to societies.
For software
specifically, ChatDev [1] and MetaGPT [2] encode a water-
fall process into agent roles, AgentVerse [11] assembles task-
specific teams on demand, self-collaboration prompting [12]
obtains the same effect from a single model playing several
roles, and AutoGen [3] provides the conversational substrate
on which such processes are assembled.
A second line treats the communication graph itself as an ob-
ject to optimise. GPTSwarm [13] represents agent systems as
graphs and optimises edges by reinforcement learning; Mac-
Net [14] studies how performance scales as the graph grows;
AgentPrune [15] removes redundant edges to cut token spend;
G-Designer [16] generates a task-conditioned topology with a
graph neural network. Our work shares the premise that topol-
ogy is a decision variable, and differs in where the decision is
made. These methods search for or generate a graph, usually
per task family, and pay a search or generation cost. We take
a small, fixed catalogue of hand-designed topologies and learn
only which catalogue entry to use for a given problem. The
catalogue is coarser, but selection over it costs a single forward
pass and permits empirical calibration of average spend on val-
idation predictions. This is not a hard budget guarantee on un-
seen tasks; attainable operating points and realised test costs
must be reported.
Evidence on whether collaboration helps at all is mixed.
Wang et al. [17] report that multi-agent discussion does not con-
sistently outperform a single strong agent with a good prompt.
Our difficulty analysis is consistent with both that finding and
with the positive reports: averaged over a benchmark whose
problems are mostly easy, discussion looks unnecessary; re-
stricted to the hard tail, it is decisive.
2.2. Iterative refinement and self-correction
Chain, star and mesh topologies inherit their machinery
from single-agent prompting and refinement. Chain-of-thought
prompting [18] and ReAct [19] established that structuring a
model’s intermediate output changes what it can solve; Self-
Refine [20] and Reflexion [21] loop generation and critique;
Self-Debug [22] feeds execution feedback back to the model;
Self-Consistency [23] and Tree of Thoughts [24] broaden the
search rather than deepen the critique. These are complemen-
tary to topology selection: each of our topologies could be in-
stantiated with a stronger refinement operator, and the routing
problem would remain.
2.3. Routing and cascades over models
Choosing which model to call for which query is a well-
studied cost-reduction strategy. FrugalGPT [25] cascades from
cheap to expensive models with a learned stopping rule; Hybrid
LLM [26] routes between a small and a large model on pre-
dicted difficulty; RouteLLM [27] learns the router from prefer-
ence data; ZOOTER [28] distils reward signals into a routing
function; meta-modelling approaches [29] predict per-model
performance and pick the cheapest adequate one; Router-
Bench [30] standardises evaluation of such systems. Cascades
with mixed thought representations [31] apply the same idea to
reasoning chains.
Two recent methods route within multi-agent systems rather
than over single models. MasRouter [32] jointly selects agents,
models and collaboration mode; Router-R1 [33] learns multi-
round routing and aggregation with reinforcement learning.
Our formulation is narrower than either—one decision, made
once, over a fixed catalogue—which is what allows the budget-
matched protocol and the significance testing we report. We
reimplement lightweight versions of both as baselines.
The broader question of how much computation to spend
per input has been studied as adaptive computation. Confident
Adaptive Language Modeling [34] exits early when a per-token
confidence threshold is met; mixture-of-experts routing [35]
conditions capacity on the input; test-time compute scaling [36]
and repeated sampling [37] characterise how accuracy responds
to additional inference budget. Topology selection is adaptive
computation at the granularity of an entire agent pipeline.
2.4. Code generation benchmarks and models
We evaluate on APPS [38], which stratifies problems into
introductory, interview and competition levels; HumanEval+,
the strengthened test suite of EvalPlus [39] over the origi-
nal HumanEval [40]; and LiveCodeBench [41], which draws
from contest problems released after model training cut-offs.
MBPP [42], AlphaCode [43] and SWE-bench [44] define ad-
jacent regimes we do not cover. Our backbones are Claude
Opus 4, DeepSeek-V3 [45], GPT-4o-mini [46] and Qwen2.5-
Coder-32B [47], the last chosen so that the gradient includes
a code-specialised model of the kind introduced by DeepSeek-
Coder [48].
2.5. Representations of code and of problem statements
A router needs a representation of the input, and the natu-
ral candidates come from code representation learning. Code-
BERT [49] pretrains a bimodal transformer on code paired
with natural language; GraphCodeBERT [50] adds data-flow
edges to the same objective; UniXcoder [51] unifies the en-
coder and decoder views; code2vec [52] predates them with
paths through the abstract syntax tree; and graph convolutional
networks [53, 54] supply the machinery for encoding either a
syntax tree or a statement graph. Program-structure methods
require the relevant syntax or data-flow representation. In con-
trast, pretrained models such as CodeBERT and UniXcoder can
encode a natural-language statement without a generated pro-
gram. Our pre-execution setting excludes unavailable program
structure, not natural-language use of pretrained encoders. Sec-
tion 7.3 compares selected pretrained encoders and a statement-
graph encoder with the hand-crafted features.
2.6. Mathematical reasoning as a second domain
Our cross-domain check uses GSM8K [55], grade-school
word problems with a single numeric answer, and MATH [56],
competition problems annotated with five difficulty levels,
which is what makes it usable for a difficulty-scaling analysis.
Multi-agent methods have been applied to both: debate [8] and
self-consistency [23] report gains on arithmetic and mathemat-
ical reasoning, and program-aided approaches [57] offload the
3

arithmetic to an interpreter. We use these suites not to compete
on them but to ask whether the difficulty-dependent value of
collaboration that we measure on code is a property of code or
a property of hard problems.

3.1. The five topologies
Single issues one call with the problem statement and a short
instruction. Chain runs three sequential calls: a proposer writes
a first solution, a critic lists concrete defects, and a reviser pro-
duces the final program. Star uses four calls: a hub decom-
poses the statement into three subtasks and three workers solve
them independently, after which the returns are concatenated
deterministically into the final program without a further model
call. FullMesh runs three peers for two rounds, six calls in to-
tal, with each peer seeing the other two drafts in the second
round. HRM, the hierarchical role-based mesh, places a man-
ager above two leads, each of which supervises two workers;
leads consolidate their workers’ output and the manager arbi-
trates, for five to eight calls depending on whether the man-
ager requests a second pass. The catalogue is deliberately small
and hand-designed: our claim concerns the value of choosing
among topologies, not the value of any particular topology.
4. Difficulty-aware topology selection
Dats estimates st(x) for all five topologies from the problem
statement alone, then applies Eq. (2) with a calibrated multiplier
(Fig. 2). Nothing in the pipeline requires executing a topol-
ogy at decision time, and nothing propagates gradients into the
agents.
Prediction and selection. The predictor estimates candidate-
specific success scores from the same task representation. The
selection rule then compares these scores after the cost penalty.
This separation allows the preferred action to change with the
deployment budget without redefining a unique correct topol-
ogy for every training example. A graph-based head can share
information between candidate descriptions; such sharing is a
modelling hypothesis, not by itself evidence of greater predic-
tive accuracy. Flat, node-wise, descriptor and adjacency con-
trols address different parts of that hypothesis.
4.1. Features
The feature map ϕ : X →R39 is deliberately interpretable,
because we want to be able to say afterwards which properties
of a problem drive the decision. It has six groups.
The lexical group (five dimensions) records length in words,
sentence count, average word length, the fraction of tokens that
appear inside code spans, and a constraint density obtained by
counting phrases such as “at most”, “exactly” and “for each”.
The structural group (four) counts worked examples, detects
an explicit edge-case discussion, counts required functions in
the signature, and scores input–output complexity from the de-
scribed formats. The complexity group (three) captures cues
that a solution will need nesting or asymptotic care: depth hints
such as “for each pair”, the number of large numeric bounds,
and the count of algorithmic keywords associated with hard
techniques. The difficulty group is a single scalar predicted from
the statement. A ridge regressor with an L2 penalty of 1 uses
the twelve lexical, structural and complexity features to pre-
dict a five-level difficulty rating. Training ratings follow a fixed
rubric: direct translation, one elementary technique, composi-
tion of familiar techniques, an advanced technique, and several
interacting advanced techniques. The prediction is clipped to
[1, 5] and mapped to [0, 1] by (r −1)/4. The scorer and its in-
put standardisation are fitted within each training fold; training
examples receive inner out-of-fold difficulty predictions. Val-
idation and test ratings are never supplied to the router. No
language-model call is needed. The same scoring procedure is
used in the primary and additional comparisons. The semantic
group (sixteen) holds the leading singular directions of a TF–
IDF representation of the statement. The algorithmic group

4.2. Predictor
The predictor maps a task representation to five marginal
success probabilities.
A flat multi-label predictor is a valid
baseline; separate sigmoid losses do not by themselves estab-
lish statistical independence of outcomes. The proposed topol-
ogy graph expresses a hypothesis that structural descriptors and
shared computation can help predict correlated candidate out-
comes. The connectivity order does not require success to be
nested: a heavier topology can fail on a problem solved by a
lighter one. Whether a particular adjacency helps must be tested
against controlled alternatives.
We therefore make the label space explicit and predict over
it with a small graph network. Let GT = (T , E) be the topol-
ogy graph: its five nodes are the topologies and its edges join
each topology to its neighbours in the connectivity order, with
the hierarchical mesh linked to Star and FullMesh as the two
structures it composes. Each node carries a static descriptor
et ∈R4—agent count, edge count, diameter and mean degree
of the topology’s own communication graph—so the network is
told what each option is, not merely that five options exist.
The problem enters through a shared trunk with affine layers
of widths 128 and 64, each followed by a ReLU. The 39 stan-

The input representation and output head are distinct design
choices. A program syntax graph is unavailable before code
generation; a natural-language statement graph and text en-
coders are available. The output topology graph is a predefined
relation between candidate communication designs. Section 7
compares the complete head with a flat alternative, without iso-
lating adjacency. Figure 3 illustrates the shared trunk, message
passing and per-node readout; the additional controlled config-
uration is specified in Section 7.2.
Two design points carry over unchanged because they are
easy to get wrong. First, the targets are per-topology outcomes,
not a single categorical label, so problems that every topology
solves and problems that none solve both contribute to the bi-
nary prediction loss. For example, targets (1, 0, 1, 0, 0) retain
two successful options, while (0, 0, 0, 0, 0) trains all success
scores downward without inventing a successful class. An all-
zero observed target does not prove every future rollout will
fail. The selector favours the cheapest option only when pre-
dicted success scores are tied (with a positive cost penalty); that
choice is not an extra training label. Second, the network never
sees cost, which is what keeps the trade-off confined to the se-
lector.

4.4. The budget-matched protocol
The calibration step above was introduced as a component of
Dats. We now separate it from Dats, because it is not specific to
our router and because without it the accuracies that this class
of method reports are not comparable to each other. We state
it as a protocol and treat it as a contribution independent of the
model it was developed for.
The difficulty it addresses is that a cost-aware router is not
a system but a one-parameter family of systems. Any method
of the form “score minus λ times cost” sweeps out a curve in
the cost–accuracy plane as λ moves, and every point of that
curve is a legitimate configuration of the same method. An ac-
curacy reported without its realised spend therefore identifies a
curve only up to an unknown position along it, and two such
numbers cannot be subtracted. The obvious repair—give ev-
ery method the same λ—does not work, because λ acts on each
method’s own score scale. A predictor whose probabilities are
compressed towards 0.5 upgrades to an expensive topology less
often, at identical λ, than one whose probabilities are spread
towards the extremes, so a shared coefficient silently places
the methods at different operating points. In our comparison
a shared λ = 0.2 spreads realised spend over 21.6 percentage
points of the reference budget (Section 6.4).
The protocol has four steps. One, declare a budget B as a
fraction of the realised spend of a reference policy that requires
no calibration; we use the most expensive fixed topology, which
makes B interpretable as “this fraction of what the standard
pipeline costs”. Two, calibrate each method separately, by bi-
section on its own coefficient inside the training folds, never on
the test fold. Three, verify the realised spend out of fold, report
it beside the accuracy, and declare a method budget-matched
only if it lands inside the stated outer-evaluation tolerance of
0.4 percentage points. Four of the six calibrated methods meet
it; E2E and Difficulty-Threshold do not. The inner search uses
its separately stated 0.5-percentage-point tolerance. Four, com-
pare accuracies only within the matched group, and report poli-
cies that cannot be calibrated—fixed topologies, the oracle—
separately, as locators of the frontier rather than as competitors.
Three properties make the protocol cheap to adopt. It re-
quires only that the cost used for the search be monotone in the
coefficient. This holds for fixed scores when the same fixed cost
table is used in both selection and search evaluation: raising λ
cannot increase that selected cost. A separately calculated to-
ken bill requires its own empirical check. It converges quickly,
needing 21.4 bisection steps on average to reach the 0.5% inner
tolerance. And on a cached evaluation grid it costs no inference
at all, since every candidate assignment is scored against out-
comes that have already been paid for. What it does not equalise
should also be stated: it matches expected monetary spend, not
latency, not worst-case cost, and not the variance of either. A
method that meets the budget by occasionally choosing a very
expensive topology is matched to one that never does, and the
two may be quite different to operate.

4.5. Cost of the router
The 39–128–64–5 shorthand specifies the input, trunk widths
and five output scores; the intervening four-dimensional node
states are specified in Section 4.2. The primary router has 13.7K
neural parameters and takes 28 seconds to train on 614 prob-
lems on CPU. Feature extraction, including the linear difficulty
scorer, takes under a millisecond per problem. The cheapest
language-model topology takes 8.4 seconds. Monetary budgets
account for topology execution tokens; local feature extraction
and router computation are reported separately and do not re-
quire a paid model call.
5. Experimental setup
5.1. Problems
We evaluate on 614 problems (Table 1): 300 from APPS,
one hundred at each of the introductory, interview and compe-
tition levels; the full 164 problems of HumanEval+; and 150
problems from LiveCodeBench drawn from contests held af-
ter the training cut-off of every backbone we test. The suites
were chosen to span difficulty rather than to maximise size: Hu-
manEval+ supplies a regime where a single call almost always
suffices, APPS-Competition supplies one where nothing reli-
ably works, and the middle suites populate the interval between
them where routing decisions are actually contested. Mean dif-
ficulty in Table 1 is the normalised rating described in Sec-
tion 4.1, reported here to show that the suites are ordered as
intended.
5.2. Backbones and execution
Claude Opus 4 is the primary backbone; DeepSeek-V3, GPT-
4o-mini and Qwen2.5-Coder-32B provide a capability gradient
for the generalisation study. Every agent inside a topology uses
the same backbone, so the comparison isolates topology from
model choice. Decoding uses temperature 0.2 and a 4096-token
output cap, and we report pass@1 from a single sample, since a
router that only helps under repeated sampling would be solv-
ing a different problem. A program counts as correct only if
it passes the complete test suite. The evaluator executes all
configured checks for the problem. The Tests column counts
grouped checks in the execution harness, rather than the num-
ber of raw inputs in an expanded benchmark distribution; in
particular, it must not be read as the raw HumanEval+ assertion
count.

5.4. Protocol
All routers are evaluated by five-fold cross-validation strati-
fied jointly on suite and difficulty tertile, repeated over five ran-
dom seeds; reported accuracy is the pooled out-of-fold accuracy
of the first seed, and the seed-to-seed standard deviation is re-
ported alongside. Hyperparameters and the cost multiplier are
selected inside a three-fold split of each training fold and never
touch the corresponding test fold.
The budget is fixed at B = 40% of the always-hierarchical
cost, that is $0.377 per problem as the nominal target. Cali-
brated methods use their own coefficients and searches under
Section 4.4; only those whose reported outer cost meets the de-
clared tolerance enter matched-budget ranking. Fixed topolo-
gies obviously cannot be calibrated and are reported at their nat-
ural cost; they are included to locate the frontier, not as budget-
matched competitors.
5.5. Baselines
Five Always-τ policies establish the fixed-topology frontier.
Random samples a topology uniformly and is included to con-
firm that the routing gain is not an artefact of mixing. Difficulty-
Threshold bins the predicted difficulty rating and maps bins to
topologies, with thresholds set on the training fold to meet the
budget; it represents the intuition that difficulty alone should
be enough. DATS-E2E is a direct five-way classification refer-
ence. Its stated target is the cheapest successful topology, which
8

is defined only when at least one candidate succeeds. The re-
tained oracle count implies 614 −546 = 68 problems with
no successful topology in the cached grid. All-failed training
examples receive the cheapest-topology fallback class, retain-
ing all training examples. Ties between successful candidates
use the same cost order. The softmax baseline is trained with
cross-entropy; this explicit fallback rule is used throughout the
E2E comparisons. Its displayed accuracy is reported at its ac-
tual operating cost, not as a controlled isolation of predict-then-
optimize factorisation. The fallback class is an explicit single-
label convention, distinct from the multi-label all-zero target
in Section 4.2. MasRouter-lite follows [32] in learning a rout-
ing distribution over collaboration modes, restricted here to a
logistic model on the lexical and structural features. RouterR1-
lite follows [33] in learning a reward regressor—ridge regres-
sion on accuracy minus normalised cost—and selecting the
argmax. RandomForest-Router substitutes a 300-tree forest for
the network in the same predict-then-optimize scaffold and is
the strongest learned competitor. The oracle completes the ta-
ble.
5.6. Statistics
Accuracy intervals are Wilson score intervals at 95% [58].
Paired comparisons between routers on the same problems use
McNemar’s test [59], exact binomial when the number of dis-
cordant pairs is below 25 and with Yates’ continuity correc-
tion otherwise, as recommended for classifier comparison by
Dietterich [60]. Families of comparisons are corrected by the
Holm–Bonferroni procedure [61]. The six feature-group abla-
tions form one comparison family; their significance markers
use Holm-adjusted McNemar p values.
Calibration is sum-
marised by expected calibration error over ten equal-width
bins [62, 63] and by the Brier score [64]. Scikit-learn [65] im-
plements ridge scoring, TF–IDF/SVD preprocessing, random
forests and the linear probes. PyTorch implements the topol-
ogy head and statement GCN; transformer encoders use the
Transformers interface with PyTorch. The graph models use
Adam, batch size 64, an initial learning rate of 10−3, at most
400 epochs and early stopping with patience 30 on training-side
validation loss. Seeds 0–4 control initialisation and minibatch
order; data partitions are held fixed for paired model compar-
isons.

6.2. The benefit of collaboration scales with difficulty
Fig. 4 splits the 614 problems into difficulty tertiles. The five
topology curves fan out as difficulty rises: on the easy third they
occupy a 2.4-point band, on the middle third 10.7 points, on the
hard third 21.1 points. The hierarchical mesh is not uniformly
better than a single call; it is barely better on easy problems and
decisively better on hard ones, at constant relative cost.
This reported heterogeneity motivates task-dependent allo-
cation. A fixed topology can remain appropriate for a homoge-
neous workload or a particular cost preference; these aggregate
strata do not establish misallocation on every problem. A sys-
tem fixed on Single forgoes twenty-one points on the hard tail.
A system fixed on HRM spends ten times the budget on the easy
two-thirds to gain between two and ten points, and in the easy
third specifically it spends $0.94 per problem to move 91.7% to
94.1%.
The Dats curve in the same figure shows what per-problem
allocation buys. It tracks the hierarchical mesh on the easy
third, where it mostly declines to pay for collaboration, and ex-
ceeds it by 10.3 points on the hard third, where it concentrates
the budget it saved. The router does not merely interpolate be-
tween the fixed policies; on the hard tertile it is above all five of
them, because different hard problems want different topologies
and no single policy can follow.

sured once against the unchanged matching tolerance. A closer
inner match can change the selected routes and the achieved
outer cost without retraining or guaranteeing a match on every
evaluation set.

not a proof of superiority over an entire cost–accuracy curve.

6.8. Beyond code: routing for mathematical reasoning
Changing the backbone tests whether the router depends on
a particular model. It does not test whether it depends on code.
The mechanism we have described is stated in terms of prob-
lem difficulty and problem type, neither of which is specific
to programming, so the claim implies a prediction: on a do-
main where difficulty also varies widely, the same construction
should work, and the collaboration gap should again widen with
difficulty. We tested the prediction on mathematical reasoning,
which shares the difficulty spread of competitive programming
but none of its surface form—no test suites, no execution, and
no code in the answer.
The setup mirrors Section 6.3 with three substitutions. The
problems are 400 items, 200 from GSM8K [55] and 200 from
MATH [56] covering levels one to five. Sampling is stratified
over three difficulty bands: 66 items from levels one and two,
67 from level three, and 67 from levels four and five. Items are
sampled without replacement within each band, using a fixed
seed before any topology execution. The five topologies keep
their communication structure and receive math-adapted role
prompts, so that the critic checks derivations rather than edge
cases and the merger reconciles final answers rather than func-
tions. Correctness is exact match on the final answer instead of
a hidden test suite. The router is re-instantiated and retrained
rather than transferred unchanged. The 39-feature interface is
retained: eight mathematical-category indicators replace the ten
algorithmic slots, with the remaining two slots fixed to zero.
The other twenty-nine features are unchanged in number, giv-
ing 29+8+2 = 39 input coordinates and 37 potentially nonzero
coordinates. Keyword votes recover the manual mathematical
labels on 82.3% of problems. Constant zero columns are left at
zero during standardisation. Costs are re-measured on this grid,
where the hierarchical topology is 9.7 times a single call, and
the budget is again set to 40% of it, $0.159 per problem.

and the most expensive is 2.5 points on GSM8K and 11.0 points
on MATH, so the same fixed pipeline is nearly free of benefit on

6.9. Calibration
The selection rule compares predicted probabilities against a
cost penalty expressed on the same scale, so systematic over- or
under-confidence would distort the trade-off even if the ranking
of topologies were correct. Figure 9 summarises the reported
calibration diagnostics. Each point compares a probability bin
with its observed success rate, and the grey bars indicate relative
bin populations. The reported ECE is 0.0275 and the reported
Brier score is 0.1723. ECE summarises bin-level calibration
differences, while the Brier score measures squared probability
error.
Probability calibration affects which substitutions are se-
lected at a given cost penalty. Budget calibration searches at-
tainable operating points on inner validation predictions; it can-
not guarantee that a target exists or that the final outer-test spend
14

matches it. Reusing a predictor at another budget requires re-
porting both the requested target and the achieved cost, with
unmatched cases identified separately.
6.10. Repeated generation with frozen routing
We select 120 problems from the 614-problem code
pool before repeated evaluation:
20 each from APPS-

Within each

7.3. Is the representation the bottleneck?
The feature map of Section 4.1 is deliberately shallow, and
the obvious objection is that a learned representation would
carry more of the signal. The objection deserves a measurement
rather than an argument, so we replaced ϕ with five stronger en-
coders and left everything else—the topology-graph head, the
selection rule, the folds and the 40% budget—untouched. Ta-
ble 15 reports the ladder.
Three things about the ladder are worth reading carefully.
The first is that no row separates from the baseline. The
spread over six representations is 2.44 points, every McNe-
mar comparison against Dats returns p > 0.35, and the two
encoders that beat the hand-crafted features do so by 0.49 and
1.30 points—smaller than the 0.71-point seed-to-seed standard
deviation of the pipeline in one case, and less than twice it in
the other. One hundred and twenty-five million parameters of
pretrained code knowledge, fine-tuned on the task, buy half a
point that we cannot distinguish from noise.
The second is why the graph encoder is the weakest row,
since a reviewer would reasonably expect it to be the strongest.
A graph neural network over an abstract syntax tree is not avail-
able to us: the router runs before any code exists, so there is no
tree to encode. The closest object at decision time is the prob-
lem statement, and our Text-GNN builds a co-occurrence and
dependency graph over it and applies a two-layer GCN [53].
That graph is a graph of English, not of a program, and it is a
lossy substitute. The same reasoning explains the frozen code
encoders: CodeBERT [49] and GraphCodeBERT [50] were
pretrained on code paired with documentation, and we feed
them documentation without code, which is a text-only use of
the encoders. The comparison does not establish which part of
their pretraining distribution they represent best.
The third is the most useful. A linear probe from the frozen
GraphCodeBERT [CLS] vector recovers our difficulty rating
with R2 = 0.61 and our algorithmic category with 71.4% accu-
racy. The probe results indicate overlap with the explicit diffi-
culty and category features. Such overlap is a possible explana-
tion for the reported 1.30-point hybrid gain, but the probe does
not establish its cause or show that the two representations are
equivalent.
We retain the interpretable features as the main representa-
tion.
The primary router takes 28 seconds of CPU training
versus 36 minutes of GPU training for the fine-tuned encoder.
DATS-Hybrid has the highest observed accuracy among the
tested representations, with a 1.30-point improvement. This is
the largest observed gain in the comparison, not a performance
ceiling. Non-significant pairwise tests do not establish equiva-
lence.
7.4. The budget knob
Figure 11 shows the reported DATS penalty sweep, with ac-
curacy and cost evaluated at each displayed coefficient. For
fixed prediction scores and a fixed cost table, selected cost is
non-increasing in λ; this property does not automatically ex-
tend to item-specific measured expenditure. The figure displays
sweep points without overlaying the foldwise calibration range.
The nominal-budget results form a separate series: the reported
15% and 55% targets have accuracies of 70.20% and 80.29%,
respectively. The additional matched-budget comparisons in
Section 6.4 are calibrated separately from this primary series.
Marginal returns below use the primary budget-sweep series,
rather than the separately calibrated points in Table 6. From the
nominal 55% to 70% targets, realised spend rises from approx-
imately 55.00% to 64.68% of the HRM reference cost, while
accuracy rises from 80.29% to 81.76%. This corresponds to ap-
proximately 1.52 accuracy percentage points per ten percentage
points of realised spend over that interval. The nominal 100%
target reaches only 66.58% of the reference cost, with 83.06%
accuracy. Thus the 70% and 100% targets are unmatched oper-
ating points; their nominal increments must not be interpreted
as additional realised expenditure.

These com-

specific training; the mathematical experiment uses domain-

its use, or of something equivalent, whenever cost-aware meth-

off, not a formal robustness guarantee. The fixed catalogue and
single routing decision also limit adaptation after execution be-
gins. The architecture controls show modest adjacency differ-
ences. Repeated generation and paired retraining quantify two
distinct sources of variability; some route assignments change
when labels are averaged. Cross-router rankings cover the three
tested budgets, and do not imply a ranking at every possible op-
erating point.
8.2. Boundaries of the claim
The catalogue is fixed and small. Dats chooses among five
hand-designed topologies; it cannot construct a new one, and
the oracle gap of 11.2 points bounds what better selection over
this catalogue could achieve. Methods that generate topolo-
gies [16, 13] operate in a larger space and are complementary: a
generator could extend the catalogue, and a selector would still
be needed to choose among what it produces.
The router decides once, before execution. It cannot notice
that a single call has produced an obviously broken program
and escalate. Cascade formulations [25, 31] make exactly that
observation and pay for it with a second inference. Combin-
ing the two—route first, escalate on a verifier signal—is the
extension we consider most likely to close part of the oracle
gap, since, under the same cached grid, 546 −477 = 69 of
the 614 −477 = 137 problems missed by DATS are solved by
another catalogue topology.
Transfer across backbones is incomplete: Section 6.7 reports
a 2.28-point loss. Before deployment, the target backbone re-
quires checks of prediction calibration and achieved budget.
Recalibration is a candidate adjustment whose benefit should
be evaluated on target-backbone validation data. The aggregate
transfer result does not isolate calibration as the cause of the
loss or establish which task rankings and topology preferences
are preserved.
The cross-domain evidence is a check, not a second study.
Four hundred mathematical problems on one backbone show
that the mechanism is not an artefact of code, since the difficulty
progression reappears almost unchanged, with domain-specific
training. This supports the applicability of the allocation mech-
anism to that mathematical dataset; transferring a trained router
between unrelated domains is a different claim.
Section 7.3 reports a largest observed improvement of 1.3
points among the tested alternatives. This is a descriptive maxi-
mum in that comparison, not a statistical upper bound on repre-
18

sentation benefits. The absence of a significant pairwise differ-
ence does not establish equivalence, and conclusions need not
extend to other encoders or a larger topology catalogue.
9. Conclusion
The amount of collaboration a multi-agent system should buy
depends on the problem in front of it, and the dependence is
strong enough to be worth learning. In the main Claude evalu-
ation, the advantage of hierarchical collaboration over a single
call grows from roughly two points on easy problems to twenty-
one on hard ones. The four-backbone table reports aggregate
comparisons rather than separate confirmation of that stratified
trend. DATS reports a 4.07-point gain over Always-HRM at
40% of its cost in the main evaluation, with aggregate gains
of 4.07–4.23 points across the reported backbones. The 400-
problem mathematical evaluation reports a 3.5-point gain after
retraining. The mathematical result concerns retraining on the
target domain, whereas the cross-backbone transfer test keeps
the source router fixed. Replacing the router’s 39 interpretable
features with a graph network or a pretrained code encoder has
a largest observed improvement of 1.3 points among the tested
alternatives, with no significant pairwise improvement. This
does not establish equivalence or bound the benefit of other rep-
resentations.
Separately from the router, we argued that cost-aware meth-
ods cannot be compared without equalising what they spend,
and we gave a protocol that does so, along with the demonstra-
tion that motivated it: under a shared cost coefficient the six
methods we study spend anywhere between 30% and 52% of
the reference budget, and two of them change places with Dats
after calibration toward the common target; the residual spread
and unmatched methods must still be reported.
The additional controls show modest descriptive differences
between graph adjacencies. On the repeated 120-problem sub-
set, the frozen DATS policy averages 77.00% against 72.00%
for HRM. DATS also has the highest accuracy point estimate
at the three tested matched budgets.
Paired retraining with
repeated-label targets changes some routes while retaining the
subset-level advantage over HRM. These checks address dis-
tinct questions; the budget rankings remain descriptive and re-
stricted to tested operating points.
What remains open is the size of the catalogue and the mo-
ment of the decision. Both boundaries point in the same di-
rection: toward systems that treat collaboration as a budget to
allocate rather than an architecture to fix.
