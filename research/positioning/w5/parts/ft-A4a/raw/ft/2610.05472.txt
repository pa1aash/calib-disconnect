Reasoning errors can propagate into later decisions and memory. This survey synthesizes 312 papers and
first-party reports on text-based reasoning hallucinations around three questions: what evidence is observable,
what study designs establish, and which corrective actions the evidence supports. UIPCA records unsupported
premises (U), invalid inferences (I), dependent reuse (P), visible answer–trace consistency (C), and action-policy
failures (A). Across 58 reviewed sources, no comparison establishes that a specified intervention improves
reasoning while reducing factual reliability under matched conditions. The synthesis connects diagnosis to
verification, repair, selective release, and persistent-state control across memory, tools, and training feedback.

Introduction
The same wrong answer can arise from distinct failures. A reasoning model may start from an
unsupported premise, make an invalid inference, reuse an earlier error, or contradict its own visible
reasoning. Repair may require retrieving evidence, correcting a step, revisiting a dependent branch,
checking answer–trace consistency, or abstaining. Final-answer accuracy alone cannot select the
appropriate repair.
These errors can also affect later decisions. Agents may store generated content in private or
shared memory, act through tools, or retain feedback for later rounds [39, 151, 187]. An unsupported
claim can then become future context. Storage alone does not establish propagation: the return
path depends on whether the claim is reused before independent repair.

Existing taxonomies, monitoring methods, and interventions address different parts of this
problem. This survey connects three questions: what the evaluator can observe, what the study
design establishes, and which corrective action the evidence supports. Their separation can obscure
the difference between consistency and correctness, association and causation, or detection and a
justified release decision. Figure 1 maps the literature supporting this evidence-to-action view.
A correct answer can follow unsupported reasoning, while coherent deductions from a distorted
graph, mismatched image region, or invented API can yield a wrong answer. Correction creates
another distinction: accepting a local edit does not establish recovery of the final outcome [106].
These failures arise in several kinds of reasoning system. Chain-of-thought prompting asks
a model to generate intermediate steps; self-consistency samples several reasoning paths and
aggregates their answers [82, 169, 173, 201]. Reasoning post-training instead changes the model’s
parameters [51, 188]. Whatever the method, a provider may disclose a trace, a summary, or neither
[7, 79, 129, 185]. A fluent explanation is therefore something to check, not proof of how the answer
was obtained.

The review asks how to diagnose and repair failures (RQ1) and what current evidence establishes
about reasoning and factual reliability (RQ2). It then examines which detection and intervention
strategies different interfaces permit (RQ3), and how benchmarks and release policies should
evaluate consequential outputs and persistent state (RQ4). Sections 2–6 address these questions in
turn (Figure 2). S1–S7 provide the corpus, evidence reviews, coding rules, catalogs, and figure data.
Scope. This is a text-centered review of reasoning hallucination. Multimodal and code studies
are transfer cases, not co-equal domain surveys. They test which distinctions survive a change in
evidence type and where a domain-specific checker is required.
2
Scope, Evidence Model, and Review Method
2.1
Constructs and Evaluation Units
Here, large reasoning model means a system that allocates or supervises multi-step computation
beyond direct generation. This includes visible scratchpads, hidden effort modes, sampled search,
tool use, and reasoning-focused post-training. A displayed rationale is generated text, not necessarily
a transcript of hidden computation.
Training methods shape what these systems learn to produce. After pretraining [161], supervised
fine-tuning (SFT) learns from demonstrations, while reinforcement learning (RL) rewards selected
behavior. Feedback can concern a final answer or individual reasoning steps, and a verifier checks
proposed answers or steps [28, 94, 130, 146]. Distillation trains a model using another model’s
outputs. In practice, data, rewards, optimization, and the retention of reasoning steps often change
together [60, 104, 190, 191]. Labels such as RL-only or SFT+RL describe a training recipe, not a
single isolated cause.
In this review, hallucination means generated content that lacks support under the task’s stated
reference frame. Two familiar checks capture different aspects of support: factuality asks whether
a claim is correct, while source faithfulness asks whether the supplied evidence supports it [68,
117, 124]. For example, a true statement drawn from outside a designated document may still be
unsupported in a document-grounded answer.
Process faithfulness concerns the explanation rather than the source: does the displayed reasoning
reflect what actually determined the answer [84]? A coherent explanation can fail this test if the
model reached its answer for another reason. Checking the explanation’s statements and testing its
causal role are therefore separate tasks.
Confidence and correction add two further questions. A calibrated system’s stated confidence
agrees with its observed correctness; corrigibility concerns whether valid feedback repairs later
reasoning and the outcome [106]. Neither guarantees a good decision to act. A system may recognize
uncertainty yet release an answer that should have been verified or withheld. Table 2 separates
these evaluation targets so that success on one is not mistaken for success on all.
2.2
Boundary Between Hallucination and Deception
Hallucination and deception can produce the same false statement, but deception requires evidence
of goal-directed concealment or strategic misrepresentation [54, 149]. A false, persistent, or self-
contradictory answer does not establish intent. Incentive changes and tests of information access
are needed to support a strategic explanation [44, 67].
UIPCA records observable support, dependency, consistency, and action failures without inferring
mental state. The same record can arise from missing knowledge, invalid inference, reward pressure,
or concealment. A factually supported answer may have no 𝑈/𝐼/𝑃/𝐶failure and still violate a
declared release policy; 𝐴= 1 records that policy failure.

2.3
Observable Interfaces and Evidence Status
The three interfaces differ in what the evaluator can inspect. A full trace exposes ordered reasoning
steps; a summary discloses selected claims; a hidden interface exposes no reasoning record. In our
comparison, the prompt, designated evidence, answer, and observed action remain available. A
summary can assist answer checking, especially when the evaluator lacks the prompt [2], but it
cannot establish what happened in omitted steps.
Visibility can also change behavior. Disclosure affects abstention, and length-penalized training
can remove monitoring cues with little accuracy loss [98, 120]. Viewing the same run through dif-
ferent interfaces is thus distinct from changing how a model generates or compresses its reasoning.
A missing source has different meanings in different tasks. If an answer must be supported
by a supplied document, absence of the required support establishes a grounding failure. In an
open-world factual search, failing to find a source does not establish that the claim is false. We
therefore distinguish supported, unsupported, and unknown claims. Unknown means that the
available evidence cannot decide; it is not a favorable judgment. S4 gives the formal rules.

does one specified intervention improve reasoning capability while reducing factual reliability?

3
The Evidence-to-Action Lifecycle
UIPCA synthesizes diagnostic distinctions from the reviewed literature into a common record; its
five fields do not describe an underlying model mechanism.
UIPCA answers a repair question: once a run is suspect, which observable check can still improve
the decision? The 𝑈and 𝐼fields locate unsupported premises and invalid local inferences; 𝑃records
dependent reuse. The𝐶field compares the answer with the visible trace, while 𝐴audits the observed
action against a fixed policy. The fields therefore serve different diagnostic purposes. UIPCA is
neither a flat error taxonomy nor a claim that every run passes through five observable stages.
Factual, logical, arithmetic, perceptual, attributional, and executable errors remain separate type
labels.
3.1
Operational Definition
Consider a calculation based on a retrieved value. If the value is unsupported, the repair starts with
its source (𝑈); if the value is correct but the arithmetic is wrong, the repair starts with the inference
(𝐼). When later calculations reuse the error, correcting only its first occurrence leaves dependent
results to repair (𝑃). An error mentioned and then discarded does not create this propagation path.
The final answer adds two different checks. Does it agree with the visible reasoning (𝐶)? Was
releasing it allowed by the policy fixed for the task (𝐴)? A wrong answer may agree with a wrong
calculation, so consistency alone cannot establish correctness. Conversely, even a correct answer
can violate a requirement for independent verification before release. These distinctions connect
each diagnosis to a different repair.
In the figures and records, 1 means that the named failure is confirmed, 0 that it is ruled out,
and ⊥that evidence is insufficient. The first-error marker 𝜏★locates the earliest confirmed premise
or inference failure. It need not locate the actual first error if earlier checks are unresolved. S4
contains the complete coding manual, aggregation rules, and boundary cases.
Visibility limits this record. With hidden reasoning, an evaluator can still check the answer
against external evidence and inspect recorded tool actions, but cannot locate errors in unexposed
steps. A summary supports checks on what it discloses, not reconstruction of what it omits. Missing
process evidence remains unknown, rather than being inferred from confidence or agreement.
UIPCA is a diagnostic record, not an automatic detector. Human judgments, executable checks,
and learned detectors can supply its evidence; Section 5 compares their strengths and errors. The
running cases below show what the record adds to answer scoring, while Section 3.6 explains its
sensitivity to analytical choices.

Multimodal reasoning makes the evidence boundary explicit. Caption and visual-QA benchmarks
detect object hallucinations or image–answer disagreement [13, 73, 144], but do not by themselves

mapping, naming, resource, and logic errors. Program-state tasks isolate input–output reasoning,
while containers catch invented symbols or imports [41, 47, 160]. Span-level detection across code,
tool output, and documents shows why the evidence object must be recorded [205]. Even “tests

rankings [100]. Repository tasks also depend on issue interpretation, file selection, build state, and

3.4
Lifecycle Components and Their Repair Points
Premise entry (𝑈). A premise may be absent, stale, unsupported, or retrieved incorrectly. Short-
answer benchmarks show the outcome [74, 172]; cloze probes, knowledge-neuron analyses, retrieval
comparisons, and bounded-corpus audits test whether needed information is recoverable [34, 106,
113, 135]. These checks can motivate retrieval or source correction without revealing everything
learned in pretraining. Image and code analogues include wrong regions and invented APIs.
Local inference (𝐼). Proof audits and program-state tasks reveal invalid transitions despite
correct premises [47, 50]. But graph-coloring errors may originate in invented edges, followed by
coherent deductions from the wrong graph [56]. The distinction selects input repair versus rule
correction. Structured methods separate premise selection and inference [32]; hybrids add symbolic
solvers or rule checks [132, 137, 181].
Propagation (𝑃). Later claims must depend on a confirmed error; length or repetition alone
is insufficient [106, 188]. Independent evidence can stop reuse, whereas self-checks can repeat a
shared false premise [114, 123, 126]. In multi-round clinical evaluation, locally coherent rationales
sometimes omitted decisive evidence requests before commitment [14]. Repair should therefore
examine both acquisition and reuse.
Answer–trace consistency (𝐶). A trace can influence an answer or rationalize it, as cue,
mediation, perturbation, and decomposition studies show [8, 26, 29, 133, 139, 163]. Behavioral
detection is easier for correct than wrong answers [5]; causal-alignment tests instead intervene
on trace dependence [171]. The 𝐶field covers only visible contradiction, not causal attribution or
hidden computation.
Action (𝐴). Abstention and knowledge-boundary benchmarks reveal unjustified answers and
refusals [3, 75, 81]. Reflection training and act-or-abstain tests connect explanations with decisions

3.5
Applying the Lifecycle Without Overclaiming
A displayed dependency (𝑃) or contradiction (𝐶) need not describe hidden computation. Causal
attribution requires changing a premise or step and observing the answer [84, 164]. Textual diagnosis
remains useful without treating rationales as mechanism transcripts.
The useful unit depends on the repair. A sentence may be appropriate for checking a source, yet
contain several arithmetic operations that need separate checks. A token-level alarm can be precise
without identifying which dependent branch should be restarted. Finer localization is useful only
when it changes what can be repaired.
U/I/P/C/A records failure locations and relations, not severity. An unused unsupported premise
can be serious, while a long failed branch can be harmless if discarded before release. Consequence
and repair difficulty therefore remain separate from the number of recorded failures.
3.6
Operational Sensitivity: Segmentation, Dependencies, and Unknowns
A diagnostic record depends on how the trace is divided and which claims are treated as prerequisites.
Merging a correct operation with a faulty one broadens the reported first-error location. Treating
every neighboring step as dependent can also turn an isolated error into apparent propagation.
These are changes in the analysis, not necessarily changes in the model’s behavior.
Missing information creates a further limit. A trace can omit a prerequisite or present several
derivations for one claim. In such cases, not finding a dependency is not enough to rule out reuse.
The affected field remains unknown when the available evidence cannot decide. This is why a
complete negative check and an unobserved check have different meanings.
Two laboratory members independently annotated 48 full-trace cases, blind to one another’s
labels and upstream evaluation labels. With 334 shared source steps, pre-adjudication Cohen’s 𝜅
was 0.653 for 𝑈, 0.730 for 𝐼, and 0.858 for 𝑃. Case-level 𝐶agreement was complete (𝜅= 1.000; four
jointly positive cases). First-error location agreed in 41/48 cases; requiring the same origin type
as well gave 40/48, including joint no-origin judgments. All 𝐴labels were unknown because no
action policy was supplied. These estimates describe label agreement with fixed segmentation, not
validation of the candidate dependency graph. S4 reports case-clustered intervals, positive/negative
agreement, disagreement categories, and the status of edge review and adjudication.

reasoning to revisit, and a decision to reconsider. Which of these diagnoses is available depends on

them [192]. Its temporal-chain comparisons are correlational. They support separate reports of
factual access and composition, not a universal ranking of training methods.

resolving it [193]. Activation interventions offer ways to isolate the signal [37, 90, 162]. The decisive
outcome is changed verification or abstention at useful coverage, including under distribution shift.
4.4
Boundary Conditions for Synthesis
Four variables explain many apparent disagreements. Output detail changes how many claims
can be checked, so long- and short-answer rates are not directly comparable [20, 124]. Answer
willingness changes the answered subset and can produce non-monotonic budget effects [198].
Evaluator access limits available checks, while checkpoint and training changes may dominate the
named reasoning mode [188].
Within-checkpoint recall and between-checkpoint long-form comparisons test different links
[20, 45]. Tool interventions identify action failures [189]; observed trace groups suggest intervention
targets. Both are informative within scope. Results are excluded when the denominator, source
identity, or supported claim cannot be resolved.
Section 5 uses these distinctions to assess when a detection signal can guide verification, repair,
or a different release decision.

5.2
Intervention Families
Inference-time methods add computation, evidence, or earlier verification. Budget and length
controls alter latency [7, 21]. Extra deliberation can improve recall, but its effects on factuality
and willingness to answer vary across models [45, 75, 198]. Multi-agent debate adds paths that
may share source errors [39]. Answer rate and selective accuracy distinguish reliability gains from
increased refusal. Completion, path diversity, and cost help assess extra computation; trace length
alone establishes neither repair nor waste.
Independent evidence gives a disputed claim a stronger check than reconsideration alone. Veri-
fication, retrieval, and tool-assisted critique can offer such checks [46, 87, 92, 97, 187], although
weak retrieval or unavailable tools can introduce errors [189]. Comparing prevention, interruption,
rejection, and recovery therefore requires matched evidence and cost budgets.
Early verification can reject a faulty step before reuse. Decoding-time mode switches or verifica-
tion can interrupt generation [52], while HaluSearch couples detection with search [24]. Clinical
critique-and-regenerate methods instead operate on completed answers [93]. They can improve
what is released while leaving the original trace unrepaired. Both approaches require an independent
final check.
Process supervision moves checking into training. Instead of rewarding only the final answer, it
provides feedback on intermediate steps. Labels backed by source evidence can identify unsupported
premises (𝑈); rule or execution checks can identify invalid transitions (𝐼) [28, 94]. Detecting
propagation (𝑃) requires more: a later step must be shown to depend on a confirmed error. Knowing
that both steps are wrong does not establish that connection.
Process/outcome reward comparisons in mathematics and step verification for scientific tools
illustrate these differences [131, 199]. Factuality and faithfulness objectives target other units
[88, 127, 143]. A step reward can miss a false premise; a factuality verifier can miss an invalid
transition. Table 7 relates supervision to these faults.

5.4
A Calibrated Control Cascade
Hallucination control begins by splitting the answer and visible reasoning into claims, then checking
each claim against declared evidence. Claims are supported, unsupported, or unknown. When
visible information permits, unsupported claims are traced to their origin and receive component-
specific repair. Unknown claims go to retrieval, clarification, abstention, or escalation; they never
count as supported. Harm, reversibility, coverage, and cost guide action selection. The subsequent
𝐴audit checks policy compliance without changing evidence status.

6.2
Critical Benchmark Gaps
Three weaknesses recur across the benchmark catalog. First, outcome-only scoring hides the
path. One score can collapse origin, reuse, answer–trace consistency, and action. It cannot show
whether a correct answer relied on an unsupported trace or where a wrong answer began.

Second, many labels depend on an LLM judge. Judge setup can change agreement with
audited labels, and surface cues may replace the intended factuality test [140, 157]. FaithformBench
tests whether controlled valid and invalid inputs preserve their status [30]. HALLMARK shows
that false positives can dominate verifier deployment even when headline recall is high [142].
Errors may be correlated when generator and judge share a model family or training style. Their
agreement can then reflect a shared mistake rather than independent confirmation. A judge is useful
for routing review, but its reliability has to be checked against evidence outside that agreement.
Memory benchmarks make this separation especially important. HaluMem uses GPT-4o for
both memory-based answer generation and scoring, although dataset construction includes human
quality checks [17]. Agreement within that pipeline is not an independent factuality guarantee.
Judge updates create another ambiguity: a changed score may reflect a changed evaluator rather
than better memory. Stable reference cases and independent evidence are needed to distinguish the
two.
Third, action coverage is thin. Methods increasingly use signals to answer, stop, revise, or
abstain. A method demonstration is not a benchmark for the full policy. Existing tasks rarely cover
clarification, retrieval, tool verification, expert escalation, memory-write denial, or rollback. Shared
benchmarks provide the least coverage of action-policy evaluation (𝐴). Better benchmarks need
process labels, externally checked judges, and complete action and cost records.
6.3
A Lifecycle-Aware Construction Agenda
The missing benchmark is not simply a larger collection of wrong answers. It is a collection that
connects an error, the evidence available to detect it, and the action taken in response. Different
views of the same run can reveal how much diagnosis is lost when steps are summarized or hidden.
Controlled changes to premises or inferences can then test whether a proposed safeguard prevents
harm, rather than merely recognizing difficult cases.
Evaluation must also distinguish better answers from fewer answers [35, 80, 103]. Confidence
metrics address only part of that distinction. AUROC measures how well a score ranks cases, not
whether its numerical confidence is accurate. Brier score and expected calibration error (ECE)
assess probability predictions in different ways; S4 gives their definitions and limitations. Similarly,
evidence that a source supports a step is not evidence that the step influenced the answer [40, 70,
166].
VeriFY illustrates why metric definitions matter. Its model-relative precision is correctness
among released answers; recall compares correct answers with correct answers plus unnecessary
abstentions [3]. Recall thus depends on how the protocol establishes the base model’s knowledge,
not objective answerability alone. S4 gives the count definitions.
For an actionable benchmark, these measurements need to accompany the same cases. A useful
error location should help repair an answer or prevent an unsafe action, not merely improve a step-
ranking score. S5 supplies the detailed construction and reporting protocol, including escalation,
write denial, and rollback.
6.4
Interface-Aware Release Policy
A release policy decides whether to answer, gather evidence, revise, ask for clarification, abstain, or
escalate. It must balance avoiding errors with providing useful answers. Refusing everything avoids
releasing a wrong answer but provides no useful coverage; answering everything may exceed the
application’s tolerance for harm. The policy’s operating point depends on that tolerance and the
capacity for further review.
For illustration, releasing 6,000 of 10,000 requests with 30 wrong answers gives an observed
selective risk of 0.5% and coverage of 60%. These estimates meet a maximum risk of 0.6% and

minimum coverage of 55%, but not a maximum risk of 0.2%. Sampling uncertainty and changes in
operational data still matter. The thresholds should reflect tolerated harm and useful service, not
be chosen after evaluation to make a policy appear successful.
Interface visibility limits which evidence can inform that choice. Full traces allow process checks;
summaries allow checks only on disclosed claims. An unavailable process check cannot be assigned
zero error. If the risk and coverage requirements cannot both be met with the available evidence and
resources, the policy is infeasible. S4 gives the optimization template; S5 gives threshold-selection
and uncertainty procedures.
The same signal can justify different actions. A reversible writing error may warrant a warning;
a dose recommendation missing patient information may require clarification or clinical review
[81, 93]. Passing software tests may still leave security unchecked [71, 100]. Score the action taken:
expressed uncertainty is not protection unless it changes unsafe release.
Operational comparisons can otherwise reward extra refusal or unreported computation rather
than better control [21, 61, 174, 203]. The relevant outcome is what the complete system releases
or does, including the cost of obtaining its evidence.
6.5
From Released Answers to Persistent State
An answer can outlive its turn through memory, shared state, tool effects, or training feedback.
Text revision, action rollback, and parameter correction are different recovery operations. Each
raises the same question: what evidence is required before a claim persists beyond the current turn?
The return path in Figure 8 requires reuse before repair, not merely retention. Self-Refine
supplies self-feedback, Reflexion retains feedback across trials, and CRITIC adds tool-assisted
checks [46, 111, 151]; iteration alone establishes no repair. FARMA demonstrates the opposite
path: forged reasoning histories are stored and reintroduced [78]. This is the single-setting attack
evidence for H4.
Existing memory controls act at different points (Table 9). A-MAC decides which candidate
memories to admit, update, or reject using a weighted admission score [196]. Its factual-confidence
feature is ROUGE-L overlap with supporting conversation spans, so lexical similarity can influence
admission without establishing entailment. TrustMem instead evaluates the transition from old
to new memory: a frozen LLM checks coverage, preservation, and support, and its scores guide
memory-policy training [186]. This supplies update-level feedback, but support inherited from
old memory still needs scrutiny if that memory is already wrong. Admission and consolidation
therefore require different checks: whether an item should enter, and whether an edit preserves
valid information without adding unsupported claims.
Shared memory adds access and consistency requirements. Governed Memory combines typed
properties, entity-scoped retrieval, and organizational policy routing; its reported conflict tests
concern sequential writes, not concurrent writers [156]. MemClaw, evaluated through ArgusFleet,
attaches scope, supersession, and provenance to shared records [115]. Its evaluation reports a con-
crete interaction between safeguards: a synchronous near-duplicate gate can reject a contradictory
write before the asynchronous conflict detector receives it. A gate intended to prevent redundancy
can thus obstruct a legitimate correction. This is evidence about control ordering in one service,
not a general rate of memory hallucination.
The distinction changes what an 𝐴audit must inspect. With private memory, the record can focus
on one agent’s write decision and its required checks. With shared memory, it must also identify
the writer’s authority, the versions visible to readers, and the scope of correction or revocation.

hypothetical values, not observed rates. S4 gives the general definition, which also accommodates

6.6
Experiments That Close the Open Links
Three open questions follow from this synthesis. The first concerns the apparent trade-off between
reasoning and reliability. Extra computation may help when it includes verification, while abstention
rewards may improve reported accuracy chiefly by reducing coverage. The matched designs in
Section 2.5 can separate these explanations. Their purpose is to identify which intervention changes
which outcome, not to rank “more reasoning” as universally better or worse.
The second concerns the practical value of localization. Does knowing the error’s location
improve repair beyond a response-level warning? Comparing these signals on the same cases
would test UIPCA’s proposed diagnostic benefit more directly than agreement with a label alone.
Restricted interfaces are important here: a useful system must act on the evidence it can access,
without filling hidden fields with inferred certainty.
The third concerns transfer and recovery. A safeguard can succeed on one task because it has
access to decisive evidence that is unavailable elsewhere. Translation may change entities, negation,
Manuscript prepared for submission to ACM Computing Surveys

24
Yu et al.
units, or evidential strength [49, 159]; context position and visual grounding can change which
evidence is used [10, 99, 101, 109]. In code, a passing result remains conditional on requirements
and tests [41, 47, 71, 100]. Transfer therefore concerns the adequacy of the check, not just the
model’s score in a new domain.
Recovery also depends on timing. Correcting a premise before reuse and repairing a final answer
after reuse solve different problems. Local edit acceptance may leave the outcome unchanged [106],
and retrieval or tools may move the first error rather than remove it [92, 189]. Comparing early
checks, branch interruption, and later correction under common conditions would show where
a safeguard still prevents harm. Domain shifts, adversarial evidence, and unavailable tools test
whether that benefit survives beyond the original setting [3, 88, 103, 178]. S3 and S5 preserve the
experimental controls and statistical procedures for these questions.
RQ4 synthesis. Answer quality, visible process evidence, coverage, action, and cost require separate
measurements. Shared benchmarks still cover few clarification, escalation, persistent-write, and
rollback decisions. Policy evaluation must therefore specify a risk–coverage operating point and
extend beyond the displayed answer to consequential writes.
7
Conclusion
UIPCA separates unsupported premises and invalid inferences, dependent reuse, visible answer–
trace consistency, and action-policy failure. These are different diagnostic questions, not sequential
stages. Text-centered evidence and selected transfer cases show why repair should follow the
earliest useful observable check. Hidden process evidence remains unknown; the record does not
infer deceptive intent.
Pipeline differences and harmful traces do not establish that reasoning generally lowers factual
reliability. No source in the 34-entry S3 review or the 24-source external follow-up meets the
specified matched joint adverse-effect criterion. This bounded finding leaves the causal question
open. H1–H5 identify tests that separate the unresolved links while measuring action, coverage,
and cost.
Answer factuality, process support, confidence, and action need separate measurements. Their
joint evaluation distinguishes better evidence from increased refusal or computation. Consequential
outputs also require checks before they enter memory, shared state, tools, or training feedback.
Verification and provenance can support writes; unresolved content may need quarantine, and
harmful writes may need rollback. Reliability concerns both the current answer and the state
inherited by later rounds.
