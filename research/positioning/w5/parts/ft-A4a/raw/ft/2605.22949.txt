MARGIN: Runtime Confidence Calibration for Multi-Agent
Foundation Model Coordination
Joss Armstrong
Ericsson, Athlone, Ireland
joss.armstrong@ericsson.com

Abstract
When a coordinator compares answers from heterogeneous foundation models, self-reported
confidence may have different meanings across responders and changing workloads. This paper
presents MARGIN (Multi-Agent Runtime Grading via Incremental Normalisation), a runtime
calibration method that learns model-specific confidence corrections from observed answer out-
comes without retraining the models or requiring a held-out calibration set. MARGIN tracks
recent accuracy and stated confidence within confidence bands, uses their ratio to correct re-
ported confidence, and blends sparse-band corrections toward a model-level estimate.
The
corrected scores weight candidate answers in a collective decision. Evaluation covers code gen-
eration, question answering, and mathematics, using an 18-model pool and a nine-model subset
for distribution-shift experiments.
On BigCodeBench, model-mean confidence is negatively
related to accuracy; among correct/incorrect response pairs, choosing the more confident re-
sponder performs below chance.
Against five online calibration baselines receiving identical
feedback and retaining their learned state across each transition, MARGIN achieves lower post-
shift expected calibration error than all five in two code-generation transitions and than four
in a question-answering transition; the remaining question-answering comparison is inconclu-
sive. In separate code-generation coordination experiments, calibration improves the ranking of
correct responses and increases answer-selection accuracy by 4.3 and 14.0 percentage points on
two of three benchmarks relative to uncalibrated confidence weighting. These results support
model-specific runtime calibration for coordination under changing workloads when correctness
feedback is available for the participating responders.
Keywords: confidence calibration, multi-agent systems, foundation models, online learning,
distribution shift.
1
Introduction
A coordinator combining answers from heterogeneous foundation models must decide how much
weight to give each response. Self-reported confidence provides an immediate signal, but the same
numerical score can mean different things for different models. For example, a responder claiming
90% confidence may be less reliable than one claiming 70%. Language-model confidence is often
miscalibrated [11, 37]; when those scores are used to compare or aggregate answers, miscalibration
becomes a coordination problem rather than just an individual-model diagnostic.
Changing workloads introduce a second difficulty. A confidence correction describes the rela-
tionship between stated confidence and correctness on the tasks encountered. When the task dis-
tribution changes, a correction learned from earlier outcomes may no longer be appropriate. This

can happen without changing the models themselves: the questions change while their weights and
response policies remain fixed. A confidence-based coordinator therefore needs both model-specific
corrections and a way to revise them as new evidence arrives.
Fixed post-hoc methods, including temperature scaling, Platt scaling, and histogram binning,
fit corrections on calibration data [14, 27, 29]. Their performance can deteriorate under distribution
shift [28]. Online calibration instead updates from subsequent outcomes [7, 10]. MARGIN oper-
ates in this online setting alongside the histogram, windowed-accuracy, and online Platt methods
evaluated here. The relevant comparison is between correction rules that receive the same feedback
and must adapt calibration learned on an earlier workload to a changed task distribution.
MARGIN maintains exponentially weighted estimates of accuracy and stated confidence within
each model’s confidence bands. Their ratio discounts or increases reported confidence according to
observed reliability. Separate bands allow confidence-dependent corrections; within-model shrink-
age shares information when a band has few observations; and exponential weighting reduces the
influence of older outcomes. The calibrated scores then weight candidate answers in a collective
vote. The construction uses only model outputs, stated confidence, and subsequent correctness
feedback, without model internals, retraining, or a separate calibration dataset.
The evaluation separates adaptation after a workload change from calibration started without
prior history. For each shift, all online methods receive the same source observations and out-
comes, retain their state at the transition, and continue updating on the target workload. MAR-
GIN has lower post-shift expected calibration error (ECE) than all five online baselines in two
code-generation transitions and than four of five in a question-answering transition. The remaining
question-answering comparison is inconclusive. Mathematics and same-task test-hardening condi-
tions complete the shift evaluation. Single-benchmark experiments separately assess performance
without prior calibration history, while a selected-model-feedback diagnostic tests the dependence
on observing outcomes for the whole responding pool.
Separate coordination experiments establish the downstream relevance of calibration. MARGIN
improves the ranking of correct responses on the three evaluated code-generation benchmarks and
answer-selection accuracy on two. Previous evaluations have found that verbalized confidence can
poorly distinguish correct from incorrect answers, including below-chance discrimination in some
model–task settings [37]. Our BigCodeBench evaluation identifies confidence inversion across the
heterogeneous pool: model-mean confidence is negatively associated with accuracy, and among pairs
containing one correct and one incorrect response to the same task, choosing the more confident
responder performs below random choice before calibration (Section 7.3). The matched-feedback
shift experiments then assess how alternative online corrections adapt when the workload changes.
The contributions are: (i) a confidence-band runtime calibration mechanism for heterogeneous
model coordination; (ii) comparative evidence on adaptation after task-distribution changes, with
online alternatives given the same feedback and retained histories; and (iii) an empirical diagnosis
of confidence inversion on BigCodeBench, with evaluations of pairwise recovery, answer selection,
and feedback requirements. A stationary estimator analysis explains the bias introduced by asym-
metric success/failure updates. Calibration and coordination performance are established by the
experiments.

trajectory-dependent confidence are outside the evaluated setting.
3
Related Work
3.1
Calibration and Online Adaptation
Post-hoc calibration fits a mapping from prediction scores to empirical correctness. Temperature
scaling adjusts the sharpness of a model distribution, Platt scaling fits a logistic correction, and
histogram methods estimate correctness within score regions [14, 27, 29]. When these corrections
are fitted once and held fixed, their validity depends on the relationship between the calibration and
deployment distributions. Ovadia et al. [28] demonstrate the importance of evaluating uncertainty
under dataset shift rather than extrapolating from in-distribution calibration.
Large language model (LLM) calibration also addresses transfer and the cost of obtaining suit-
able supervision. Thermometer learns an auxiliary calibrator from multiple tasks for use on new
tasks [31]. ConfTuner fine-tunes verbalized confidence using a tokenized Brier-score objective [23].
Luo et al. [26] use a pre-trained reference model to calibrate a post-trained model without labelled
calibration data, restricting confidence alignment to examples where their predictions agree. These
approaches differ in their access and training requirements. MARGIN keeps the responding models
fixed and updates the coordinator’s correction from observed answer outcomes.
Sequential calibration studies probabilities that are evaluated and revised as outcomes arrive [7,
10]. Prediction with expert advice uses successive losses to allocate weight among predictors [3];
multicalibration requires calibration across specified, potentially overlapping groups [15]. These
address related but distinct objectives. MARGIN uses fixed model/confidence-band groups and
evaluates both their probability correction and its consequences for answer selection. Its estimator
theorem concerns update bias and makes no claim about regret or multicalibration.
Adaptive
conformal inference instead adapts a coverage criterion under shift [13], which is different from the
point-confidence objective here.
The direct empirical comparison is with online histogram, windowed-accuracy, and logistic
calibration rules specified in Section 6.2. They receive the same observations and retain their own
source state when the workload changes. This comparison assesses the combined ratio, banding, and
3

shrinkage construction against alternative update rules. Exponential weighting itself is established
estimation machinery [18, 30].
3.2
Confidence Signals and Model Selection
Confidence elicitation determines the signal available to a coordinator; calibration determines how
to interpret that signal against outcomes.
Tian et al. [33] study direct requests for verbalized
probabilities. Xiong et al. [37] compare confidence-elicitation strategies and report that vanilla
verbalized confidence can have poor failure-detection performance, including below-chance point
estimates in some settings. Their analysis ranks different answers produced by an individual model.
Our pairwise diagnostic compares responders answering the same task; a separate statistic measures
the association between models’ mean confidence and accuracy. The measurements are related
without being interchangeable.
Other approaches derive confidence from repeated responses. Self-consistency samples reasoning
paths and aggregates their answers [34]; semantic uncertainty groups meaning-equivalent responses
rather than treating every different string as a distinct answer [9, 21]. Surveys organise the wider
set of confidence-estimation and calibration approaches [11, 25]. MARGIN’s primary evaluation
uses verbalized confidence, while matched agreement-based signals are sensitivity evidence.
Its
runtime correction acts on the supplied signal rather than changing the elicitation procedure.
Confidence-based model selection and cost-aware cascades make different decisions about model
use. Gerych et al. [12] predict confidence for model–prompt pairs with an auxiliary regressor to
select a pair for each query. FrugalGPT learns cascades that trade answer quality against model-
query cost [4]. Here the candidate responses have already been obtained: MARGIN weights their
contributions to the final answer. The experiments assess this post-response coordination layer.
Savings from choosing which model to query are a separate question.
3.3
Multi-Agent Coordination and Trust
Foundation-model multi-agent systems employ debate, delegation, and structured conversation [8,
32, 36, 38]. In those settings, interaction can alter the answers that agents eventually provide. The
relationship between such systems and classical agent models is discussed by La Malfa et al. [22].
MARGIN studies the confidence-bearing interface between independent responders and a coordi-
nator. The respondents’ policies remain fixed; what changes is the coordinator’s interpretation of
their reported confidence.
Trust and reputation methods likewise use evidence about participants to guide subsequent
decisions. Jøsang et al. [19] survey their varied formulations and evidence sources. EigenTrust, for
example, aggregates transaction-based ratings into a global reputation value for each peer [20]. A
single participant-level score can hide differences between confidence regions: the same responder
may be reliable when reporting moderate confidence yet overstate its reliability at high confidence.
MARGIN tracks this particular context explicitly through model/confidence-band state. Its mul-
tiplicative factor describes how a reported score should be adjusted, rather than serving as an
unconditional ranking of agents.
This connects the method to outcome-based trust while keeping the evaluated information con-
tract concrete: candidate answers, stated confidence, and later correctness feedback. The selected-
model-feedback diagnostic tests what happens when the coordinator observes outcomes only for
the responder it chooses, thereby concentrating the evidence used for future calibration.

Method
MARGIN estimates how much a responder’s stated confidence should be corrected within each
confidence region. Two exponentially weighted moving averages (EWMAs) track observed accuracy
and stated confidence. Their ratio supplies the correction, within-model shrinkage stabilises sparsely
observed bands, and the corrected scores enter an answer-level vote.

4.5
Prediction and Outcome Events
Algorithm 1 separates prediction from feedback. At task arrival, every candidate is scored from
the currently available state, the answer is selected, and the confidence and band used are retained.
At outcome arrival, the supplied correctness label updates those stored states. A delayed outcome
therefore uses the confidence reported for its original task rather than a new response. Exact-code

Experimental Setup
6.1
Workloads, Cohorts, and Evaluation Design
The experiments ask two questions: how well does calibration adapt after the workload changes,
and how useful are the corrected scores for choosing an answer? The first is evaluated on source-
to-target transitions. The second is evaluated separately by replaying answer selection within a
benchmark from an initially empty calibration history, referred to below as cold start. A source
phase provides previous calibration experience. No method is tuned on it as a held-out dataset.
Code-generation tasks provide correctness labels through execution. HumanEval (HE) [5] and
Mostly Basic Python Problems (MBPP) [1] supply the two source workloads; the hard subset of
BigCodeBench (BCB) [39] is the coding target. HumanEval+ and MBPP+ use expanded tests from
EvalPlus [24] on the original candidate programs. These two hardening checks change the correct-
ness criterion for previously seen tasks; they are not independent unseen-question generalisation
tests.
The question-answering transition (MMLU-shift) moves from science, technology, engineering
and mathematics (STEM) questions in Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) [16] to
business questions in MMLU-Pro [35]. The latter introduces harder questions and up to ten answer
options; benchmark, subject, and format change together. Mathematics shifts from grade-school
problems in GSM8K [6] to competition problems in MATH [17]. Table 1 summarises retained task
support. Appendix B gives the model inventory, prompts, scoring support, and exact comparison
protocols.

6.2
Online Comparators
Raw stated confidence supplies an uncalibrated reference. The five online baselines each main-
tain per-model state and receive the same confidence/outcome observations as MARGIN. Sliding-
window histogram predicts the observed success frequency in the current confidence bin within the
model’s most recent W = 200 observations. Decayed histogram tracks success within each bin
using exponential decay 0.98. Both use ten equal-width bins, with raw confidence as the fallback
before the bin has observations.
Windowed accuracy in replace mode uses the model’s success rate over its last 200 observations
as the confidence score. In multiply mode it scales the current confidence by the ratio of windowed
accuracy to mean stated confidence. Online Platt scaling updates a per-model logistic map by
generation than the rest of the pool, and mixing generations would conflate model capability with model era.

stochastic gradient descent, with learning rate 0.1 and L2 coefficient 10−4. Appendix B.4 specifies
initialisation and updates, including the first-observation behaviour of decayed histogram. The
no-shrinkage MARGIN variant is reported separately as a method ablation.
These comparisons use the stated fixed configurations. No estimator was tuned to its optimum.
Post-hoc design-time methods motivate the problem but are not used to claim an advantage unique
to MARGIN. The coordination experiments use already-elicited answers from the pool; they neither
compare total inference cost against a single model nor establish superiority to the best model
selected in hindsight.

Adaptation after Distribution Shift

7.3
Confidence Inversion in the Heterogeneous Pool
BigCodeBench provides the clearest diagnosis of the raw coordination signal. Across the 18-model
pool, mean stated confidence is negatively correlated with observed pass rate (r = −0.692, 95%
interval [−0.759, −0.514]). Raw pairwise resolution is 42.37% with interval [38.99, 45.58]: among
pairs with one correct answer, choosing the more confident responder is worse than random on this
support.
The between-model correlation remains negative in the nine-cloud subset (r = −0.574, interval
[−0.775, −0.081]). Cloud-only pairwise resolution, however, is 45.73% with interval [40.07, 51.43], so
below-chance pairwise performance is not established for that subset. These are different statistics:
one relates model-level means, while the other compares responses to the same problem.
Nei-
ther identifies the relative causal contributions of model capability, task characteristics, or serving
configuration.
The calibration and selection improvements in Table 4 use the same retained support and do
not require attributing that raw failure to a particular cause. Appendix B.1 gives the exact cohort
identifiers.

Parameter Sensitivity
All main comparisons keep K = 3, α = 0.04, and ks = 100 fixed. Table 7 shows that α = 0.005
produces much higher ECE on the BCB transitions, while α = 0.08 improves on 0.04.
These
sensitivity results use historical-observation ordering rather than the primary problem ordering;
their matched 0.04 values should not be substituted into Table 3.
Shrinkage is likewise a design choice whose benefit depends on the condition. On the primary
problem-order shift grid, removing shrinkage yields inconclusive differences on the BCB, question-
answering, and mathematics transitions, and lower ECE on both same-task hardening checks.
Those six ablation rows accompany the online comparisons in Table 10. Table 13 in Appendix C.3
gives the complete retained parameter grid. The findings characterise the fixed configuration; they
do not select a new one or establish an optimal default for arbitrary workloads.
Band count matters little while the partition stays coarse. Between one and five bands, ECE on
the BCB, question-answering, and mathematics transitions changes by less than 0.3 points, and one
band is 0.05 points lower than three on HE→BCB. On the hardening checks, three bands are 0.54
points lower than one on MBPP→MBPP+, and the HE→HE+ difference is inconclusive. Ten and
twenty bands raise ECE by 2.9 to 6.9 points on the BCB transitions. The grid therefore supports
a small number of bands without singling out three.
Table 13 also compares symmetric and asymmetric success/failure update rates on HE→BCB
and MBPP→MBPP+.
Several asymmetric settings substantially increase ECE relative to the
fixed symmetric setting. These experiments complement Theorem 1’s stationary bias analysis by
evaluating outcome-dependent updates on the recorded workloads.

Discussion and Limitations
For a coordinator that uses confidence to compare answers, calibration history is useful only insofar
as it remains relevant to the workload. The shift experiments evaluate precisely that situation:
each method arrives at the new workload with estimates learned on earlier tasks. MARGIN’s lower
post-shift ECE against the tested online baselines in the coding transitions, and against four in the
MMLU-to-MMLU-Pro transition, supports its use as an adaptive correction in these settings. The
different cold-start ordering shows why calibration learned from an empty history should not be
treated as interchangeable with adapting an existing correction.
The MAS contribution lies in the coordinator’s interpretation of heterogeneous signals. MAR-
GIN assigns a model-specific meaning to a stated confidence and updates that interpretation from
outcomes. The BCB inversion demonstrates a directional failure of raw confidence, and the sep-
arate answer-selection experiments show that correction can affect the collective decision. Those
gains are relative to uncalibrated confidence weighting. They do not establish that an ensemble is
preferable to deploying a known strongest model at lower inference cost. This evidence supports
the evaluated coordinator/responder interface; it is not evidence about negotiation, interactive
deliberation, or autonomous multi-step agents.
The comparisons assess complete fixed configurations. They do not isolate a universal benefit of
band stratification, shrinkage, or a particular forgetting schedule. Theorem 1 explains an accuracy-
estimation bias under stationarity. The comparative adaptation result and the quality of the final
vote rest on the experiments. The cold-start comparisons and parameter ablations show where the
fixed MARGIN configuration is not preferable.
Correctness feedback is the main operational condition. The full-feedback setting is applica-
ble when candidate responses can be checked, as in the retained executable-test experiments. A
deployment that verifies only its chosen responder generates a selectively observed history. The
diagnostic shows substantial calibration damage in that setting. Exploration, periodic verification
of alternative responses, or off-policy adjustment are possible follow-on approaches, but are not
evaluated here.
Generalisation is limited by the archived cohort, retained task support, and abrupt workload
transitions. Test hardening reuses tasks; gradual or recurring drift is untested. Provider, model
capability, quantisation, and serving configuration were not varied independently, and per-request
context and termination telemetry are incomplete.
The study therefore cannot establish that
truncation did not occur or identify the serving causes of inversion. The bootstrap intervals quantify
variation conditional on the recorded histories and support, with no simultaneous or new-model-
population guarantee.

9
Conclusion
For coordinators that use self-reported confidence to compare model answers, both model hetero-
geneity and workload changes can undermine a common confidence scale. The BigCodeBench con-
fidence inversion demonstrates the resulting risk to coordination. MARGIN learns model-specific,
confidence-dependent corrections from observed outcomes while leaving the responding models un-
changed. Under matched feedback and retained source histories, it has lower post-shift calibration
error than all five online baselines in two coding transitions and than four in a question-answering
transition. Separate code-generation experiments show pairwise improvements on all three bench-
marks and answer-selection gains on two. Together, these results establish an empirically evaluated
runtime calibration mechanism for heterogeneous coordination under changing task distributions,
with its applicability conditioned on available correctness feedback.

B.3
Correctness Labels and Vote Grouping
Analysis consumes the archived extracted confidence score. Correctness requires a determinate
retained label, and calibration additionally requires valid confidence. Table 8 therefore distinguishes
the outcome and calibration denominators.
Unknown, quarantined, and unresolved labels are
excluded rather than converted to failure. Original request counts and complete serving telemetry
are not recoverable from the retained observations alone.
For code generation, benchmark evaluators determine correctness. Exact-code signatures serve
only to group votes. They normalise line endings and strip leading and trailing newline characters
while preserving internal whitespace. A valid alternative program can pass the evaluator without
matching another candidate’s signature. Empty programs form no vote group, and a task with no
usable group contributes zero selection success.
B.4
Comparator Definitions
All methods operate per model on confidence/outcome pairs. Formulas in this section use confidence
c ∈[0, 1]; the implementation’s equivalent external scale is 0–100. Predictions are made before the
corresponding outcomes are incorporated. The online methods carry their own state from source to
target without reset. The settings below are fixed configurations, chosen without a target-specific
hyperparameter search.
Sliding-window histogram.
Partition confidence into ten equal-width bins, with the last bin
including one. For each model, retain its last W = 200 observations. A new confidence in bin b is
replaced by hb/nb, the success count divided by the count of prior observations in that bin within
the model’s current window. An empty bin returns raw confidence. Every new model observation
enters the window, and the oldest is evicted when its size exceeds 200.
Decayed histogram.
Use the same ten fixed bins and a separate success estimate per model and
bin. Before the first observation in a bin, return raw confidence; the first update assigns its observed
outcome directly to the bin estimate. Subsequent updates use pb ←0.98pb +0.02o. Prediction uses
pb. Thus its decay, binning, and initialisation differ from MARGIN; it is not a controlled ablation
of the ratio alone.
Windowed accuracy, replace.
For each model, keep the last 200 confidence/outcome pairs.
Replace the new score by the window’s mean correctness ¯oW . With an empty window, return raw
confidence. The estimate does not condition on a confidence bin.
23

Windowed accuracy, multiply.
Use the same window but return clip(c¯oW /¯cW , 0, 1), where ¯cW
is its mean stated confidence. An empty window or nonpositive confidence mean uses the raw score.
This shares MARGIN’s multiplicative form but uses a single model-wide hard window rather than
confidence-band EWMAs.
Online Platt scaling.
Each model has p = σ(ac + b), initialised with a = 1, b = 0. After
observing o, update
a ←a −η[(p −o)c + λa],
b ←b −η[(p −o) + λb],
with η = 0.1 and λ = 10−4. This is a single gradient step on regularised log loss per observation,
and the full history is never refitted. Its initial prediction is σ(c) rather than identity.
MARGIN ablations.
The primary ratio implementation uses K = 3, α = 0.04, and ks =
100. State is initialised at confidence-band midpoints and predictions are clipped to [0, 1]. Both
denominators are safeguarded below by 10−12. The model-level shrinkage target is the ratio of
count-weighted band totals, as in the manuscript. With ks = 0, the effective correction is the band
ratio; all other settings remain unchanged. Asymmetric-rate variants use the specified rate after
success or failure for both EWMA states, while retaining the remaining construction.
B.5
Order, Aggregation, and Intervals
The primary problem protocol permutes target problem identifiers with seeds 1–100 and source
identifiers with seeds 10001–10100. Every method uses the same permutations. All usable scores
for a target problem are calculated before any outcome of that problem updates a calibrator. Model
ordering within the problem is the fixed sorted identifier order.
Historical-observation ordering instead permutes the archived source and target observation
lists.
Code-generation source/target seeds are 1–100/101–200; QA/mathematics seeds are 42–
141/142–241. Under full feedback, a prediction is followed by its outcome update before the next
observation is processed. A problem’s different model observations can therefore occur at different
positions in that history. Under selected feedback, the permuted target list is grouped by first
problem appearance, all available candidate scores for that problem are computed, and only the
stable argmax is updated. Equal-score ties follow the candidate order induced by that recorded
list. These historical conventions serve as sensitivity analyses, and the primary all-predict-before-
any-update protocol remains the reference. Parameter and delay grids use historical-observation
ordering.

The retained extended record also includes matched confidence-channel, transfer, and delay
analyses. They use distinct estimands from the main comparison. Agreement confidence is com-
puted from five additional archived sample signatures matching the original candidate; correctness
labels are not inputs to constructing that signal. Cross-channel comparisons retain the same origi-
nal candidates and restrict support to those with both signals. A larger inference cost alone does
not establish a scientific advantage for either channel.
The four supported transfer directions (HE to MBPP, MBPP to HE, and each source to BCB)
hold learned source factors fixed on the target and compare them with from-scratch target learning.
Those historical transfer summaries average per-model, per-history ECE rather than using the
pooled prequential ECE of the main tables. Order variation describes repeated arrangements of
the same observations and adds no new experiments.
These auxiliary results do not establish
