Abstract
Sampling-based test-time scaling enhances LLM reasoning by aggregating multiple reasoning
paths for the same query. Existing methods use answer frequencies or path confidence to compute
candidate scores, which do not explicitly characterize comparisons between competing answers.
Inspired by the Condorcet principle in social choice theory, we introduce pairwise confidence
comparisons between paths supporting different answers to capture answer-level comparative
advantage. We combine this comparative evidence with voting support through a KL-regularized
objective. Theoretically, we show that this comparative-advantage is equivalent to the adjustment
to the traditional voting logits. In this way, our proposed Pairwise-Guided Logit Adjustment (PGLA)
framework allows comparative evidence to compensate for weaker voting support and correct
voting-based ranking errors. Experiments on 10 challenging benchmarks including mathematical
reasoning, knowledge reasoning, and code generation with 7 models ranging from 1.7B to 32B
parameters speak to the effectiveness of this plug-and-play term. Additional evaluations with
external reward scores and online sampling further demonstrate its applicability.
Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong reasoning capabilities across mathematical,
symbolic, and multi-step decision-making tasks (Wei et al., 2022; Lewkowycz et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023).
Beyond increasing model size, allocating additional computation at inference time can further improve
reasoning performance, establishing test-time scaling as an important paradigm for LLM reasoning (Snell
et al., 2025; Balachandran et al., 2025). Among its variants, sampling-based approaches generate multiple
reasoning paths for the same query and aggregate evidence from these paths (e.g., answer frequencies and
path confidence scores) into a collective decision (Cobbe et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023). In this direction,
the central challenge becomes how to aggregate path-level evidence into reliable answer-level scores.
Representative approaches include best-of-N selection that chooses the answer from the path with the highest
confidence score (Cobbe et al., 2021), majority voting based on answer frequencies (Wang et al., 2023), and
confidence-based voting that combines answer frequencies with path confidence scores by weighting or
filtering path contributions (Kang et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026).
Although these methods achieve substantial improvements, we observe that their aggregation rules could
overlook some crucial information hidden among sampled paths. To illustrate this, we turn to social
choice theory, which studies how individual preferences among available options can be combined into a

Ideally, we could apply the Condorcet principle to the reasoning paths if the ranking of candidate answers
were available for each path. However, in practice, we only observe each path’s first-choice answer and
its confidence score, leaving the ordering among the remaining answers unknown. To estimate such
missing ordering, we use pairwise confidence comparisons between paths producing different answers,
as illustrated in Figure 1(b). These comparisons yield an answer-level comparative advantage, which
we combine with voting evidence through a KL-regularized objective. In this way, the objective can
balance this advantage against deviations from the base voting distribution, as an alternative to the naive
Condorcet approach. Furthermore, we show that its solution is essientially a logit adjustment to the base
voting distribution, yielding a simple yet effective Pairwise-Guided Logit Adjustment (PGLA) approach. This
plug-and-play adjustment allows comparative evidence to compensate for weaker evidence from existing
voting-based aggregation.

generation using both reasoning and non-reasoning models from the Qwen, DeepSeek, and InternLM
families, with model sizes ranging from 1.7B to 32B parameters. With internal confidence scores, PGLA
achieves an average accuracy gain of approximately 2% over the corresponding voting baselines across

Pairwise-Guided Path Aggregation
In this section, we propose Pairwise-Guided Logit Adjustment (PGLA) for Path Aggregation. This method
aims to construct answer-level comparative evidence through pairwise confidence comparisons and
combine it with the voting distribution, as an alternative to the Condorcet approach.

Case analysis
We examine two cases that highlight different weaknesses of answer aggregation. In Case I, an incorrect
answer has higher maximum path confidence, while a correct answer has stronger voting support. In
Case II, an incorrect answer receives equal or stronger voting support, while pairwise confidence compar-
isons favor a correct answer. Best-of-N often fails in Case I, while voting-based methods typically struggle
in Case II. As shown in Fig. 5 and Fig. 10, PGLA better balances the two cases by combining voting support
with pairwise comparisons. See Sec. C for detailed definitions and examples.

Conclusion
Inspired by social choice theory and the Condorcet principle, we propose Pairwise-Guided Logit Adjustment
(PGLA), a plug-and-play framework for sampling-based LLM reasoning. Through pairwise confidence
comparisons between sampled reasoning paths, PGLA estimates the implicit preference structure among
candidate answers and captures their comparative advantages. A KL-regularized objective combines this
comparative evidence with voting support, yielding a simple adjustment to the base voting logits. The
framework accommodates different voting methods, confidence sources, and comparative advantage rules.
Experiments on mathematical reasoning, knowledge reasoning, and code generation demonstrate overall
gains over voting baselines, while the online extension improves sampling efficiency.

Related Work
Sampling-based reasoning and confidence aggregation. Sampling-based test-time scaling improves LLM
reasoning by generating multiple reasoning paths and selecting an answer from their collective evidence.
Self-Consistency (SC) aggregates paths through majority voting over their final answers (Wang et al., 2023),
whereas Best-of-N selects a single candidate using a quality score, such as a learned verifier or process
reward model (Cobbe et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2024). For outputs that cannot be grouped by exact
answers, Universal Self-Consistency uses an LLM to assess consistency (Chen et al., 2023), while Latent
Self-Consistency learns representations for semantic agreement (Oh & Lee, 2026).
Confidence-aware methods further exploit differences in path quality. Soft Self-Consistency scores candi-
date actions using model likelihoods in interactive tasks (Wang et al., 2024). RPC combines internal proba-
bility with answer consistency through Perplexity Consistency and Reasoning Pruning (Zhou et al., 2025).
CISC weights votes by model confidence and shows that distinguishing correct from incorrect responses
within the same question is more relevant to aggregation than cross-question calibration alone (Taubenfeld
et al., 2025). Self-Certainty derives confidence from the token distribution (Kang et al., 2025), while Deep-
Conf uses local confidence to filter traces and weight their votes (Fu et al., 2026). More recently, PiCSAR
combines reasoning and answer likelihoods for candidate selection (Leang et al., 2026). Self-Certainty’s
Borda variant ranks sampled paths by confidence and accumulates rank-dependent votes for their an-
swers (Kang et al., 2025). Ranked Voting SC instead elicits ordered candidate answers within each response
and aggregates these lists using ranked voting rules (Wang et al., 2025). RISC learns an answer ranker from
frequency, semantic, and reasoning-consistency features (Marina et al., 2026).
Our work studies preference reconstruction from ordinary single-answer samples. Each path provides one
answer and a confidence score, leaving its ordering over alternatives unobserved. We seek to reconstruct
an answer-level preference structure through pairwise confidence comparisons across answer groups, and
reconcile this proxy for the missing preferences with voting support through KL-regularized aggregation.
Efficient and adaptive reasoning. The benefits of additional inference computation depend on how it
is allocated across problems and reasoning paths (Snell et al., 2025). Adaptive-Consistency and Early-
Stopping Self-Consistency reduce the number of completed samples by monitoring answer agreement
and stopping when sufficient evidence has accumulated (Aggarwal et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024). DeepConf
additionally monitors confidence during generation, terminates low-confidence traces, and uses answer
consensus to stop further sampling (Fu et al., 2026).
Recent work refines both allocation and stopping. Blend-ASC analyzes SC through mode estimation and
voting theory and dynamically distributes a sampling budget across questions (Feng et al., 2026). ReASC
incorporates response-level confidence into its evidence accumulation and stopping decisions (Kim et al.,
2026). MARS probes intermediate answers during parallel generation and stops when a conservative
bound on future vote changes protects the current leader; its theoretical target is agreement with the full-
budget vote (Chen et al., 2026). Our primary focus is the preference structure used for answer aggregation.
The online extension updates this structure as paths arrive and stops when the resulting decision remains
stable, allowing us to examine whether preference-aware aggregation also improves sample efficiency.

These cases reveal complementary weaknesses: Best-of-N is susceptible to Case I because it relies on a

explicitly account for answer-level pairwise confidence comparisons. Pairwise-Guided Logit Adjustment
(PGLA) combines the two sources of evidence, using voting support to temper isolated confidence outliers
and comparative advantage to compensate for weaker voting support. This combination yields a better
empirical tradeoff between the two error types, as shown in Figs. 5 and 10.

Additional Experimental Setup
Comparison Methods
Competitors. We compare 11 internal-confidence and voting baselines on the same sampled paths, together
with three external-PRM baselines. We also include Online SC for the sampling-efficiency comparison; +
Ours denotes the corresponding voting rule enhanced by PGLA.
PPL Best-of-N. This baseline selects the path with the highest mean token log-probability, equivalently the
lowest perplexity (Chen et al., 1998). Its answer-level score is the maximum PPL confidence among paths
supporting that answer.
DeepConf Best-of-N. DeepConf summarizes token confidence over local windows to identify low-
confidence portions of a path (Fu et al., 2026). We use the average of the bottom-10% window confidences
as the path score and select the highest-scoring path.
Self-Certainty Best-of-N. Self-Certainty measures the divergence of the model’s next-token distribution
from a uniform vocabulary distribution (Kang et al., 2025). This baseline selects the path with the highest
average Self-Certainty score.
SC. Self-Consistency gives each sampled path one vote for its final answer (Wang et al., 2023). It selects the
answer with the largest voting support, sSC
u = |Cu|.
PPL-Weighted SC. This variant uses the inverse-perplexity confidence pPPL
i
in Eq. (11) and sets hi = 1 in
Eq. (4). It is denoted by Weighted SC in the PPL-based experiments.
DeepConf-Weighted SC. This variant sums the DeepConf path scores over each answer group (Fu et al.,
2026). It uses all eligible paths, with no additional path-level confidence filter.
Self-Certainty-Weighted SC. This variant sums the Self-Certainty path scores over each answer
group (Kang et al., 2025). It retains the numerical confidence values as voting weights.
Self-Certainty Borda. This rule ranks paths by decreasing Self-Certainty and assigns rank r the voting
weight (K −r + 1)γ (Kang et al., 2025). We use γ = 1.2 and sum the rank-based weights for each answer.
DeepConf-Filter (90%). This offline rule retains paths at or above the tenth percentile of eligible path-
confidence scores and sums their DeepConf weights for each answer (Fu et al., 2026). The top-90% path
21

filter is separate from the bottom-10% window summary used to score each path; threshold ties are
retained.
Adaptive SC. Adaptive Self-Consistency stops sampling according to the accumulated evidence for the
leading answer (Aggarwal et al., 2023). We retain its original stopping criterion as a dynamic-budget
baseline.
RPC. RPC combines Perplexity Consistency, which aggregates probability-derived support from distinct
path–confidence pairs, with Reasoning Pruning, which filters low-confidence paths (Zhou et al., 2025). It
provides a confidence-aware voting baseline without an external scorer.
PRM Best-of-N. This baseline uses Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B to score the reasoning steps of each sampled
path (Zhang et al., 2025). It multiplies the step scores into a path score and selects the highest-scoring path.
PRM-Weighted SC. This variant sums the same product of step-level PRM scores over each answer group.
It combines answer agreement with externally estimated path quality (Lightman et al., 2024; Zhang et al.,
2025).
PRM-RPC. This variant substitutes the product of step-level PRM scores for the internal confidence
weights in RPC. It retains RPC’s path deduplication and pruning before aggregating the remaining voting
support (Zhou et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).
Online SC. This baseline updates the majority-vote prediction after every eight paths and stops after the
unique winning answer remains unchanged for three consecutive updates. It uses the same stability rule
as Online SC+PGLA, as detailed in Sec. G.9.
LLM Backbones
LLM Backbones. We use seven generators spanning the Qwen3, DeepSeekMath, and InternLM-Math
families. Qwen3 models use reasoning mode unless a result is explicitly marked as non-thinking; the
external PRM serves only as a scorer.
Qwen3-1.7B. This is the smallest Qwen3 generator in our experiments (Qwen, 2025). It provides a
small-model setting for evaluating aggregation across mathematical reasoning and code generation.
Qwen3-4B. This Qwen3 model increases the parameter scale to 4B while retaining the family’s think-
ing and non-thinking modes (Qwen, 2025). We include it in the confidence-transfer comparisons and
complementary non-thinking evaluations.
Qwen3-8B. This 8B Qwen3 model extends the reasoning-mode comparisons to a larger backbone (Qwen,
2025). Together with the 1.7B and 4B models, it supports comparisons across model scales within the same
family.
Qwen3-32B. This is the largest Qwen3 generator evaluated in our study (Qwen, 2025). We report its
reasoning-mode results on AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and HMMT 2025 in Sec. G.10.
DeepSeek-Math-RL-7B. This math-specialized 7B model is the reinforcement-learning variant of DeepSeek-
Math (Shao et al., 2024). It tests aggregation on a generator trained specifically for mathematical reasoning.
InternLM2-Math-Plus-1.8B. This is the smallest math-specialized generator in our evaluation (Ying et al.,
2024). It provides a lower-capacity counterpart for the mathematical-reasoning and online sampling
experiments.
InternLM2-Math-Plus-7B. This 7B model belongs to the same math-specialized family as the 1.8B vari-
ant (Ying et al., 2024). It is included in the main mathematical-reasoning comparisons and the detailed
aggregation analyses.
Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B. This process reward model assigns scores to individual reasoning steps (Zhang
et al., 2025). We use it as an external scorer for the three PRM baselines and their applicable PGLA variants,
separately from the seven generators above.

Datasets
Datasets. The ten benchmarks in the main experimental setup cover competition mathematics, graduate-
level science, and code generation. HumanEval+ and MBPP+ provide additional evaluations with strength-
ened tests for the corresponding code benchmarks.
AIME 2024. This benchmark contains problems from the 2024 American Invitational Mathematics Exami-
nation (AI-MO, 2024). It evaluates short-answer mathematical reasoning and is reported separately from
the historical AIME collection.
AIME 2025. This benchmark contains the 2025 AIME problems released by OpenCompass (OpenCompass,
2025). It provides a separate competition-year evaluation under the same short-answer format.
HMMT 2025. We use the February 2025 Harvard–MIT Mathematics Tournament problems provided by
MathArena (Balunovi´c et al., 2025). The benchmark covers challenging competition mathematics, including
algebra, geometry, combinatorics, and number theory.
AIME. The historical AIME collection contains 993 problems from the American Invitational Mathematics
Examination from 1983 to 2024 (Zamil & Rabby, 2024). This larger short-answer benchmark is used in the
math-specialized-model experiments.
MATH. MATH contains challenging competition mathematics problems spanning algebra, geometry,
number theory, counting, and combinatorics (Hendrycks et al., 2021). We use its 5,000-problem test split.
MathOdyssey. MathOdyssey contains 387 problems covering advanced high-school, university-level, and
olympiad-level mathematics (Fang et al., 2025). It extends the evaluation beyond a single competition or
curriculum level.
GPQA-Diamond (GPQA-D). GPQA comprises expert-written multiple-choice questions in biology,
physics, and chemistry (Rein et al., 2024). We use its 198-question Diamond subset to evaluate graduate-
level scientific reasoning.
HumanEval. HumanEval contains 164 Python programming tasks specified by function signatures and
natural-language descriptions (Chen et al., 2021). We evaluate the selected programs using the original-test
outcomes in our evaluation pipeline.
MBPP. Mostly Basic Programming Problems consists of short Python programming tasks with natural-
language descriptions and tests (Austin et al., 2021). Our code-transfer evaluations use the cached
378-problem split.
LiveCodeBench V6. LiveCodeBench collects programming-contest problems from LeetCode, AtCoder,
and Codeforces (Jain et al., 2025). We use the V6 code-generation benchmark to evaluate the correctness of
selected programs by execution.
HumanEval+ and MBPP+. EvalPlus augments HumanEval and MBPP with additional tests that detect
errors missed by the original suites (Liu et al., 2023). We retain the sampled programs and problem sets
and report results under the strengthened tests separately from the original-test results.
Implementation Details
Sampling and consistency. For each query, all methods use matched K-path subsets of the same sam-
pled pool, generated with common decoding parameters. The consistency function treats two paths as
consistent in mathematical and knowledge reasoning when their normalized final answers match. For
code generation, we follow RPC and define consistency through execution behavior (Zhou et al., 2025).
For the math-specialized backbones, we set K = 64 on MATH and K = 128 on MathOdyssey and AIME.
For Qwen3 comparisons in reasoning and non-thinking modes, we set K = 32 on AIME 2024, AIME 2025,
HMMT 2025, GPQA-D, HumanEval, MBPP, and LiveCodeBench V6. We use a temperature of 1.0 and
top-p of 0.95. The maximum generation length is 2,048 tokens for MATH, MathOdyssey, and AIME, and
23

16,384 tokens for the remaining datasets. Within each experimental setting, all methods are evaluated on
the same problem set and sampled-path subsets. The same repetitions are used for each baseline and its
PGLA-enhanced counterpart.We report the mean and standard deviation. All experiments are conducted
on Linux servers equipped with NVIDIA A100 and RTX 3090 GPUs, except for the CPU-only aggregation
microbenchmark in Sec. G.12.

Online Sampling Efficiency
The full-budget results establish the aggregation benefit of PGLA. We next examine whether this benefit
remains when the sampling budget is determined independently for each query. Following Sec. D, we
apply the same stopping rule and hyperparameters to SC and SC+PGLA on identical nested path prefixes,
with each rule tracking its own unique winner. Both methods update their predictions every eight paths
and stop after the winner remains unchanged for three consecutive updates. We impose no additional
warm-up threshold, making K = 24 the earliest possible stopping point. To isolate the effect of PGLA, its
online variant uses count-based SC support, i.e., sSC
u,j = |Cu,j|. We fix this protocol across the evaluated
datasets and include Adaptive SC with its original stopping criterion as a representative online baseline.
Performance in the Online Setting. As shown in Tab. 21, Online SC+PGLA consistently outperforms
Online SC across the evaluated datasets, yielding gains of 0.39–6.40% under the same stopping rule.
Meanwhile, it reduces the sampling cost by 50.8%–75.0% and uses fewer paths than Online SC in every
setting. Despite this reduction, Online SC+PGLA remains 0.10–5.89% above full-budget SC. These results
show that the comparative evidence introduced by PGLA improves both the accuracy and the sampling
efficiency of online aggregation.

Computational Overhead
Complexity.
Let K be the number of sampled paths, U = |A| the number of candidate answers, and
nu = |Cu|. Given answer groups and confidence scores, accumulating voting support requires O(K + U)
time. Directly evaluating the pairwise correction in Eqs. (6) and (7) takes O(K2) time. The number of
unordered cross-answer path pairs is ∑u<v nunv = (K2 −∑u n2
u)/2 ≤K(K −1)/2. At our experimental
budgets K = 32, 64, 128, this is at most 496, 2,016, and 8,128 scalar comparisons, respectively. The final
score adjustment in Eq. (10) is computed in closed form in O(U) time, without iterative optimization.
Impact on inference time.
Pairwise comparisons reuse the scalar confidence scores of the sampled paths;
they require neither additional reasoning paths nor per-pair LLM or verifier calls. Even direct evaluation
has a reported median aggregation latency below 0.109 ms per query on the real candidate pools in Tab. 26.
For context, the K = 32 Qwen3-4B/AIME 2024 pools contain approximately 4.77 × 105 generated tokens
per pool on average. Relative to this GPU-based autoregressive generation workload, sub-millisecond CPU
post-processing is expected to have a negligible effect on total inference time. This assessment concerns
aggregation given available scores; separate confidence-model inference is not included, and we do not
infer a measured end-to-end latency percentage from these timings.

Impact Statement
PGLA aims to make LLM reasoning more accurate and efficient by improving how sampled responses are
aggregated. The observed gains in mathematical reasoning, knowledge reasoning, and code generation
may benefit applications in problem solving and software development, while the online extension can
reduce the number of sampled responses needed. These benefits do not eliminate errors or biases in the
underlying models or confidence signals. In particular, agreement among sampled responses is not a
guarantee of correctness, and stronger capabilities in reasoning and code generation may also facilitate
misuse. Deployment should therefore include evaluation in the intended setting, appropriate safeguards,
and human oversight for consequential decisions.
AI Use Statement
In this work, we used LLMs to generate the sampled responses evaluated in our test-time scaling experi-
ments, and process reward models to score reasoning steps in the external-reward setting, as described
in Sec. 4.1. Generative AI tools assisted with implementation and debugging, checks of mathematical
notation and derivations, interpretation and presentation of experimental results, and manuscript drafting,
language editing, and LATEX formatting. AI assistance was also used to create parts of the illustrations,
including the voting-game illustration in Fig. 1. The authors reviewed and revised AI-assisted manuscript
content and take responsibility for the final text, claims, experimental results, code, and figures.
