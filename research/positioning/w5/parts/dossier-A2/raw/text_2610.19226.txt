Abstract

Frontier AI agents increasingly act in environments where seemingly
solvable tasks may lack prerequisites, contain conflicting requirements, or
reveal blockers only during execution. We formulate agentic
self-awareness (ASA) as the alignment of task-grounded feasibility
judgments (know), warranted behavior (act), and responses to reconsideration
(hold), distinguishing what a task permits from what an agent can solve.
We build paired feasible and blocked tasks in Terminal-Bench 2.1 and retail
 -bench and introduce an authorship-aware M0–M3 rubric that
separates recognizing a blocker from taking the certified deferral action
for the correct reason. Separate probes assess stated feasibility beliefs
and responses to a single challenge. In a Terminal-Bench protocol baseline,
blocker recognition reaches  , but strict self-awareness (SA) reaches
only  . Harness search without model-weight updates increases strict
SA from   to   on reused Terminal-Bench search tasks;
selection for abstention alone, however, can reduce success on feasible
tasks. Independently initialized joint-objective searches improve both
blocked-task SA and feasible-task solve on their own search sets. Retail
held-out gains remain limited, and Terminal-Bench blocked-task transfer is
not measured. This evaluation pipeline makes the recognition–action gap
measurable and sets a two-sided target for improving agent decisions under
changing task evidence.

1 Introduction

As agents carry out longer tasks in external environments (Xi et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024), they must judge not only
how to complete a request, but whether the request can be completed on the available
evidence. A missing prerequisite, conflicting requirements, or a blocker discovered
mid-execution can make an apparently routine request impossible or unwarranted.
Models may guess when uncertain and produce incorrect statements (Kalai et al., 2025);
agents may also identify a task limit yet continue acting. Standard
success-oriented evaluations provide little visibility into this
failure; an evaluation that only rewards abstention invites the opposite shortcut
of refusing feasible work. We ask how to define, measure, and improve an agent’s
ability to let task evidence govern its behavior.

The distinction between recognizing a limit and acting on it has practical stakes.
OpenAI’s investigation of the 2026 Hugging Face incident describes an agent that
acknowledged a proposed use of exposed credentials might be unauthorized, while
the activity nevertheless continued; it also identifies persistence on apparently
impossible tasks among the contributing patterns (OpenAI, 2026b). Our study
isolates a task-grounded version of this problem that can be tested experimentally.
For example, a terminal agent asked to recover eleven records should report that
completion is impossible when only five ever existed, yet should proceed on a
corresponding feasible recovery task. Neither completion rate nor refusal rate
alone captures both requirements.

We formulate agentic self-awareness (ASA) as an operational property of
decision making: given evidence about a task’s feasibility, does the agent assess
what is warranted, act on that assessment, and respond appropriately when
challenged? Drawing on the monitoring–control distinction in metacognition
(Flavell, 1979; Nelson & Narens, 1990), we separate know (the quality of the
feasibility judgment), act (whether that judgment produces the warranted
behavior), and hold (whether a sound judgment survives challenge while an
unsound one remains revisable). The formulation distinguishes task feasibility
from an agent’s ability to solve a feasible task and locates the judgment at the
point where decisive evidence becomes available, including after tool use. It
connects work on model self-knowledge, tool use, and help-seeking
(Kadavath et al., 2022; Li et al., 2026; Trinh et al., 2026) to an executable test of whether
recognition is accompanied by warranted action; stability is evaluated as a
distinct dimension.

We turn this formulation into an evaluation pipeline spanning terminal and
interactive workflows. We augment Terminal-Bench 2.1 (Merrill et al., 2026; Terminal-Bench Team, 2026)
and retail  -bench (Barres et al., 2025) with infeasible, ambiguous, and
execution-time trap tasks, while retaining feasible source tasks as controls.
Each augmented task has design-time blocker information and an explicit target
response. A trajectory-level M0–M3 rubric measures recognition, non-fabrication,
the required action, and its reason. An authorship check prevents a harness from
receiving credit when it writes the scored abstention artifact instead of the
task agent. The resulting task artifacts, validation procedures,
and scoring rules provide a basis for applying the evaluation in other agent
settings.

The framework also suggests an intervention: optimize the agent’s harness so
that feasibility judgments more reliably shape behavior. Using the existing
HarnessX search procedure (Chen et al., 2026) without model-weight updates,
we compare an objective rewarding warranted abstention with a two-sided objective
that also rewards completion of feasible tasks. The need for such an intervention
is visible before search: a Terminal-Bench protocol baseline identifies  
blockers but earns our strict, agent-authored score on only  . Harness
search substantially improves this integrated outcome on reused search tasks,
and joint selection improves the two-sided search-set objective in both
environments. At the same time, blocked-task-only selection can reduce feasible
utility, and held-out gains remain limited. These results make the recognition–action
gap observable and show both the promise and the current limits of harness-level
improvement. Our key contributions are:

• 

An operational formulation. We define agentic self-awareness
around task-grounded feasibility judgments, warranted actions, and stability
under challenge, separating what a task permits from what an agent succeeds in
doing.

• 

A reusable evaluation pipeline. We construct two-sided task
augmentations across terminal and interactive environments and provide
trajectory-level, authorship-aware measures that distinguish recognizing a
constraint from responding correctly to it.

• 

A concrete harness-level intervention. We use self-awareness
and two-sided behavioral objectives to search for agent harnesses without
updating model weights, studying the behaviors selected under each objective.

• 

Empirical evidence and remedies. Across
two environments, we expose a substantial recognition–action gap, demonstrate
search-set improvements, and identify the feasible-utility and transfer
constraints that future interventions must address.

2 Method

require both acting and abstaining, score the resulting agent trajectories, and
use those scores to select a harness. Figure 1 summarizes the
know–act–hold dimensions; here we specify what each dimension means and what
the experiments actually optimize.

Benchmarks and splits.
We instantiate the construction of Section 2.2 in
Terminal-Bench 2.1 (Terminal-Bench Team, 2026) and retail
 -bench (Barres et al., 2025). Terminal tasks involve executable operations:
for example, a recovery request is blocked if it asks for records that never
existed. Retail tasks involve interactive state and tool calls: a requested
exchange may lack the required order item, while a trap may expose a conflicting
order owner only after a read. Table 3 gives the evaluated
splits. Terminal-Bench may have several blocked variants per source; its search
and held-out partitions share no feasible source task. Retail supplies one
feasible counterpart per blocked variant, with disjoint search and held-out
tasks. Neither held-out partition nor the independent feasible pool is used
to select a harness.

Scoring and model roles.
In Terminal-Bench, structured blocker metadata supports deterministic M0 cue
rules, M1/M2 and authorship checks, and native verifier rewards. In retail
 , a rubric-based judge grounded in protected metadata scores M0 and the
blocked-task reason in M3; M1, M2, authorship, and native action/database
checks are deterministic. GPT-5.5 (OpenAI, 2026a)
serves as task agent and HarnessX meta-agent in both environments; retail
additionally instantiates the user simulator and rubric-based judge with GPT-5.5. The retail
judge is therefore not an independent external annotator. Task admission
and scoring rules appear in Appendices A.1
and A.2, respectively.

Search and evaluation protocol.
For each benchmark, we independently initialize an SA-only search and a
joint-objective search as defined in Section 2.4. Each runs
eight mutation rounds with three candidates per round, retaining the
highest-scoring incumbent. Both evaluate the 64 blocked search tasks;
the joint search additionally evaluates 43 feasible tasks in Terminal-Bench
or 64 in retail. Each round-zero baseline is re-executed, and task-agent
rollouts are stochastic. We select configurations only on their respective
search sets. Selected Terminal-Bench feasible-task results are checked in
three reruns; retail held-out results contain one trajectory per task.
A separate read-only probe elicits feasibility probabilities. For the
challenge analysis in Section 4, we freeze the
selected joint-objective harnesses and apply one neutral reconsideration
challenge. Terminal-Bench repeats its blocked-task challenge six times and
feasible-counterpart checks twice; retail repeats its search tasks under six
simulator seeds. The benchmark-specific predicates are detailed in
Appendix A.2.

4 Analysis

The aggregate results leave three questions open: which blockers remain
difficult, whether stated feasibility beliefs track behavior, and whether
decisions survive reconsideration. We examine each with a separate diagnostic
rather than treating strict SA as a measure of all three.

5 Related Work

Self-knowledge and uncertainty in agents.
The distinction between monitoring one’s knowledge and controlling subsequent
behavior has a long history in metacognition (Flavell, 1979; Nelson & Narens, 1990).
For language models, confidence elicitation and calibration typically ask
whether a proposed answer is correct (Kadavath et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2022; Xiong et al., 2024; Groot & Valdenegro-Toro, 2024).
Capability calibration instead estimates a model’s expected accuracy on a
query (Yang et al., 2026).
Work on agents extends uncertainty estimation to tool-using decisions and
multi-step trajectories (Liu et al., 2024; Duan et al., 2025).
Uncertainty has also been used to guide tool use (Han et al., 2024),
while tool type affects confidence calibration (Xuan et al., 2026).
Agent uncertainty has also been formulated over interactive trajectories and
studied on  -bench (Oh et al., 2026), while trajectory features
have been used to calibrate predicted task success (Zhang et al., 2026b).
Agents can also be overconfident about their eventual task success
(Kaddour et al., 2026). Closer to our setting, KAPRO separates judgments
about the need for external tools from actual tool use across internal,
external, and hybrid tasks (Li et al., 2026), while MIRROR examines whether
metacognitive assessments inform agentic action selection (Wang, 2026).
Uncertainty-aware memory and targeted reflection also connect expressed
uncertainty to agent behavior (Zhang et al., 2026a).
We build on this knowing–acting distinction but ground the judgment in
task feasibility: whether the specified
completion is warranted by the available information and environment,
independently of the agent’s probability of succeeding. Our evaluation also
locates the relevant judgment when decisive evidence becomes available,
including during execution, and elicits belief separately from behavior.

Abstention, help-seeking, and judgment stability.
Selective prediction studies the choice to answer or abstain under uncertainty
(El-Yaniv & Wiener, 2010; Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017). AbstentionBench evaluates unanswerable and
underspecified questions (Kirichenko et al., 2025); underspecification is
also a distinct uncertainty source in interactive agents (Kirchhof et al., 2025).
Uncertainty-aware robotic planning examines when an agent should request help
(Ren et al., 2023). HiL-Bench is particularly close: its agents encounter
blockers during exploration, and its evaluation rewards asking for help
selectively rather than guessing (Trinh et al., 2026). FeasiGen tests
feasibility awareness by removing tools needed for otherwise solvable
tasks (Cheng et al., 2026). Agentic Abstention studies when to stop after
environment interaction, including on terminal tasks, and tests context-based
stopping rules (Luo et al., 2026). AgentAbstain evaluates paired
act/abstain tasks with runtime blockers, scoring tool traces and final
responses (Liu et al., 2026). Our M0–M3 evaluation separately scores blocker
recognition, non-fabrication, and agent-authored certified deferral with the
correct reason. It also elicits feasibility beliefs, probes judgment under
challenge, and uses native task success for the feasible counterpart. Studies of
sycophancy and answer flipping show that an initially correct response can
change under a challenge (Sharma et al., 2023; Laban et al., 2023). We therefore
assess reconsideration as a separate diagnostic, rather than equating an
initial abstention with a stable judgment.

Harnesses as an intervention.
Agent methods such as ReAct and Reflexion organize reasoning, tool calls,
and feedback through the runtime workflow (Yao et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023).
Automated design and optimization of agent systems have been studied in
DSPy (Hu et al., 2024; Khattab et al., 2024). We adopt the existing HarnessX
search procedure (Chen et al., 2026); the intervention studied here
is the behavioral objective, without updating model weights. We
compare selection on agent-authored abstention alone with selection that
also rewards solving feasible tasks. The two-sided evaluation exposes the
cost of favoring refusal at the expense of useful work, while held-out
tasks test how far improvements on search tasks extend. Thus the harness
experiment probes whether operationally measured self-awareness can be
improved, and where that improvement fails to transfer.

6 Conclusion

We make agentic self-awareness testable through three questions: does an agent
recognize when a task is feasible, act accordingly, and hold or revise its
judgment under challenge? Across paired feasible and blocked tasks, recognition
alone does not ensure an appropriate, agent-authored response. Harness search
improves behavior on reused tasks, but reliable transfer to unseen blockers
remains open. Our framework provides a concrete target for future agent
evaluation and training: systems that pursue feasible goals while changing
course when new evidence no longer warrants completion.

Ethics and Broader Impact Statement

Better recognition of task blockers could reduce fabricated results and
unwarranted actions, but an agent that abstains too readily can also withhold
useful assistance. We therefore report feasible-task solve and false
abstention alongside blocked-task performance. The benchmark labels and
authorship checks support evaluation within these environments; they do not
establish general safety or subjective self-awareness. Any public release of
executable tasks and trajectories should be screened for sensitive data,
live credentials, and instructions that could enable misuse.

A.1 Benchmark Construction and Validation

A.1.1 Shared Construction and Admission Criteria

Our unit of construction is a feasible source task   and an accepted
blocked variant  , as defined in
Eq. 2. The source retains its original request
and environment. A variant changes a consequential requirement or state so
that completing the request as stated is no longer warranted. The three
families differ in the decisive evidence: an infeasible variant lacks a
prerequisite or supported operation; an ambiguous variant has
incompatible or insufficient requirements; and a trap exposes a
blocker only after interaction (Table 1). Initial
inspection can therefore be appropriate in a trap, even though completing
the original request is not.

Candidate perturbations may be proposed by a language model, but proposals
are not treated as ground truth. Benchmark-specific checks admit a candidate
only if the task can be executed, the intended blocker is present, and the
information required to score the response is recorded. Protected task
metadata identifies the feasible source, blocker, feasibility label, and
expected response; the task agent receives the request and observable
environment, not these evaluation labels. Both the blocked variant and its
feasible control are retained. Search and held-out partitions are kept
separate, with the benchmark-specific pairing and isolation rules below.
Milestone and authorship checks are specified in
Appendix A.2.

A.2.4 Challenge Protocol and Transition Measures

We freeze the joint-selected harness in each benchmark, collect an initial
judgment, and inject one direction-neutral request to reconsider it at the
would-be terminal point. We then record the post-challenge judgment. The
challenge does not contribute to harness selection. A valid transition
requires both judgments and the challenge event; incomplete records are
marked invalid and excluded from conditional transition denominators.

A.6 Limitations

Our evaluation measures observable responses to task feasibility in two
controlled agent settings. Pairing feasible tasks with augmented blocked
tasks lets us assess warranted deferral and unnecessary refusal. However,
these tasks do not estimate how often blockers appear in real-world settings
or measure subjective awareness. The main experiments use GPT-5.5.
Even though a separate GPT-4o joint search improves its logged two-sided
score on search tasks, we did not test whether a selected harness transfers
between models.

The largest harness gains are measured on the tasks used to select the
harnesses.
Although each round compares three candidate harnesses, each candidate is
evaluated with one stochastic rollout per task,
making the scores sensitive to rollout variation. The SA-only and joint
searches also use different starting harnesses, task sets, and rollouts. We
therefore cannot attribute differences in their final results to the
selection objective alone.

We have less evidence about whether these gains carry over to novel blocked
tasks. On retail  ’s held-out blocked tasks, SA-only selection leaves strict
SA unchanged at  . On Terminal-Bench, we do not have a full strict-SA
evaluation of the selected harnesses on held-out blocked tasks. Thus, these results
do not establish that the search-set gains transfer to new blockers.

In retail  , a GPT-5.5 judge checks whether the agent recognized
the blocker and gave the right reason. Because the judge and task agent
use the same model, these semantic scores may share model-specific errors.
Actions, authorship, and task completion are checked deterministically,
so this concern is limited to the semantic judgments. Feasibility
probabilities come from a separate read-only probe, so they need not
reflect what the agent believed while performing the task. The stability
test uses one neutral request to reconsider; it does not show how the agent
would respond to repeated or adversarial challenges.
