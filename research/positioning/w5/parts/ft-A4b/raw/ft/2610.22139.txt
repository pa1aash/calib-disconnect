 Abstract 
 Confidence scores determine whether an LLM answer is accepted, reviewed, or
regenerated, yet scalar recalibration can only correct errors that are visible
through the score itself. We introduce RADAR 
( R epair, A dopt, and D iagnose A fter
 R ecalibration), a reliability framework that separates confidence
repair from deciding whether the repair should be deployed and auditing the
errors that remain. Under squared loss, we decompose prediction risk into
outcome noise, information hidden by the score, and mapping error; restricting
repair to order-preserving maps additionally introduces a pooling gap.
For repair , we propose Probe-Anchored Isotonic Calibration (PAIC),
which learns a monotone map from correctness-probe predictions on unlabeled
target prompts. We show that PAIC’s excess risk depends on the probe’s
score-conditional bias and fitting error, rather than on its overall prediction
accuracy. For adoption , we derive a paired procedure with finite-sample
control of false improvements. For auditing , we develop a
prompt-clustered kernel test that detects feature-aligned residual errors while
accounting for dependent answers from the same prompt. Across multiple LLM
checkpoints, benchmarks, and distribution shifts, we find that richer
predictors can improve confidence estimation, but projecting their information
back to a scalar score can lose these gains, and lower prediction risk does not
necessarily yield better review decisions. Across 47,400 replayed raw-score allocations, paired betting adopts candidates with lower held-out test risk in 42.95% of allocations, compared with 15.09% for one-sided empirical Bernstein. Together, RADAR provides a principled workflow to repair what a scalar confidence score can express, adopt a repair only when supported by evidence, and diagnose the systematic errors that recalibration leaves behind. Code and data will be released upon acceptance. 
 1 Introduction 
 A confidence score can be calibrated and still fail predictably. 
 LLM confidence often determines whether an answer is accepted, reviewed, or
regenerated. A standard remedy is scalar recalibration , which learns a
transformation of confidence using methods such as temperature scaling, Platt
scaling, or isotonic regression
 ( Zadrozny and Elkan, 2002 ; Guo et al., 2017 ) . Yet scalar recalibration has two
fundamental limitations: it typically requires labeled data from the deployment
population, which may be unavailable under distribution shift, and it can
change score values but cannot recover information absent from the score. 
 For example, suppose every answer receives confidence 0.8 0.8 , 80 % 80\% are
correct, and the remaining 20 % 20\% systematically confuse minutes with hours.
Expected calibration error is zero
 ( Pakdaman Naeini et al., 2015 ; Guo et al., 2017 ) , yet the failure remains
perfectly predictable. Even correcting an original confidence of 0.9 0.9 to
 0.8 0.8 reduces squared loss without revealing this subgroup. Thus,
 correcting a score and recovering distinctions hidden by that score are
different problems . 
 RADAR: Repair, Adopt, and Diagnose After Recalibration. 
 We formalize this distinction through RADAR 
( R epair, A dopt, and D iagnose
 A fter R ecalibration), illustrated in
Figure  1 . Under squared loss, the risk of a scalar-repaired
score decomposes into outcome noise, information hidden by the score, and
mapping error; only the last term can be corrected by scalar recalibration.
Restricting the repair to preserve score ordering can introduce an additional
pooling gap. This decomposition motivates three distinct questions:
 how should a score be repaired without target labels, when should the
repair be adopted, and what systematic errors remain afterward? 
 1. Repair the score

prompts (PAIC, § 3.2 ). 
 Matters: the probe’s bias at each score.
 2. Adopt
Compare paired losses on labeled prompts; adopt only a certified
improvement (§ 3.3 ). 
 Evidence : paired labeled losses; optional probe assistance. 
 3. Diagnose after Recalibration
Test feature-aligned mean residuals, with prompts as sampling units
(§ 3.4 ). 
 Targets: residual error visible to the chosen features

population ⋅ \;\cdot\; prompt-disjoint roles

 Our approach. 
 For Repair , we introduce Probe-Anchored Isotonic Calibration
(PAIC) , which distills an independently trained correctness probe into a
monotone function of the original confidence using unlabeled target prompts. PAIC requires no target labels to fit, but held-out target labels test whether the fitted repair lowers target risk.
We show that PAIC’s excess risk is controlled by the probe’s
 score-conditional bias and its fitting error, rather than by overall
probe accuracy. 
 For Adopt , we show that agreement with an imperfect probe can
misorder candidate confidence scores. We derive a paired risk comparison using
held-out labels and a betting-based procedure that controls the probability of
adopting a candidate that does not lower squared risk. 
 For Diagnose , we develop a feature-aware residual audit. Because
multiple answers generated for the same prompt may be dependent, the audit
uses cross-prompt kernel products and a prompt-level multiplier procedure to
test systematic residual structure without treating sibling answers as
independent. 
 Contributions. 
 Our contributions are: 
 1. 
 A unified framework for confidence repair, adoption, and residual diagnosis : RADAR specifies separate data requirements and statistical guarantees for fitting a confidence repair, comparing candidate risks, and auditing residual errors. PAIC implements its repair stage using probe predictions on unlabeled target prompts. 
 2. 
 Risk guarantees for repair and adoption. 
PAIC performs target-label-free monotone repair with excess risk bounded by
 ( ‖ b n ‖ 2 + u n ) 2 (\|b_{n}\|_{2}+\sqrt{u_{n}})^{2} 
(Theorem  3.2 ), while our paired adoption rule controls the
probability of deploying a non-improving candidate
(Proposition  3.3 ). 
 3. 
 Residual diagnosis beyond scalar calibration. 
We introduce a prompt-clustered kernel audit that detects
feature-aligned residual errors while remaining valid under dependent
answers from the same prompt
(Theorem  3.4 ). 
 Our experiments show that scalar recalibration can improve risk under some shifts, lose information relative to richer predictors in others, and fail to improve downstream decision utility even when prediction risk decreases. RADAR is designed to distinguish these regimes rather than assume that improved calibration alone is sufficient for deployment. 
 More broadly, this work aims to make AI systems more trustworthy not by making them answer more questions correctly, but by repairing their confidence when we can—and identifying when that confidence still should not be trusted. 
 2 Related Work 
 Grouping loss and monotone repair. 
 Forecast decompositions separate reliability from resolution
 ( DeGroot and Fienberg, 1983 ; Bröcker, 2009 ) . Grouping loss measures predictive information
hidden by a score, and non-injective recalibration can increase it
 ( Perez-Lebel et al., 2023 ) ; our I I is grouping loss under squared loss,
and isotonic calibration ( Zadrozny and Elkan, 2002 ; Dimitriadis et al., 2021 ) is our
monotone benchmark. Multiaccuracy, multicalibration, and semantic grouping correct
with features ( Kim et al., 2018 ; Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018 ; Yang et al., 2023 ) .
For LLMs, Chen et al. (2024) fit label-based isotonic maps within
learned groups, so serving needs group features, and
 Melo et al. (2026) estimate grouping loss for local audits and
deferral. PAIC instead serves a single map of the score
(Appendix  A.10 ). 
 Unlabeled adaptation and distillation. 
 Under covariate shift, importance weighting reweights labeled source data
toward the target ( Shimodaira, 2000 ) , including for calibration
 ( Park et al., 2020 ; Wang et al., 2020 ) ; label-shift methods
re-estimate class priors ( Lipton et al., 2018 ; Alexandari et al., 2020 ) ; and
ATC and DoC predict target accuracy from unlabeled data
 ( Garg et al., 2022 ; Guillory et al., 2021 ) . PAIC takes the complementary
outcome-model route: instead of a density ratio over high-dimensional features,
it transfers a probe’s predictions on unlabeled target prompts into the map. It
is distillation with a student restricted to the score
 ( Hinton et al., 2015 ) , akin to learning with privileged information
 ( Lopez-Paz et al., 2016 ) ; our contribution is a guarantee identifying which
teacher errors matter. 
 Evidence for adopting a change. 
 Prediction-powered inference (PPI) corrects model-based estimates with labeled
residuals ( Angelopoulos et al., 2023 ; Angelopoulos et al., 2024 ) ; we apply it
to paired squared-loss contrasts between frozen candidates. Our adoption rule
tests bounded means by betting ( Waudby-Smith and Ramdas, 2024 ) , and
empirical-Bernstein bounds ( Maurer and Pontil, 2009 ) give simultaneous
intervals (Appendix  A.6 ). 
 Kernel calibration tests. 
 MMCE and the squared kernel calibration error (SKCE) embed residuals in a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
 ( Kumar et al., 2018 ; Widmann et al., 2019 ; Marx et al., 2023 ) . Their
estimators treat observations as independent; we handle answers clustered by
prompt, with multipliers in the spirit of wild-bootstrap kernel tests
 ( Chwialkowski et al., 2014 ) . 
 LLM confidence. 
 Confidence can come from token likelihoods ( Jiang et al., 2021 ) ,
self-evaluation such as P(True) ( Kadavath et al., 2022 ) , verbalized
statements ( Lin et al., 2022 ; Xiong et al., 2024 ) , agreement across
samples ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Kuhn et al., 2023 ; Farquhar et al., 2024 ) ,
or probes of internal states ( Orgad et al., 2025 ) . Our methods accept any
bounded scalar score; we evaluate likelihood-based and fixed-choice scores.
 Nakkiran et al. (2026) relate semantic calibration to local loss
optimality; we instead repair and audit scores for fixed realized answers. 
 See Appendix  D for a detailed and extended related work.

 When must a correction use only the score? 
 We study deployments in which the original score P P is available for each answer, while richer probe inputs or computation are available during offline adaptation. PAIC serves a fitted map of P P and preserves its ordering up to ties. When probe inputs are available at serving time, we include the direct probe in the candidate set (Table  1 ).
A scalar map is the right object when deployment fixes the score’s interface
while richer signals are available offline. Interface constraints: a
threshold, triage queue, or API field consumes one score, and an
order-preserving map corrects its values while keeping its validated ranking,
up to ties. Fitting-time signals: an expensive teacher, such as a larger
verifier, agreement among sampled answers, or execution checks, can score an
unlabeled calibration set once but is too costly for every served answer.

 5 Conclusion 
 RADAR reframes confidence calibration as more than a score-correction problem. By separating confidence repair, evidence-based adoption, and residual diagnosis, it clarifies what scalar recalibration can improve, what information it cannot recover, and what evidence is needed before a repaired score should be deployed. Across models, tasks, and distribution shifts, our results show that lower prediction risk does not necessarily imply better downstream decisions. Our results argue for treating confidence not as a number to be calibrated once, but as a quantity that should be repaired, validated, and audited before it is trusted in deployment.

 Ethics statement 
 Automated grades can reflect grader bias or mistakes, and repeated grading
does not measure all such errors. Neither low calibration error nor audit
non-rejection establishes safety for a high-stakes use. The intended use is
to support inspection and comparison of confidence scores, not to replace
domain-specific validation or human oversight. Any data and code release
must respect model and dataset licenses and remove credentials and
identifying infrastructure information.

 A.10 Repair classes and relation to prior work 
 Inputs define what a repair can change. 
 Suppose Z Z contains every input used by the compared predictors and all
risks use the same outcome and population law. A global scalar map
 τ ⁡ ( P ) \tau(P) assigns the same output whenever P P agrees. A group-dependent
map τ γ ⁡ ( Z ) ​ ( P ) \tau_{\gamma(Z)}(P) additionally uses a group assignment γ ⁡ ( Z ) \gamma(Z) .
When the group is not determined by P P , it can distinguish answers that
no function of P P alone can distinguish. Each group map may be
nondecreasing without preserving order across groups. Both outputs are
scalars: the distinction is their input restriction, not output dimension.
If a candidate uses features outside the declared Z Z , augment the
information set before applying the common-target identities. 
 Chen et al. (2024, Section 4) learn a supervised tree partition
from LLM top-layer query embeddings, using a squared-loss criterion on
correctness labels, and then fit a separate label-based isotonic regressor
within each group. Their groups are learned, not restricted to supplied
demographic categories. The deployed correction needs the group assignment.
PAIC instead fits one global map of P P to predictions from an independently
trained correctness probe. Table  1 separates these
choices from direct feature-based prediction. 
 Melo et al. (2026) develop partition-based estimators of grouping
loss and excess decision risk, with local confidence audits and LLM
cascades. Their work already connects errors hidden by average calibration
to downstream decisions. Our guarantees address a different set of objects:
the conditional probe bias affecting a global monotone map, paired risk
contrasts between frozen candidates, and residual moments with dependent
answers grouped by prompt. 
 All rows require accounting for fitting and tuning labels. PAIC’s map does
not see its fitting prompts’ labels, but its probe is label-trained.
The table describes the correction’s inputs, not the cost of obtaining P P ,
which may already require learned features. A score-only constraint must be
declared before comparisons. Enlarging a population predictor class cannot worsen its optimal
risk, but finite fitted methods can differ in approximation and estimation
error. A richer fitted predictor beating a scalar fit does not identify I I 
(Appendix  A.3 ).

 Appendix C Fixed-choice confidence on BoolQ and ARC 
 Question and design. 
 The study asks whether hidden states help predict correctness beyond the full
choice scores, and whether that information survives when deployment is
restricted to a monotone function of one scalar score. Each model selects one
option once per question, and every candidate predicts whether that fixed
selection is correct. The design crosses four checkpoints, Llama 3.2 3B
Instruct ( Meta, 2024 ) , Qwen 2.5 3B and 7B Instruct
 ( Qwen Team, 2025 ) and Mistral 7B Instruct v0.3
 ( Mistral AI, 2024 ) , with two benchmarks. The same questions occur
across models, so the eight cells are not independent replications, and
pretraining exposure to these public benchmarks is unknown. 
 Tasks and data separation.

validation split is the evaluation population T T , and its training data supply
development D D , fitting F F and two unlabeled projection sets C C and U U from
the same population. ARC ( Clark et al., 2018 ) supplies a difficulty shift: Easy
training supplies D / F D/F , Easy validation C C and Easy test a source evaluation
set S S ; Challenge training and validation supply unlabeled target inputs U U ,
and Challenge test is T T (Table  19 ). Groups of
questions linked by normalized exact matches of questions, passages or IDs are
kept within one role, and all predictions were saved before evaluation labels
were joined.

 Appendix D Extended Related Work 
 Classical calibration and probabilistic prediction. 
 Calibration asks whether predictive probabilities agree with empirical outcome
frequencies. Classical forecast decompositions separate reliability,
resolution, and irreducible uncertainty
 ( Murphy, 1973 ; DeGroot and Fienberg, 1983 ; Bröcker, 2009 ) , while proper scoring rules
provide decision-theoretic objectives for probabilistic prediction
 ( Gneiting and Raftery, 2007 ) . In machine learning, post-hoc methods such as
Platt scaling, isotonic regression, temperature scaling, beta calibration, and
Dirichlet calibration modify predictive probabilities without retraining the
underlying model
 ( Platt, 1999 ; Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana, 2005 ; Zadrozny and Elkan, 2002 ; Guo et al., 2017 ; Kull et al., 2017 ; Kull et al., 2019 ) . Reliability diagrams and
Expected Calibration Error (ECE) are widely used summaries, although their
interpretation depends on binning and finite-sample estimation
 ( Pakdaman Naeini et al., 2015 ; Vaicenavicius et al., 2019 ; Kumar et al., 2019 ; Roelofs et al., 2022 ) . More recent studies also emphasize
that calibration behavior depends on architecture, scale, pretraining, and
distribution shift rather than following a universal relationship with
accuracy  ( Minderer et al., 2021 ; Ovadia et al., 2019 ) . Our analysis differs
from work that asks only whether the reported scalar is calibrated: we ask
which part of correctness risk remains recoverable after the prediction has
already been compressed into that scalar. 
 Grouping loss, sufficiency, and feature-dependent calibration. 
 A scalar confidence score can be calibrated while hiding systematic
differences among examples assigned the same value. Grouping loss formalizes
the predictive information lost through this compression
 ( Perez-Lebel et al., 2023 ) ; under squared loss, our information term
 I = 𝔼 ⁡ [ Var ⁡ ( m ⋆ ​ ( Z ) ∣ P ) ] I=\mathbb{E}[\Var(m^{\star}(Z)\mid P)] is precisely this quantity under the declared
representation Z Z . Non-injective recalibration may further merge distinct
score values and thereby increase grouping loss
 ( Perez-Lebel et al., 2023 ) . Related approaches enrich the conditioning
information rather than repairing only the original scalar. Multiaccuracy
corrects systematic residuals over identifiable subpopulations
 ( Kim et al., 2018 ) , while multicalibration requires calibration over
many computationally identifiable groups
 ( Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018 ) . Semantic and representation-aware
variants similarly use features to expose errors hidden by an aggregate score
 ( Yang et al., 2023 ) . Our decomposition separates this pre-existing
information loss from an additional monotone-pooling term G G : even the
population-optimal order-preserving repair can therefore be scalar-calibrated
while remaining insufficient relative to Z Z . 
 Group-dependent calibration for LLMs. 
 Recent LLM work has begun to examine this distinction directly.
 Chen et al. (2024) use learned representation-based groups and fit
label-supervised isotonic calibrators within those groups. Their deployed
prediction therefore depends on both the original confidence and the learned
group assignment. Melo et al. (2026) use grouping-loss estimates to
identify local epistemic uncertainty and support auditing and deferral. These
approaches exploit richer information at serving time. PAIC addresses a
different constraint: richer features or a correctness model may be available
during fitting, while deployment must retain a single map of the
original score . Our theory therefore asks how much of a richer predictor can
be transferred into that restricted interface, and which errors of the richer
predictor affect the resulting scalar map. 
 Calibration under distribution shift. 
 Calibration commonly deteriorates when the deployment distribution differs
from the calibration distribution
 ( Ovadia et al., 2019 ; Minderer et al., 2021 ) . Under covariate shift,
importance weighting reweights labeled source observations to approximate
target expectations  ( Shimodaira, 2000 ) , and analogous ideas have
been developed specifically for calibration
 ( Park et al., 2020 ; Wang et al., 2020 ) . Under label shift, class
priors can instead be estimated and corrected
 ( Lipton et al., 2018 ; Alexandari et al., 2020 ) . Other work uses unlabeled
target data to estimate aggregate target performance, including ATC and
difference-of-confidence approaches
 ( Garg et al., 2022 ; Guillory et al., 2021 ) . These methods primarily
estimate target performance or reconstruct the target calibration objective
through assumptions on distribution shift. PAIC takes an outcome-model route:
a source-trained correctness probe predicts outcomes on unlabeled target
examples, and these predictions define the target-population scalar repair.
Accordingly, its relevant assumption is not an accurate target density ratio
but small score-conditional probe bias on the target population. 
 Distillation and privileged information. 
 PAIC is also related to knowledge distillation, where a richer teacher provides
soft supervision for a restricted student
 ( Hinton et al., 2015 ) , and to learning using privileged information,
where additional features are available during training but unavailable at
test time  ( Lopez-Paz et al., 2016 ) . Standard distillation typically asks
whether the student can approximate the teacher’s predictions or improve task
accuracy. In our setting, the student hypothesis class is deliberately narrow:
it is a nondecreasing function of a pre-existing scalar score. Consequently,
global teacher accuracy is not the quantity governing transfer. Only the
teacher’s systematic error after conditioning on the student input P P shifts
the population projection. The PAIC guarantee makes this distinction explicit
and shows why two probes with similar global prediction error can induce very
different scalar repairs. 
 Selective prediction, abstention, and decision utility. 
 Confidence is often useful because it determines whether a model prediction is
accepted, rejected, reviewed, or deferred. Selective classification studies
this accuracy–coverage trade-off and constructs predictors that abstain on
low-confidence inputs  ( Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017 ) ; related reject-option and
risk–coverage formulations likewise emphasize that uncertainty estimates are
valuable through the decisions they support. These objectives are distinct
from calibration itself: a monotone transformation can change probabilities
while preserving ranking, whereas a fixed decision threshold can change
behavior substantially after recalibration. Our decision experiments therefore
evaluate prediction risk and downstream review utility separately rather than
assuming that lower Brier risk implies a better selective policy. This
distinction is particularly important when the application assigns explicit
costs to acceptance and review. 
 Evidence for adopting a fitted repair. 
 A lower empirical loss on the data used to construct a repair is not evidence
that the repair improves population risk. Prediction-powered inference (PPI)
combines abundant model predictions with a smaller labeled sample by correcting
the model-based estimate with observed residuals
 ( Angelopoulos et al., 2023 ) ; PPI++ adapts the prediction contribution to
improve statistical efficiency
 ( Angelopoulos et al., 2024 ) . We specialize this model-assisted
estimation principle to paired squared-loss differences between frozen
confidence candidates. The resulting identity also clarifies which component
of probe error can reverse a candidate comparison. For finite candidate
families, empirical-Bernstein inequalities provide observable simultaneous
bounds  ( Maurer and Pontil, 2009 ) . When the operational question is only
whether a candidate improves over the deployed score, rather than estimation
of every pairwise contrast, we additionally use bounded-mean betting
 ( Waudby-Smith and Ramdas, 2024 ) . Our contribution here is not a new generic
inference paradigm, but an explicit separation between constructing a
confidence repair and obtaining independent statistical evidence that it
should be deployed. 
 Kernel calibration and residual-moment tests. 
 Kernel methods provide a complementary way to detect calibration errors that
are invisible to low-dimensional summaries. MMCE
 ( Kumar et al., 2018 ) and squared kernel calibration error (SKCE)
 ( Widmann et al., 2019 ) embed calibration residuals in a reproducing
kernel Hilbert space, while distribution-matching formulations generalize
these ideas to richer calibration conditions
 ( Marx et al., 2023 ) . In our setting the residual embedding 
 𝔼 ⁡ [ ( Y − s ⁡ ( Z ) ) ​ Φ ​ ( Z ) ] \mathbb{E}[(Y-s(Z))\Phi(Z)] 
 tests whether mean correctness errors remain associated with the declared
features. With a sufficiently rich RKHS, a zero population embedding
identifies the conditional mean predictor; with a finite feature map it tests
only the corresponding residual moments. This feature dependence is
intentional: an audit can only detect errors represented by its chosen
features and kernel. 
 Dependence, clustered answers, and kernel inference. 
 Standard calibration estimators commonly treat evaluated examples as
independent. LLM evaluation often violates this assumption because multiple
answers are sampled from the same prompt and can share reasoning paths,
difficulty, or failure modes. Pairing sibling residuals in a kernel
U-statistic can therefore introduce a nonvanishing finite-sample term even
when the population conditional-mean null holds. We instead treat prompts as
the independent sampling units and average residual features within prompt
before forming cross-prompt products. The corresponding multiplier procedure
is related to wild-bootstrap methods for degenerate kernel statistics
 ( Chwialkowski et al., 2014 ) , but is formulated for the prompt-clustered
statistic used here. This distinction is important for LLM settings with
best-of- K K , self-consistency, repeated decoding, or other multiple-answer
evaluation protocols. 
 Confidence estimation in language models. 
 LLM confidence can be derived from several fundamentally different signals.
Token and sequence likelihoods use the model’s generative probability
 ( Jiang et al., 2021 ) ; self-evaluation methods ask the model whether its own
answer is correct, including P(True)
 ( Kadavath et al., 2022 ) ; verbalized-confidence methods elicit numerical or
linguistic uncertainty directly
 ( Lin et al., 2022 ; Tian et al., 2023 ; Xiong et al., 2024 ) ; and sampling-based
methods estimate uncertainty through agreement or disagreement among multiple
generations
 ( Wang et al., 2023 ; Kuhn et al., 2023 ; Farquhar et al., 2024 ) .
These quantities need not coincide: likelihood measures probability of the
generated text, whereas answer-level confidence targets the probability that
the realized answer is correct. Empirical studies consequently find that the
quality of different confidence signals varies with model, task, elicitation
method, and access regime. Our framework is agnostic to the origin of the base
score: it treats any bounded scalar as the object to be retained, repaired, or
compared against richer alternatives. 
 Semantic uncertainty and answer-level correctness. 
 Sampling-based semantic uncertainty methods address the fact that different
surface forms can express the same underlying answer. Semantic entropy
aggregates probability across meaning-equivalent generations
 ( Kuhn et al., 2023 ; Farquhar et al., 2024 ) , while self-consistency exploits
agreement across reasoning trajectories
 ( Wang et al., 2023 ) . Nakkiran et al. (2026) study conditions
under which calibration of semantic answer classes can emerge from local
sequence-level loss optimality. These works concern uncertainty over semantic
answers or distributions of generated responses. Our object is different: the
answer A A has already been generated and fixed, and we ask whether a score
attached to that realized answer predicts its correctness. Semantic or
sampling-based signals can enter our framework as components of P P , as probe
features in Z Z , or as richer candidate predictors, but the repair,
comparison, and audit questions remain unchanged. 
 Internal representations and correctness probes. 
 A growing line of work predicts hallucination or correctness from hidden
representations rather than from output probabilities alone
 ( Orgad et al., 2025 ) . Such probes can reveal correctness information that
is absent from the reported scalar, making them natural witnesses of score
insufficiency. They also create an important comparison problem: if hidden
states are available at serving time, the direct probe should be compared
against any scalar projection of that probe rather than being used only as a
teacher. Conversely, when hidden states, additional sampling, execution
checks, or verifier calls are available offline but unavailable or too costly
at deployment, distilling their information into the existing scalar
interface becomes meaningful. Our experiments therefore distinguish the
 information available for fitting from the information permitted
at serving . 
 Relationship to our framework. 
 Taken together, prior work provides powerful tools for calibration,
group-dependent correction, adaptation, model-assisted inference, and residual
testing. Our framework connects these tools through a different organizing
question: what evidence is required before changing a deployed
confidence score? The risk decomposition first separates error recoverable
from the scalar from information already lost by scalar compression; PAIC
studies target-population repair when richer supervision is available only
offline; labeled comparisons ask whether a fitted candidate actually improves
correctness risk; and a separate prompt-level audit tests structured residual
errors that average-risk comparisons can miss. Thus, “repair,” “adopt,”
and “audit” are treated as distinct statistical problems rather than as
different names for calibration. 
 Appendix E Broader Impact and Implications 
 Reliable confidence estimates matter when LLM outputs trigger review,
abstention, regeneration or downstream actions. This work shows that improving
average calibration is not sufficient: a scalar score may discard
correctness-relevant distinctions, a fitted repair may not warrant adoption, and
residual failures may remain after average risk improves. Separating these
questions encourages deployment practices that state the information available
to a confidence predictor, compare repairs on held-out correctness evidence, and
audit structured residual errors at the right sampling unit. None of
calibration, a successful repair or audit non-rejection is a certificate of
safety: downstream utility depends on the application, decision costs, label
quality and the failure modes an audit represents. 
 Where the distinctions change a decision. 
 For review or abstention, the question is whether the score separates the
answers on which errors are costly. If relevant distinctions were discarded,
moving a threshold or fitting another scalar map cannot recover them, and richer
inputs become a candidate to evaluate; a repair that lowers average squared loss
still needs comparison at the intended review cost. For a new population without
labels, PAIC fits a score-only map from an available correctness probe, which
helps when the probe’s inputs are available during adaptation but costly or
unavailable at serving; its guarantee names the required transfer
property—small probe bias averaged at each score—and held-out target labels
are still needed for adoption. For regeneration or other corrective actions, an
audit can locate feature-aligned mean errors, but whether a new answer fixes
them needs its own comparison of success and cost.
