 Abstract. 
 Large language models still struggle with contest-level programming, while many agentic remedies rely on massive inference-time sampling or expensive multi-stage post-training. We study when execution feedback reliably helps an LLM CP solver and which mechanisms govern the gains. We model feedback-driven solving as a calibrated stopped process and identify three quantities: false-admission risk, program-level evidence against bad programs, and the active-state success hazard. Under held-out trace calibration and selection from a pre-declared finite controller manifest, the resulting structural certificate lower-bounds the clean success probability before false admission. We instantiate mechanisms targeting these quantities as Dual-Granularity Verification, Test Augmentation, and Experience-Driven Self-Evolving, yielding CP-Agent . Without updating any parameters, CP-Agent raises Pass@1 from 25.8% to 48.5% on LiveCodeBench Pro and improves Refine@5 by 11.0% on ICPC-Eval. Across three LLM backbones, CP-Agent lies on the cost–accuracy efficiency frontier, and ablations show that each component primarily affects its corresponding certificate quantity. 
 Keywords:  competitive programming, large language models, agentic reasoning, execution feedback, code generation

 1. Introduction 
 Large language models (LLMs) now perform well across a broad range of downstream tasks, including code generation and everyday software-engineering assistance. Contest-level competitive programming (CP), however, remains a sharp failure case: on ICPC/IOI-style benchmarks  ( Jain et al., 2025 ; Xu et al., 2026 ; Zheng et al., 2026 ) , even strong LLMs reach below 30% Pass@1 (Section  4 ). CP is judged by hidden correctness and asymptotic feasibility under strict time and memory limits, so plausible implementations earn no credit when the algorithm or an edge case is wrong, and software-engineering virtues such as maintainability or modular design do not compensate. 
 Recent work has explored two directions: (i) tackling competitive programming problems through massive code generation combined with heuristic-based inference and execution-based pruning, exemplified by AlphaCode  ( Li et al., 2022 ) , AlphaCode2  ( Leblond et al., 2023 ) , and o1-ioi  ( El-Kishky et al., 2025 ) ; (ii) enhancing the intrinsic reasoning ability of LLMs, including large reasoning models  ( Guo et al., 2025 ; El-Kishky et al., 2025 ; OpenAI, 2025b ; Liu et al., 2025 ) that are often trained with reinforcement learning. While these directions improve performance, they often come with substantial computational cost-large-scale sampling during inference, or expensive rollouts during training. 
 CP offers rich, executable feedback: compilation errors, test failures, and counterexamples directly expose concrete failure modes  ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) . This makes agentic solving a natural fit. Two gaps, however, remain for agentic CP. First, the literature disagrees on which mechanisms reliably help. MapCoder  ( Islam et al., 2024 ) reports that additional test-case generation is unhelpful while AlphaCodium  ( Ridnik et al., 2024 ) reports gains; MapCoder and Oi-Assistant  ( Yuhua et al., 2025 ) find retrieval of similar problems useful, while AlgoSimBench  ( Li and Mooney, 2025 ) finds it unstable. Second, recent strong agentic CP systems increasingly invest in multi-stage agentic post-training  ( Team et al., 2026 ) . In contrast, for training-free LLM agents on CP, it remains unclear whether tool orchestration alone improves performance and which mechanisms drive its gains. 
 These gaps converge to one essential question: which controllable mechanisms turn execution feedback into measurable gains on contest-level CP? We answer it through a calibrated risk-control view of feedback-driven solving.
We analyze feedback-driven CP solving as a calibrated, risk-controlled stopped process. Its state reduces to three certificate quantities: the residual bad-candidate envelope ρ t \rho_{t} , the accumulated program-level evidence I t I_{t} , and the active-state success hazard h ¯ t \underline{h}_{t} . Each is moved by exactly one abstract action class: RiskProbe on ρ t \rho_{t} , EvidenceAcquire on I t I_{t} , and ContextAcquire on h ¯ t \underline{h}_{t} . The calibrated false-admission UCB q ¯ t ≈ ρ ¯ t ​ exp ⁡ ( − ℐ t ) \bar{q}_{t}\approx\bar{\rho}_{t}\exp(-\mathcal{I}_{t}) follows as a derived quantity. This one-to-one reduction yields a unified design-and-selection framework: for any pre-declared finite manifest of controllers, it supplies a post-selection-valid lower bound on clean-before-false success.
Guided by this framework, we design CP-Agent with one controller per action class: Dual-Granularity Verification contracts ρ t \rho_{t} , Test Augmentation adds to I t I_{t} , and Experience-Driven Self-Evolving raises h ¯ t \underline{h}_{t} . A frozen admission gate and a refinement loop complete the agent.
Extensive empirical results across three backbones on two contest-level benchmarks, together with theory-grounded mechanism ablations on the five-controller calibration class, jointly validate CP-Agent ’s effectiveness and its calibrated certificate.

 3. Method 
 Motivation & Overview. 
 CP-Agent is the instantiation of the selected controller π ⋆ \pi^{\star} : each abstract action class maps to a concrete tool (Table  1 ).
All manifest entries—prompts, the observable admission gate, the HP and TA interfaces, the retrieval budget, the historical memory snapshot M ⋆ M^{\star} , the write mode, and the maximum horizon—are frozen before benchmark evaluation, either pre-declared or audited on held-out traces D cal D_{\rm cal} via Theorem  2.6 . 
 3.1. Dual-Granularity Verification 
 RiskProbe and EvidenceAcquire ​ ( m ) \textsf{EvidenceAcquire}(m) act at different granularities: the former screens a reasoning state before it is admitted, the latter gates a complete candidate before it is trusted.
 CP-Agent realizes them as a Hypothesis Prober for the logged upstream gate G t HP G_{t}^{\mathrm{HP}} and a Solution Validator for the program-level admission screen inside Γ t \Gamma_{t} .
Separating the two roles prevents the upstream screen from being double-counted as calibrated program-level evidence. 
 Hypothesis Prober. 
 During early-stage reasoning, the agent often needs to verify local claims that are hard to establish purely in natural language—e.g., whether a recurrence holds on random small instances, whether a greedy rule fails on a constructed counterexample, or whether an invariant is preserved by a transition.
 CP-Agent exposes a Hypothesis Prober that compiles and runs short C++ snippets for this purpose. Under the frozen prompt and decoding policy the backbone itself decides when to call HP from its observable reasoning state—no hand-written trigger grid—and every call/pass/fail outcome is logged for calibration.
A surviving HP call contracts the posterior odds of an upstream reasoning error (Proposition  2.7 ) and tightens the residual envelope ρ ¯ t probe , π \bar{\rho}_{t}^{\mathrm{probe},\pi} entering Eq. ( 9 ); HP thus moves ρ t \rho_{t} (and the aggregate q ¯ t \bar{q}_{t} ) alone, not the program-level evidence channel. 
 Solution Validator. 
 Once the agent produces a complete candidate implementation, the Solution Validator compiles and runs it against the local test suite: any public/local failure rejects the candidate and returns counterexample-level feedback.
By default SV is a deterministic hard screen and contributes no calibrated statistical evidence; only when the suite uses fresh sampled tests or an externally calibrated set is an all-pass event additionally folded into ℐ t \mathcal{I}_{t} as the binary survival channel of Proposition  2.8 . 
 3.2. Test Augmentation 
 The action class EvidenceAcquire ​ ( m ) \textsf{EvidenceAcquire}(m) makes the admission gate more informative against public-test-passing errors and exposes the evidence-acquisition intensity as a frozen controller entry.
Public test suites 𝒯 basic \mathcal{T}_{\text{basic}} are fixed and shallow, so passing them is a weak certificate: candidates routinely clear 𝒯 basic \mathcal{T}_{\text{basic}} yet fail hidden tests in corner regimes.
Test Augmentation (TA) instantiates EvidenceAcquire by appending consensus-filtered checks beyond 𝒯 basic \mathcal{T}_{\text{basic}} ; an all-pass on the augmented suite enters ℐ t \mathcal{I}_{t} as the binary survival channel of Proposition  2.8 . 
 Augmentation pipeline. 
 CP-Agent implements test augmentation via three stages.
 Input Synthesis. Given the statement, the agent synthesizes a C++ input generator based on testlib.h 2 2 
 2 
 https://github.com/MikeMirzayanov/testlib and command suites that sweep corner regimes such as min/max values, degenerate structures, adversarial patterns, and random sizes.
 Oracle Labeling. Ignoring time and memory limits often makes it easier to write a correct brute-force solver than an efficient one  ( Ridnik et al., 2024 ; He et al., 2025 ) ; CP-Agent therefore executes independently generated brute-force solvers on synthesized inputs to obtain candidate reference outputs.
 Consensus Filtering. Because brute-force programs can still be imperfect, CP-Agent runs multiple solvers in parallel and majority-votes their outputs. Candidates are evaluated on augmented tests only after passing public tests, so augmentation serves as additional diagnostic evidence after the basic admission screen.
 Budget and retries. The frozen TA manifest caps synthesis at 32 candidate augmented inputs per problem. This is an upper bound rather than a fixed suite size: the generator does not keep sampling until a nonempty suite is obtained, and there is no separate retention cap after filtering. Instead, CP-Agent retains all valid inputs that pass synthesis, compilation/execution, and consensus labeling within the cap; generator synthesis/compilation/execution may be retried up to five times. Consequently, GenRate measures the fraction of problems for which this capped protocol yields a nonempty suite, while the candidate-level evidence calibration treats the resulting suite as one binary survival channel.

For each problem q i q_{i} , the system prompt is initialized with the general bank of M ⋆ M^{\star} and, when helpful, retrieved algorithmic experiences E i alg E_{i}^{\mathrm{alg}} . The frozen backbone then drafts a solution and may call the Hypothesis Prober to falsify local hypotheses before committing; the Solution Validator compiles and runs each draft on public tests, whose feedback drives subsequent edits; once all public tests pass, Test Augmentation generates consensus-filtered cases. In deployment mode, attempts may be appended to retrievable memory via Eq. ( 20 ); in certificate-evaluation mode they are written only to the audit log M ~ \widetilde{M} via Eq. ( 22 ). The mapping to the abstract controller of Section  2 is direct: HP → RiskProbe \to\textsf{RiskProbe} , SV/TA → EvidenceAcquire ​ ( m ) \to\textsf{EvidenceAcquire}(m) , Experience → ContextAcquire ​ ( k ) \to\textsf{ContextAcquire}(k) . Algorithm  1 gives the full loop, with an outer loop over the T T refinement steps of Def.  2.1 and an inner loop over within-step HP probes (the interaction steps of Fig.  3(a) ), so a single refinement step—including T = 1 T{=}1 on LCB-Pro—can host many HP calls before the candidate is committed.

 4. Experiment 
 4.1. Experiment Setup 
 Backbone LLMs 
 To evaluate the general applicability of our method across different LLMs, we conducted experiments using three distinct backbone LLMs: GPT-4o  ( Hurst et al., 2024 ) , Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct  ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , and DeepSeek-V3.2-Chat  ( Liu et al., 2025 ) for all multi-agent workflows and CP-Agent variants. 
 Baselines 
 We compare against two baseline groups:
 (i) Frontier LLMs: DeepSeek-R1  ( Guo et al., 2025 ) , DeepSeek-V3.2-Reasoner  ( Liu et al., 2025 ) , Gemini-3-pro  ( Pichai et al., 2025 ) , and o4-mini-high  ( OpenAI, 2025b ) .
 (ii) Agent frameworks/workflows: For a fair comparison, we evaluate three agent frameworks: Reflexion  ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) , CodeSim  ( Islam et al., 2025 ) , and AlphaCodium  ( Ridnik et al., 2024 ) , each with the same three backbones, all targeting C++. 
 Evaluation datasets. 
 We evaluate all baselines and CP-Agent variants on two competition-style, contamination-controlled benchmarks: LiveCodeBench Pro  ( Zheng et al., 2026 ) and ICPC-Eval  ( Xu et al., 2026 ) . The target split for LCB-Pro is the held-out 2025Q2 subset. The temporal partition that Experience uses on LCB-Pro and the controller transfer to ICPC-Eval are described in § 4.2 (summarized in Table  3 ). 
 Metrics 
 For ICPC-Eval, we use Refine@K  ( Xu et al., 2026 ) to measure iterative refinement under execution feedback, i.e., whether the model passes the hidden judge within K K response–revision attempts; we report Refine@5 ( K = 5 K{=}5 ) to reflect the interactive nature of competitive programming.
For LiveCodeBench Pro, we report Pass@1 to match its standard single-shot setting and prior work, and use per-refinement-step analyses ( t = 1 , 2 , 3 t{=}1,2,3 ) when studying the role of experience. 
 Implementations. 
 The full frozen controller manifest—refinement horizon T T , retrieval budgets ( k gen , ℓ alg ) (k_{\rm gen},\ell_{\rm alg}) , TA intensity m m and consensus rule γ evid \gamma_{\rm evid} , decoding parameters, and prompt template—is given in Table  3 . Baselines (Reflexion, CodeSim, AlphaCodium) use their official prompts and control flow with the same three backbones. HP, TA, and Experience are added only inside CP-Agent variants and never touch baselines. 
 4.2. Calibration Protocol and Auditability 
 The controller is calibrated before final benchmark reporting and is never recomputed after observing evaluation outcomes. We use a temporal three-stage protocol, 
 D hist mem → D cal → D test . D_{\rm hist}^{\rm mem}\rightarrow D_{\rm cal}\rightarrow D_{\rm test}. 
 First, the 387 historical pre-test problems in biannual_2024_7_12 construct candidate memory snapshots through the memory-building rule in Eq. ( 20 ). Second, for each candidate policy π ∈ Π α \pi\in\Pi_{\alpha} , we run the frozen agent with a fixed snapshot M ⋆ M^{\star} on the disjoint 166-problem calibration split quater_2025_1_3 , record every active trace row, compute the one-sided bounds in Eq. ( 7 ), audit the observable admission gate using Eq. ( 8 ), and select the best declared manifest from Π α \Pi_{\alpha} by Eq. ( 12 ). Third, final benchmark runs on D test D_{\rm test} use the selected controller in certificate-evaluation mode: the policy, memory snapshot, TA consensus threshold, HP tool interface, prompts, decoding parameters, maximum horizon, and write mode are frozen, and target-test experiences are written only to the audit log but not retrievable. For ICPC-Eval we transfer the LCB-Pro–selected controller and the same frozen snapshot M ⋆ M^{\star} directly to the ICPC target split, under the identical certificate-evaluation mode. 
 Hidden-judge outcomes are used in calibration only as labels for S t S_{t} and F t F_{t} after a candidate has passed the observable gate Γ t \Gamma_{t} ; they are not exposed to the agent during refinement and are never used for target-split memory writes, retrieval, prompt updates, routing, or controller selection. This separation is important: public/local execution can guide repair, whereas hidden outcomes define the calibration labels for the stopped-process certificate. A trace contributes to the denominator n t n_{t} only if it is active at step t t ; a candidate that fails SV or TA contributes neither to s t s_{t} nor to f t f_{t} because it was not admitted. These conventions align the empirical hazards with Definition  2.1 and the admission risk with Theorem  2.5 .

 Experience-Driven Self-Evolving 
 Certificate-bearing benchmark results use the temporal frozen snapshot protocol in Section  4.2 : historical problems build M ⋆ M^{\star} , calibration audits the controller with M ⋆ M^{\star} fixed, and final testing reads the same snapshot. Deployment-mode growth diagnostics in the full study show that general experiences saturate quickly while algorithmic memories keep expanding as new problem types appear; the curriculum effect follows our write-in criterion, because experiences are stored only after a failure is repaired. These diagnostics explain snapshot construction on D hist mem D_{\rm hist}^{\rm mem} , while all reported certificates freeze the resulting snapshot before D cal D_{\rm cal} and D test D_{\rm test} .

 5. Related Work 
 Competitive programming agents. 
 Among tool-augmented LLM agents that ground reasoning on deterministic execution feedback  ( Yao et al., 2022 ; Madaan et al., 2023 ; Gao et al., 2023 ; Chen et al., 2022b ; Gou et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2025 ; Das et al., 2024 ) , work on CP benchmarks  ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ; Chen et al., 2021 ; Jain et al., 2025 ; Zheng et al., 2026 ; Xu et al., 2026 ) mostly raises accuracy by scale: dedicated training or massive sampling  ( Li et al., 2022 ; Leblond et al., 2023 ; El-Kishky et al., 2025 ; Guo et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2025 ) . Inference-time pipelines such as MapCoder, AlphaCodium, and CodeSim avoid retraining by iterating generation and verification, but are session-local and discard repair experience after each problem  ( Islam et al., 2024 ; Ridnik et al., 2024 ; Islam et al., 2025 ; Yuhua et al., 2025 ; Li and Mooney, 2025 ) . Reflexion-style self-refinement  ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) keeps verbal memory across problems but offers no admission rule for when retrieved memory should enter the loop. CP-Agent adds neither training nor sampling: a calibrated controller ties generation, verification, and a frozen memory snapshot to a single held-out audit, closing the gap to a stronger reasoner  ( Liu et al., 2025 ) on ICPC-Eval Refine@5. 
 Testing, risk control, and frozen memory. 
 Randomized testing and generated checks have long driven program repair and synthesis  ( Claessen and Hughes, 2000 ; Solar-Lezama et al., 2006 ; Chen et al., 2022a ; He et al., 2025 ) , but are usually consumed as raw signals. We treat SV and TA as calibrated evidence channels via binomial calibration, conformal-style risk control, and anytime-valid evidence  ( Clopper and Pearson, 1934 ; Angelopoulos et al., 2024 ; Howard et al., 2021 ; Vovk and Wang, 2021 ) , so coverage and post-survival risk are read off held-out traces. Experience enters as a temporal frozen memory prior with PAC-Bayes/MDL complexity control  ( McAllester, 1999 ; McAllester, 1999 ) : tools and memory are fixed on D hist mem D_{\rm hist}^{\rm mem} before D cal D_{\rm cal} and D test D_{\rm test} (Section  4.2 ) so the audit remains valid; memory may still grow in deployment. 
 6. Conclusion 
 A calibrated risk-controlled controller substantially improves contest-level programming without additional training. The theory derives certificate quantities and abstract action classes that are audited on held-out traces and frozen before evaluation; CP-Agent instantiates them as Dual-Granularity Verification, Test Augmentation, and Experience-Driven Self-Evolving, matches DeepSeek-V3.2-Reasoner on ICPC-Eval Refine@5, and provides trace-calibrated mechanism evidence for each action class.

 D.6. Tool Use vs. Problem Difficulty 
 Figure  3(a) analyzes verification-tool usage along the interaction-step axis on a single backbone. This subsection complements that view with the orthogonal difficulty axis: how much tool-mediated effort CP-Agent spends per problem as problems become harder. The diagnostic uses the DeepSeek-V3.2-Chat backbone on LCB-Pro and counts the average number of HP/SV/TA invocations per problem, broken down by the benchmark’s Easy/Medium/Hard difficulty bands. 
 Figure 5 . Average tool-use frequency (calls per problem) on LCB-Pro by difficulty band (Easy/Medium/Hard), broken down by tool channel (HP, SV, TA). DeepSeek-V3.2-Chat backbone. See body for the resulting per-band totals and their interpretation. 
 Adaptive scaling on Easy/Medium. 
 Compared with a fixed-pipeline baseline (each method calls every tool a constant number of times), CP-Agent issues 5.5 5.5 tool calls per problem on Easy and 5.1 5.1 on Medium. The slight drop from Easy to Medium reflects two competing effects under the certificate machinery of Section  2 : (i) Medium problems trigger longer chain-of-thought before an executable candidate is produced, so HP and SV start firing later in the trajectory; (ii) once an executable candidate is produced, the admission gate Γ t \Gamma_{t} continues to require evidence, so tool calls remain non-trivial. Empirically the two effects roughly cancel, and CP-Agent still relies heavily on external feedback on Easy and Medium problems. 
 Why Hard drops to 3.3 3.3 . 
 On Hard problems, average tool calls drop to 3.3 3.3 . This is consistent with two structural facts already documented in the main paper: 
 • 
 Few executable candidates are reached. Hard problems often consume the budget on high-level algorithmic discovery before any candidate is produced. SV is only meaningful on executable candidates, and TA is only invoked when SV first returns pass on public tests; both channels stay neutral on trajectories that never reach this state. This is the same structural barrier that produces the unchanged Hard column in Table  10 : refinement-loop tools cannot substitute for missing high-level ideas. 
 • 
 Public-test-only filter. The augmented suite 𝒯 aug \mathcal{T}_{\rm aug} is generated at most once per problem (Algorithm  1 ), conditional on the candidate first passing public tests. On Hard problems this trigger fires rarely, suppressing the TA contribution to the per-problem call count. 
 In other words, the drop on Hard is not a controller-level decision to invoke fewer tools, but a downstream consequence of the candidate stream itself becoming sparse: the gates G i , t HP , G i , t SV , G i , t TA G^{\mathrm{HP}}_{i,t},G^{\mathrm{SV}}_{i,t},G^{\mathrm{TA}}_{i,t} from Algorithm  1 record neutral on inactive channels (Definition  2.1 ). Combined with Fig.  3(a) , which shows HP concentrated in early interaction steps, this gives a coherent picture: CP-Agent spends tool budget where evidence is actionable, and remains efficient elsewhere by leaving inactive channels neutral rather than firing them spuriously. 
 D.7. Experience-Driven Efficiency 
 Section  3.3 attributes the Experience module to ContextAcquire ​ ( k ) \textsf{ContextAcquire}(k) , whose effect in Proposition  2.9 is to lift the active-state success hazard h ¯ t ​ ( M ) \underline{h}_{t}(M) . Beyond the per-step acceptance lift reported in Table  5 , a higher h ¯ t \underline{h}_{t} should also let CP-Agent commit earlier in the refinement loop, with downstream effects on both the average number of refinement steps and the per-problem cost. We isolate this efficiency channel by an internal ablation of π ⋆ \pi^{\star} that toggles only the frozen Experience snapshot M ⋆ M^{\star} on the LCB-Pro target split (DeepSeek-V3.2-Chat backbone, manifest of Table  3 ). 
 Figure 6 . Average refinement steps and average per-problem API cost on LCB-Pro with and without the frozen Experience snapshot M ⋆ M^{\star} (DeepSeek-V3.2-Chat backbone, π ⋆ \pi^{\star} from Table  3 ). Both axes are averaged over all 167 167 LCB-Pro problems. 
 Both axes move in the right direction. 
 With Experience, the average refinement step count drops and the average per-problem cost drops jointly (Fig.  6 ). The same direction shows up in the component ablation of Table  4 : adding Experience on top of + SV + HP + TA +\textsf{SV}+\textsf{HP}+\textsf{TA} moves CP-Agent from $ 0.043 0.043 to $ 0.035 0.035 per problem and improves the only-solved # ​ T \#T on ICPC-Eval from 2.2 2.2 to 1.8 1.8 , while raising LCB-Pro Pass@1 from 46.1 46.1 to 48.5 48.5 . Two measurement axes—LCB-Pro all-problem average in Fig.  6 and ICPC-Eval only-solved # ​ T \#T in Table  4 —thus agree on the sign of the effect, while differing in absolute magnitude due to the averaging convention. 
 Mechanism interpretation. 
 The cost reduction is not driven by Experience being a cheaper module: Table  13 shows Experience itself consumes only ∼ 3.4 % \sim\!3.4\% of the total tracked API cost. Instead, the savings act through the certificate state. A higher h ¯ t ​ ( M ) \underline{h}_{t}(M) lets the admission gate Γ t \Gamma_{t} close earlier on the trajectories that would otherwise have continued, which in turn truncates the HP/SV invocations downstream of those refinement steps. Since HP and SV together account for 87.3 % 87.3\% of tracked cost (Table  13 ), the saved HP/SV calls dominate the additional retrieval cost incurred by Experience. In the language of Section  2 , ContextAcquire converts hazard lift into refinement-step savings, and the savings on the expensive verification channels outweigh the retrieval overhead—giving the qualitative claim in Section  4 that “retrieved repair experiences reduce ineffective trial-and-error” a concrete per-problem efficiency signature. 
 Appendix E Datasets 
 ICPC-Eval  ( Xu et al., 2026 ) 
 is a competitive-programming benchmark designed to reflect real contest settings. It is built from problems drawn from 11 11 recent ICPC contests (collected via QOJ and VJudge): starting from 139 139 problems, the dataset is cleaned and finalized to 118 118 problems. It filters out problems that rely on crucial non-textual figures, interactive problems, or problems without an official reference solution. For tasks requiring special judging, ICPC-Eval retains them and additionally implements 12 12 custom special judges (e.g., for floating-point outputs or multiple valid answers). All problem statements are standardized into LaTeX for consistent parsing and model input.  Appendix F Metrics 
 Refine@K  ( Xu et al., 2026 ) 
 is an execution-feedback-based evaluation metric designed to measure an LLM’s ability to iteratively improve a solution under a bounded turn budget. In ICPC-Eval, each problem is solved through up to K K response–refinement chances, and the metric asks whether the model can “pass the test within response and refinement chances” (i.e., succeed within K K turns). Concretely, the evaluation is multi-turn: in the first turn, the model receives the full problem statement; in subsequent turns, the model is additionally provided with its previous response and the corresponding evaluation feedback. Thus, a model is counted as successful under Refine@K if any of its first K K attempts (conditioned on prior code + feedback) produces a correct program. Compared with Pass@K (independent resampling), Refine@K better captures the behavior of reasoning/reflection-capable models because it explicitly evaluates whether a model can use feedback to correct mistakes rather than merely getting lucky with another sample. 
 # Turn  ( Xu et al., 2026 ) 
 represents the average number of correct response turns, computed only over problems that are successfully solved within the refinement budget. Specifically, for each solved problem, we record the turn index at which the model first produces a correct solution, and then average this index across the solved subset. A smaller #T indicates the model typically fixes its solution within fewer turns, suggesting more efficient use of execution feedback, whereas a larger #T implies the model often needs more trial-and-error refinement rounds before arriving at a correct program. 
 Pass@1 
 measures the single-try success rate of code generation: for each problem, the model produces exactly one candidate solution (one response/submission). If this first solution passes the evaluation (e.g., all test cases), the problem is counted as solved. Pass@1 is the fraction of problems solved on the first attempt: 
 Pass @ 1 = 1 N ∑ i = 1 N 𝕀 [ \displaystyle\mathrm{Pass@1}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\big[ 
 the model’s first solution for 
 problem  i  passes ] . \displaystyle\text{problem }i\text{ passes}\big]. 
 Intuitively, Pass@1 reflects the model’s initial answer quality without any retries or feedback-driven refinement, in contrast to iterative metrics such as Refine@K.

#include <bits/stdc++.h>
using namespace std;
const double INF = 1e18;
const double EPS = 1e-12;
int main() {
 ios::sync_with_stdio(false);
 cin.tie(nullptr);
 int d, w;
 cin >> d >> w;
 vector<string> dice(d);
 for (int i = 0; i < d; i++) {
 cin >> dice[i];
 // each dice[i] has exactly 6 characters
 }
 vector<string> words(w);
 for (int i = 0; i < w; i++) {
 cin >> words[i];
 }
 // Preprocess words: sort characters
 unordered_set<string> wordSet;
 for (string& wrd : words) {
 string sorted = wrd;
 sort(sorted.begin(), sorted.end());
 wordSet.insert(sorted);
 }
 int total_configs = pow(6, d);
 // faces[config][i] = face index of die i
 vector<vector<int>> faces(total_configs, vector<int>(d));
 vector<bool> win(total_configs, false);
 bool any_win = false;
 for (int id = 0; id < total_configs; id++) {
 int tmp = id;
 vector<char> symbols(d);
 for (int i = 0; i < d; i++) {
 int f = tmp % 6;
 tmp /= 6;
 faces[id][i] = f;
 symbols[i] = dice[i][f];
 }
 string sorted_sym(symbols.begin(), symbols.end());
 sort(sorted_sym.begin(), sorted_sym.end());
 if (wordSet.count(sorted_sym)) {
 win[id] = true;
 any_win = true;
 }
 }
 if (!any_win) {
 cout << "impossible" << endl;
 return 0;
 }
 // DP arrays
 vector<double> dp(total_configs, 0.0);
 vector<double> dp_new(total_configs, 0.0);
 for (int i = 0; i < total_configs; i++) {
 dp[i] = win[i] ? 0.0 : 1000.0; // initial guess
 }
 // Precompute popcount and powers of 6
 vector<int> popcount(1 << d, 0);
 for (int m = 0; m < (1 << d); m++) {
 popcount[m] = __builtin_popcount(m);
 }
 vector<double> pow6(d + 1, 1.0);
 for (int i = 1; i <= d; i++) {
 pow6[i] = pow6[i - 1] * 6.0;
 }
 // Precompute offsets for sum array
 int total_partial = pow(7, d);
 vector<double> sum(total_partial, 0.0);
 vector<int> offset(1 << d, 0);
 int cur_offset = 0;
 for (int m = 0; m < (1 << d); m++) {
 offset[m] = cur_offset;
 cur_offset += pow(6, popcount[m]);
 }
 // Helper: compute projection id for config id and mask
 auto getProjId = [&](int config_id, int mask) -> int {
 int proj_id = 0;
 int base = 1;
 for (int i = 0; i < d; i++) {
 if (mask >> i & 1) {
 int face = faces[config_id][i];
 proj_id += face * base;
 base *= 6;
 }
 }
 return proj_id;
 };
 // Value iteration
 int iter = 0;
 double max_diff;
 do {
 // Update sum array
 fill(sum.begin(), sum.end(), 0.0);
 for (int c = 0; c < total_configs; c++) {
 double val = dp[c];
 for (int m = 0; m < (1 << d); m++) {
 int proj_id = getProjId(c, m);
 int idx = offset[m] + proj_id;
 sum[idx] += val;
 }
 }
 // Compute dp_new
 max_diff = 0.0;
 for (int c = 0; c < total_configs; c++) {
 if (win[c]) {
 dp_new[c] = 0.0;
 continue;
 }
 double best = INF;
 int full_mask = (1 << d) - 1;
 for (int R = 0; R < full_mask; R++) {
 int U = full_mask ^ R;
 int k = popcount[U];
 int proj_id = getProjId(c, R);
 int idx = offset[R] + proj_id;
 double expected = 1.0 + sum[idx] / pow6[k];
 if (expected < best) best = expected;
 }
 dp_new[c] = best;
 max_diff = max(max_diff, fabs(dp_new[c] - dp[c]));
 }
 swap(dp, dp_new);
 iter++;
 } while (max_diff > EPS && iter < 5000);
 // Compute initial expected rolls
 double total = 0.0;
 for (int c = 0; c < total_configs; c++) {
 total += dp[c];
 }
 double ans = 1.0 + total / pow6[d];
 cout << fixed << setprecision(9) << ans << endl;
 return 0;

 Appendix I Extended Discussion on Related Work 
 LLM Agents. 
 Large Language Models have been increasingly deployed as autonomous agents capable of interacting with external environments and solving multi-step tasks. In the domain of web navigation, researchers have developed agents to autonomously browse and interact with online interfaces  ( Ning et al., 2025 ; He et al., 2024 ; Lai et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ) . In the field of software engineering, agents such as SWE-Agent have been proposed to handle repository-level maintenance and development tasks  ( Luo et al., 2024 ; Gao et al., 2025 ) . Furthermore, the application of agents has extended to high-level intellectual domains, including automated scientific discovery  ( Toledo et al., 2026 ; Baek et al., 2025 ; Lu et al., 2024 ) and data science analysis, where agents assist in data visualization and interpretation  ( Zhu et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2024 ; Jiang et al., 2024 ) . These advancements collectively demonstrate the potential of LLM agents to generalize across diverse and complex workflows. 
 In contrast to the extensive body of work on autonomous agents interacting with dynamic external environments, a more focused stream of research investigates Agentic Reasoning—the use of deterministic tools to enhance the model’s internal cognitive processes. Unlike autonomous agents that use tools as “effectors” to change the environment state, this paradigm employs tools—primarily code interpreters (e.g., Python) and calculators—as “cognitive prosthetics” to ground the reasoning process itself. Early works such as PAL  ( Gao et al., 2023 ) and PoT  ( Chen et al., 2022b ) demonstrated that decoupling reasoning from computation significantly reduces hallucinations in mathematical and symbolic tasks. Advancing this trajectory, recent frameworks  ( Wu et al., 2025 ; Gou et al., 2024 ; Das et al., 2024 ; Shang et al., 2025 ) establish a more sophisticated interleaved reasoning–action loop. By integrating code execution directly into the chain of thought, these methods utilize deterministic feedback for iterative step verification and error correction, enabling the model to navigate complex logical paths with higher reliability. Although fewer in number compared to general agent papers, these studies highlight a critical distinction: for challenging reasoning tasks, the value of a tool lies not in its complexity or connectivity, but in its ability to provide deterministic, verifiable feedback that constrains the model’s probabilistic output. 
 Competitive Programming. 
 Program synthesis has evolved significantly from early deductive  ( Manna and Waldinger, 1980 ) and inductive  ( Spector and Robinson, 2002 ) synthesis to neural approaches like DeepCoder  ( Balog et al., 2016 ) . However, the release of challenging benchmarks  ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ; Jain et al., 2025 ; Zheng et al., 2026 ; Xu et al., 2026 ; Sun et al., 2026 ) revealed a harsh reality: early models failed near-completely on competition-level problems. AlphaCode  ( Li et al., 2022 ) marked a major milestone in this domain by leveraging massive sampling for competitive programming problems, while AlphaCode2  ( Leblond et al., 2023 ) and OpenAI’s o1-ioi  ( El-Kishky et al., 2025 ) later adopted reinforcement learning and heuristic inference strategies to achieve gold-medal performance. Most recently, OpenAI o3  ( OpenAI, 2025a ) demonstrated that general-purpose reasoning models could reach Grandmaster status on Codeforces without domain-specific hand-crafting. 
 Since training such reasoning models is computationally prohibitive, recent research has pivoted to inference-time enhancements using hand-crafting LLM workflows like MapCoder  ( Islam et al., 2024 ) , AlphaCodium  ( Ridnik et al., 2024 ) , and CODESIM  ( Islam et al., 2025 ) . These methods simulate human problem-solving through iterative “generate-and-verify” cycles. However, they mainly rely on fixed, stateless workflows, which suffer from rigidity: they lack the adaptivity to scale reasoning effort within a problem, and crucially, they fail to transfer experience across problems, discarding valuable debugging insights after each session. This limitation highlights the need for more dynamic and long-term evolving mechanisms.
