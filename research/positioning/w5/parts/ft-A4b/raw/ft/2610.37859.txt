 Abstract 
 Language-model agents differ fundamentally from prior intelligent systems in their ability to learn from natural language in context.
This capability enables agents to iteratively refine their answers in response to feedback, a prevalent mode of operation in modern agentic workflows.
However, the community primarily ranks models by metrics such as Pass@1 or Pass@K , which cannot capture how they utilize feedback on prior attempts.
We introduce Seq@K , a natural evaluation protocol that measures whether a model can solve a problem within K K sequential attempts, provided feedback after each previous failure.
Unlike multi-turn agentic benchmarks that examine long-horizon planning, Seq@K isolates and formalizes the core capability of refinement from feedback.
We evaluate Seq@K on a suite of 7 benchmarks and 16 frontier models, and show that Seq@K reveals a materially different model-selection landscape: under Seq@K , (1) model rankings often differ,
(2) the Pareto frontiers admit more efficient configurations and (3) the gains from choosing the optimal model configuration can widen or shrink relative to those of Pass@K .
We further show that analyzing model trajectories under Seq@K helps characterize iterative refinement behavior, such as the rate at which models address issues raised in feedback, or how rigidly they hold onto their own answers.
Finally, we study how the nature of feedback affects models’ iterative refinement, showing that practitioners must carefully design and benchmark the feedback loops of iterative agentic systems.
Together, these results establish how Seq@K offers a more faithful metric for intelligent systems that typically operate in such feedback loop, and the necessity to consider Seq@K in evaluating models for these systems. 
 1 Introduction 
 Multi-turn interaction with feedback is a fundamental pattern of machine learning, driving classical paradigms including multi-armed bandits  ( Auer et al., 2002 ) , active learning  ( Settles, 2009 ) , and reinforcement learning  ( Sutton & Barto, 2018 ) .
Recently, this same principle has catalyzed much of the progress in large language models (LLMs), underpinning advances in test-time compute  ( Snell et al., 2024 ) , automatic prompt optimization  ( Yuksekgonul et al., 2025 ) , and agentic systems  ( Park et al., 2023 ; Yao et al., 2023 ) .
Yet a striking disconnect has emerged between how these systems operate and how they are evaluated.
Even as modern methods  ( Shinn et al., 2023 ; Madaan et al., 2023 ) and environments  ( Wang et al., 2024 ; Yao et al., 2025 ) center on iterative refinement, the community still ranks models primarily by Pass@K   ( Chen et al., 2021 ) , a strictly feedback-blind metric. 
 We argue that relying solely on feedback-blind evaluation is insufficient for developing agentic systems—it may reward the wrong behaviors and obscure the right ones.
For example, consider a coding agent: the user issues an initial request, the agent proposes an edit, the user responds with targeted critiques, and the agent revises.
This loop of proposal, feedback, and revision is the dominant pattern of agent usage today, with applications extending far beyond coding.
Yet Pass@K is blind to whether an agent can excel in this sequential decision-making loop.
This blind spot hides failure modes and undervalues models that successfully improve through iteration. 
 In this work, we quantify a model’s ability to improve under sequential feedback by measuring it along a new axis, which we name Seq@K .
 Seq@K measures whether a model solves a task within K K attempts when it receives feedback after each failed attempt.
Ranking models by Pass@K implicitly assumes that a model that is accurate in one turn also uses feedback well.
We show that this assumption often breaks down.
We evaluate Seq@K on coding (MBPP Pro, HumanEval Pro  ( Yu et al., 2025 ) ), complex instruction following (AdvancedIF  ( He et al., 2025 ) ), domain reasoning (CLBench DKR  ( Dou et al., 2026 ) ), and medical benchmarks (HealthBench  ( Arora et al., 2025 ) ).
Our results reveal how model rankings, cost frontiers, and failure modes change when models are allowed to revise their work based on feedback. 
 Our contributions and key empirical insights are structured around three primary questions: 
 1. 
 Model Selection & Cost Dynamics: Model rankings can change between evaluations based on Pass@K and Seq@K .
We demonstrate (a) the benefit of Seq@K by showing that it leads to better and more cost-efficient model as calibration metric, (b) Pass@K can understate or overstate differences in model capability under sequential feedback.
(c) Seq@K often exposes superior Pareto frontier at deployment than that of Pass@K and (d) how Seq@K tracks a different capability from Pass@K (Section  5.1 ). 
 2. 
 Uncovering Revision Patterns: Beyond reporting Seq@K ,
we further analyze the way in which models revise their attempts: (a) models can differ in when they gain, with some gaining most on the first retry and others on later attempts,
(b) revisions do not always address the feedback, and some models resubmit the same attempt unchanged,
and (c) even revisions that address all of the feedback can fail by breaking requirements that passed before (Section  5.2 ). 
 3. 
 Diagnosing the Feedback Loop: Seq@K also helps diagnose the feedback itself.
We show (a) the usefulness of informative feedback depends on both the benchmark and the model capability,
(b) critiques written with the grading rubric tend to help models repair far more failed requirements,
and (c) summarizing the previous attempts can lower success rate, so how the history is kept should be reported along with Seq@K (Section  5.3 ). 
 2 Related Work 
 Refinement from feedback. 
 A line of related work considers having a model reasoning and revising in response to critique or execution feedback as methods to improve agent performance ( Madaan et al., 2023 ; Shinn et al., 2023 ; Gou et al., 2024 ) .
Reflexion ( Shinn et al., 2023 ) is the closet to our setting by considering agent’s reasoning on top of binary pass/fail signal or results on self-generated test cases.
In contrast, our work centers on Seq@K as an evaluation metric, and as a result, considers situation where the feedback is provided under privileged information about the correct answer or grading criteria.
This reflects the common setting where users of an agent provides information on how its attempts were inadequate.
Other recent work evaluate this ability by introducing novel tasks.
RefineBench ( Lee et al., 2025 ) introduces a new set of tasks with checklist-based evaluation, and finds that self-refinement yields little improvement while targeted feedback drives frontier models close to saturation.
ICPC-Eval’s Refine@K ( Xu et al., 2025 ) measures revision from execution feedback in competitive programming.
Instead of constructing new tasks, we demonstrate that on existing benchmarks and settings the community already considers, the Seq@K evaluation protocol already reveals insights on model performance rankings and novel cost-performance Pareto frontiers.
These insights demonstrate how Seq@K is an important metric to report alongside Pass@K on benchmarks or settings where iterative refinement is expected in practice. 
 Multi-turn and agentic evaluation. 
 Agent benchmarks such as SWE-bench, WebArena, and τ \tau -bench ( Jimenez et al., 2024 ; Zhou et al., 2024 ; Yao et al., 2025 ) and multi-turn evaluations such as MINT ( Wang et al., 2024 ) score models over long interactions with tools and environments.
In these settings, success depends jointly on planning, tool use, state tracking, and the use of feedback.
 Seq@K isolates the last of these, where each piece of feedback provides information on the entire attempt.
Hence, differences in Seq@K reflect how models revise their attempts and not how they navigate an environment. 
 3 Seq@K Evaluation Protocol 
 Both Pass@K and Seq@K evaluate whether a model succeeds at least once within an attempt budget of K K , but they differ in whether candidate draws are independent or generated through iterative refinement from feedback.
Independent sampling generates each candidate from a static task prompt, whereas sequential evaluation conditions each revision on preceding candidate responses and feedback. We mathematically formulate these metrics as follows. 
 Let 𝒟 = { x i } i = 1 N \mathcal{D}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N} denote a benchmark of N N tasks, where x i x_{i} is the initial prompt for task i i . Let 𝒴 \mathcal{Y} denote the candidate solution space. Each task x i x_{i} is paired with a verifier V i : 𝒴 → { 0 , 1 } V_{i}:\mathcal{Y}\to\{0,1\} indicating whether a candidate y ∈ 𝒴 y\in\mathcal{Y} passes the success requirement. The verifier may be an LLM if the evaluation protocol of the benchmark requires an LLM grader. A model with parameters θ \theta induces a conditional decoding distribution π θ ( ⋅ ∣ c ) \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c) over 𝒴 \mathcal{Y} given an input context c c . We call this model the actor, to distinguish it from the models that write feedback (critics) or grade attempts (graders). 
 Pass@K . 
 Under independent sampling, context is restricted strictly to the original task prompt ( c = x i c=x_{i} ). For each task x i x_{i} , the model independently draws K K candidate solutions Y i , 1 , … , Y i , K ∼ π θ ( ⋅ ∣ x i ) Y_{i,1},\dots,Y_{i,K}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{i}) . A task succeeds if at least one candidate passes the verifier V i V_{i} . The Pass@K metric computes the expected success rate across the benchmark: 
 Pass @ K = 1 N ∑ i = 1 N 𝔼 Y i , 1 , … , Y i , K ∼ π θ ( ⋅ ∣ x i ) [ max 1 ≤ t ≤ K V i ( Y i , t ) ] . \mathrm{Pass@}K=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{i,1},\dots,Y_{i,K}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{i})}\left[\max_{1\leq t\leq K}V_{i}(Y_{i,t})\right]. 
 (1) 
 Because V i ​ ( y ) ∈ { 0 , 1 } V_{i}(y)\in\{0,1\} , the maximum over attempt steps t t evaluates to 1 1 if any attempt succeeds and 0 0 otherwise. 
 Seq@K . 
 Under sequential evaluation, attempts are generated iteratively, allowing the prompt context to expand with past candidate responses and environmental feedback. Let ℱ \mathcal{F} denote the space of feedback messages. A feedback mode m m specifies what feedback message the actor receives after a failed attempt (Section  4 ). The interaction history before attempt t t is H i , t − 1 = ( ( Y i , 1 , F i , 1 ) , … , ( Y i , t − 1 , F i , t − 1 ) ) H_{i,t-1}=\big((Y_{i,1},F_{i,1}),\dots,(Y_{i,t-1},F_{i,t-1})\big) , with H i , 0 = ∅ H_{i,0}=\emptyset . 
 At attempt t ∈ { 1 , … , K } t\in\{1,\dots,K\} , the model samples Y i , t ∼ π θ ( ⋅ ∣ x i , H i , t − 1 ) Y_{i,t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{i},H_{i,t-1}) . If the attempt fails ( V i ​ ( Y i , t ) = 0 V_{i}(Y_{i,t})=0 ), the environment returns feedback F i , t ∈ ℱ F_{i,t}\in\mathcal{F} according to the feedback mode m m , which is appended to H i , t − 1 H_{i,t-1} for the next turn.
If the attempt succeeds ( V i ​ ( Y i , t ) = 1 V_{i}(Y_{i,t})=1 ), interaction terminates early.
We call an attempt with t ≥ 2 t\geq 2 , which is conditioned on earlier attempts and feedback, a revision or a revised attempt.
Let τ i \tau_{i} denote the resulting sequential trajectory, and let P θ , m , i ( K ) ( ⋅ ∣ x i ) P_{\theta,m,i}^{(K)}(\cdot\mid x_{i}) denote the joint distribution over trajectories induced by the model, feedback mode m m , and budget K K . The Seq@K metric evaluates the expected trajectory success rate: 
 Seq @ K = 1 N ∑ i = 1 N 𝔼 τ i ∼ P θ , m , i ( K ) ( ⋅ ∣ x i ) [ max 1 ≤ t ≤ K V i ( Y i , t ) ] . \mathrm{Seq@}K=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{i}\sim P_{\theta,m,i}^{(K)}(\cdot\mid x_{i})}\left[\max_{1\leq t\leq K}V_{i}(Y_{i,t})\right]. 
 (2) 
 When a trajectory halts early upon success, the maximum is evaluated over generated attempts within budget K K . Unlike Pass@K , successive candidate attempts in Seq@K are not independent draws from a static prompt; each revision depends directly on previous attempts and feedback. 
 Feedback Modes and Protocol Dependencies. 
 The Seq@K metric forms a family of feedback-conditioned evaluations indexed by the feedback mode m m . A numerical Seq@K value is well-defined only after specifying feedback mode m m alongside the benchmark task split, verifier, prompt builder, decoding hyperparameters, and attempt budget.

 Feedback modes. 
 We compare independent parallel sampling ( Pass@K ) against three distinct sequential feedback modes, categorized by the signal provided to the actor following a failed attempt: 
 ∙ \bullet 
 Binary Feedback: Provides only an outcome pass/fail verdict. This mode evaluates whether knowing that a candidate failed provides sufficient leverage to guide subsequent revision. 
 ∙ \bullet 
 Raw Verifier Feedback: Returns direct environment or evaluator diagnostics—including execution stack traces, failed test assertions, or rubric item breakdowns. This mode tests whether models can interpret and act upon evaluator diagnostics. 
 ∙ \bullet 
 LLM Critic Feedback: Uses a language model to generate targeted critique from the candidate response and the information supplied to the critic. The critic role can be fulfilled by the actor model itself or another language model. We systematically vary the critic’s context, including whether it has explicit access to underlying grading rubrics. 
 Feedback mode specifies the information boundary maintained during revision rounds. For example, raw test diagnostics can expose test failures and expected outputs, whereas rubric-aware critics use evaluation rubrics to generate guidance for the actor. 
 Having established the benchmark coverage, feedback channels, and evaluation protocols, we next present our empirical findings on how Seq@K reshapes model selection, exposes revision dynamics, and quantifies feedback utility across these systems. 
 5 Results 
 We structure our empirical investigation around three core questions: (1) Does evaluating models under sequential feedback alter model selection and cost–performance trade-offs? (2) How do revisions unfold across successive feedback turns? (3) What properties of the feedback channel determine whether an attempt succeeds or stalls? 
 5.1 Model-selection under Seq@K

 The cost of selecting a worse actors
depends on how far apart the candidate actors are in performance (Table  3 ).
On AdvancedIF, the gap between the best and worst actor is 33.3 33.3 points under Pass@5 but only 6.7 6.7 points under Seq@5 with raw verifier feedback.

 5.1.4 Seq@K tracks a distinct capability signal from Pass@K 
 The previous results show the operational implications of Seq@K for choosing models.
We complement them with a more direct study of how Seq@K tracks a different capability from Pass@K .
At first glance, the two metrics are somewhat correlated.
Over 26 26 model–benchmark pairs, Pass@5 and Seq@5 have Spearman correlation + 0.68 +0.68 (Figure  4 a ).
This is because the two protocols have approximately the same success rate on the first attempt, which reflects the actor’s one-shot capability on the task.
To control for this, we measure instead how much attempts 2 to 5 add under each protocol: 
 Δ ​ Pass@K = Pass@K − Pass@1 , Δ ​ Seq@K = Seq@K − Seq@1 . {\Delta\text{Pass@K}=\text{Pass@K}-\text{Pass@1},\qquad\Delta\text{Seq@K}=\text{Seq@K}-\text{Seq@1}.} 
 With the first attempt removed, the correlation drops to − 0.08 -0.08 (Figure  4 b ).
That is, how much a model gains from more independent samples says little about how much it gains from feedback.
Hence, the iterative refinement capability that Seq@K evaluation aims to track is distinct from what Seq@K captures.

 5.2 Seq@K reveals when and how revisions succeed 
 The numerical results on success rates earlier do not show when revisions succeed or how an actor responds to feedback.
But the rate at which agents’ revisions succeed and how well the revisions address the feedback directly impacts the usability of agentic systems.
In this section, we examine when revised attempts yield gains and failure modes in agents’ revision patterns. 
 5.2.1 Revision timing distinguishes front-loaded from distributed gains 
 Models with similar Seq@5 can follow distinct revision trajectories across turns. This profile matters when deployment limits the number of revision turns. To characterize this behavior, we analyze how performance improvements distribute across revision turns. For any model exhibiting net improvement ( Δ ​ Seq@5 > 0 \Delta\text{Seq@5}>0 ), we compute the Early-Gain Share (EGS) and Last-Gain Share (LGS): 
 EGS = Seq@2 − Seq@1 Δ ​ Seq@5 , LGS = Seq@5 − Seq@4 Δ ​ Seq@5 . {\mathrm{EGS}=\frac{\text{Seq@2}-\text{Seq@1}}{\Delta\text{Seq@5}},\qquad\mathrm{LGS}=\frac{\text{Seq@5}-\text{Seq@4}}{\Delta\text{Seq@5}}.} 
 The early-gain share (EGS) is the fraction of improvement obtained on the first retry. The last-step share (LGS) is the fraction obtained on the final allowed attempt. 
 We evaluate these metrics using rubric-guided DKR setups across two distinct critic styles: directive feedback (providing explicit actionable revision instructions) and socratic feedback (providing targeted probing questions). On the other hand, a non-zero LGS like that of Opus 4.7 or DeepSeek V4 Pro indicates potential gains from more repeated attempts.

 5.2.2 Revisions do not always address the feedback 
 We next examine whether provided feedback are addressed in agents’ next attempts.
Some unhelpful revisions are easy to identify since the next attempt entails the same answer as before with no changes.
In coding, we find such examples with Qwen3 Coder Flash and DeepSeek V3.2:
 48 % 48\% and 6 % 6\% of revised attempts respectively are exactly the same attempt as before without modifications, whereas Sonnet 4.6 and Gemini 3 Flash never submit an unmodified revised attempt (Figure  5 a ). 
 Other unhelpful revisions update the attempts but nonetheless fail to address the feedback.
Unlike the aforementioned failure mode that can be programmatically identified, finding cases where revisions took place but are ineffective takes manual inspection.
Below, we show 2 of the 14 examples we identify (Examples  5.2.2 and  5.2.2 ) and more in Appendix  E .

For each feedback, the annotator judges whether the revised attempt satisfies it.

Across 842 842 revisions, we find that 186 186 ( 22 % 22\% ) are unhelpful, 289 289 ( 34 % 34\% ) address some, and 367 367 ( 44 % 44\% ) address all of the feedback (Appendix  D.2 ).

 5.3 Seq@K diagnoses the feedback loop 
 So far, we examined how agents perform under Seq@K and respond to feedback.
In this section, we look at the feedback itself: what information it contains, whether the critic knows what is being graded, and how much of the previous attempts the agent sees. 
 5.3.1 Effectiveness of binary feedback depends on the benchmark 
 Binary feedback only tells the agent that its attempt failed, without saying which requirements are not met.
In DKR, binary feedback is largely unhelpful.
To measure this, we take the requirements that the first attempt fails and measure the fraction of them fixed in the best later attempt (the attempt that satisfies the most requirements, taking the latest one if tied). We call this fraction criterion repair .
In Table  5 , we compare independent retries, binary feedback, and critiques written with and without access to the grading rubric.
Binary feedback and critiques without the rubric repair about as many criteria as independent retries, whereas critiques with the rubric repair many more.
Binary feedback is similarly unhelpful for DeepSeek V3.2 and Qwen3 Coder Flash in coding, where it changes Seq@5 by at most 3.3 3.3 points from Pass@5 (Appendix  F.2 ). 
 On the other hand, binary feedback is helpful for Sonnet 4.6 in coding, since Sonnet can reason on its own about why its code failed when it was run.
Compared to Pass@5 , Seq@5 with binary feedback is 17 17 to 26 26 points higher for Sonnet on HumanEval Pro and MBPP Pro (Appendix  F.2 ), whereas in DKR it is only 1 1 point higher on average across six models.
Overall, this shows that effectiveness of different feedback type is dependent on both benchmark and model-capability.
Therefore, in designing feedback harnesses around models, practitioners may benefit more from highlighting criteria that agents would otherwise not come up on their own from reasoning.

 6 Discussion and Conclusion 
 We introduced Seq@K , which measures whether a model solves a task within K K attempts when it receives feedback after each failed attempt.
Model rankings, gaps between actors, and cost-performance frontiers can all differ between Seq@K and Pass@K , so choosing a model by Pass@K can pick the wrong one for an agent that revises from feedback.
 Seq@K trajectories also show when models gain and how revisions fail, for example by ignoring the feedback or by breaking requirements that passed before.
Finally, Seq@K helps diagnose the feedback loop itself.
We find how much feedback helps depends on what it tells the agent, whether the critic knows what is being graded, and how the history is kept.
Practitioners building agentic systems around a feedback loop should choose models with Seq@K under the feedback the agent will receive, since Pass@K may favor a model that does not use that feedback well.

 A.1 Feedback, history, and grading 
 Feedback. 
 Binary feedback tells the actor only that its attempt failed. Raw verifier feedback on AdvancedIF gives the verdict for each rubric item. Rubric-aware critics see the grading rubric when they write feedback, and rubric-blind critics do not. In coding, feedback either says that the code failed (binary) or shows the failed tests (test details), and Appendix  A.2 gives the exact text. Raw verifier feedback and test details show the actor part of what is graded, so Seq@K under these modes measures success with that information. 
 History. 
 By default, the prompt for each sequential attempt contains the task, a note on the attempt number, and every earlier attempt with its feedback. In the summarized-history runs, a summary replaces the earlier attempts (Appendix  F.3 ). 
 Temperature and repetitions. 
 The main comparisons use T = 0.7 T=0.7 , and we do not tune the temperature. Coding runs are repeated three times, labeled seeds 1–3. Some coding runs in the history comparison use T = 0 T=0 . 
 Grading. 
 Unless stated otherwise, AdvancedIF attempts are graded by Sonnet 4.6. CLBench attempts are graded by GPT-5.4, with GPT-5.2 as a fallback when GPT-5.4 refuses to grade. 
 Confidence intervals. 
 We compute 95 % 95\% intervals by resampling tasks with replacement. Each resampled task keeps the results of every actor and protocol on it, so both sides of a comparison are always computed on the same tasks. Coding scores are averaged over the three repetitions before resampling. The model-selection analysis resamples tasks within each calibration and evaluation group (Appendix  B.1 ).

 B.1 Selection test 
 Runs. 
 The candidates are Sonnet 4.6, DeepSeek V3.2, Gemini 3 Flash, and Qwen3 Coder Flash.  Selection and loss. 
 On each set of calibration tasks, we score every actor by its success averaged over the three repetitions and pick the highest, breaking ties at random. The picked actor is evaluated by its test-detail Seq@5 on the evaluation tasks, averaged over the three repetitions.  The evaluation tasks are never used for picking. The loss is zero when the picked actor is the best on E E , and it can be positive even under Seq@5 calibration, because the best actor on C C need not be the best on E E .  Cost. 
 The calibration cost is the sum of the actor API charges over the four actors, the calibration tasks, and the three repetitions. Coding has no judge or critic, so this is the full model cost. Under both protocols, a trajectory is charged up to its first success or its last allowed attempt. The cost does not include running the tests. We report the mean cost and do not cap the spending on any single calibration set. 
 Calibration and evaluation groups. 
 We split MBPP Pro into groups of 22 22 and 23 23 tasks and HumanEval Pro into two groups of 20 20 . We calibrate on one group, evaluate on the whole other group, and then swap. In each direction, we draw 128,000 128{,}000 random orders of the calibration tasks and use the first n n tasks of each order, so every calibration metric sees the same tasks. We chose this number by increasing it from 2,000 2{,}000 until two independent sets of draws agreed on the gain of Seq@3 over Pass@5 at n = 10 n=10 within 0.1 0.1 points. The two directions are averaged, weighted by the size of their evaluation group.

Each resample draws tasks within each group and keeps the results of every actor, protocol, and repetition on a task together. On each resample, we rerun the selection over 200 200 random orders of the calibration tasks in each direction. Using 400 400 orders instead changes the interval endpoints by at most 0.3 0.3 points.  D.2 Annotation of revisions 
 This section gives the details of the revision annotation in Section  5.2.2 : which revisions we annotate, how the annotator labels them, and how often two annotators agree. 
 Settings. 
 We reuse recorded runs from four settings (Table  13 ). Each setting fixes the feedback, critic, grader, temperature ( T = 0.7 T=0.7 ), and history, so actors within a setting differ only in the actor model and seed. A revision is one step from attempt t t , through the feedback on it, to attempt t + 1 t{+}1 . A trajectory is included if its first attempt failed. 
 Task split. 
 Tasks were split at random (seed 20260925 20260925 ), so every run and attempt of a task falls on one side. The prompt was developed on the 14 14 audited examples (Appendix  E ) and 11 11 random tasks ( 4 4 AdvancedIF, 3 3 DKR, 3 3 HumanEval Pro, 1 1 MBPP Pro). The two AdvancedIF settings share 16 16 of their held-out tasks, giving 102 102 distinct tasks: 60 60 AdvancedIF, 23 23 DKR, 11 11 HumanEval Pro, and 8 8 MBPP Pro. For coding we annotate 20 20 random tasks out of 100 100 ; four of them have interface defects (Appendix  A.3 ). Figure  5 b excludes the 62 62 revisions on those four tasks and the 131 131 revisions whose feedback contains no valid request, leaving 842 842 .

 Annotation. 
 For each revision the annotator sees the task prompt, the rubric text, earlier attempts and feedback, attempt t t , the feedback on it, attempt t + 1 t{+}1 , and a line diff. The actor, run, and all verdicts are hidden, and an annotation batch contains at most one revision per trajectory. The annotator lists each change the feedback requests and labels it valid, interpretive, unclear, or invalid (for example, a test that contradicts the prompt). It then records whether attempt t + 1 t{+}1 satisfies each request (yes, partly, or no), with a verbatim quote as evidence. A request also counts as satisfied when attempt t t already met it and attempt t + 1 t{+}1 still does. Whether a revision is unhelpful, addresses some, or addresses all of the feedback is computed from these labels after dropping invalid requests. 
 To develop the prompt, Claude Opus and Claude Sonnet independently labeled 68 68 revisions from the development tasks ( 16 16 random revisions per setting and the two example AdvancedIF trajectories). The prompt was revised where they disagreed, then frozen before any held-out revision was annotated and before any verdict after attempt 1 was read. 
 Reliability. 
 Claude Sonnet independently annotated a stratified 15 % 15\% of held-out revisions ( 165 165 ). The two annotators agree on this category for 80 % 80\% of them ( κ = 0.73 \kappa=0.73 ); agreement is lowest in DKR ( κ = 0.53 \kappa=0.53 ). A third blind Claude Opus pass resolved the 69 69 disagreements; using its labels leaves the conclusions unchanged. All 2,029 2{,}029 non-empty evidence quotes occur verbatim in the revised attempt. These are LLM labels, not human annotations, and three of the actors are Claude models. 
 Broken criteria. 
 For the counts of revisions that address all feedback yet fail, a criterion counts as broken if it passed at attempt t t , fails at attempt t + 1 t{+}1 , and no annotated request refers to it. Requests the annotator did not tie to a rubric item are treated as unmentioned, so these counts may slightly overstate breakage. Intervals in Figure  5 b are 95 % 95\% percentiles over 4,000 4{,}000 resamples of tasks within each setting.
