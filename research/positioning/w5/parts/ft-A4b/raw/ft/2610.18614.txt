 Can an Agent Learn to Predict Its Own Failure?
 Outcome-Grounded Confidence Calibration in LLM Agents 
 Ashutosh Hathidara
 Affiliation:  SAP
 Email:  ashutosh.hathidara@sap.com 
 Abstract 
 Controlling an acting LLM agent means deciding, at each step of a long-horizon task, whether to trust its next action, abstain, or hand off to a human. That decision needs a per-step confidence signal predicting whether the current rollout will eventually succeed. The agent’s own self-reported verbalized confidence is the natural candidate, but existing reinforcement learning methods calibrate it against the correctness of a decision at the current step. We propose a method to calibrate it against the eventual outcome of a multi-step episode: under GRPO, the group of rollouts for a task supplies success labels, and a proper scoring rule scores each step’s confidence against the realized outcome. In offline RL on recorded trajectories of a Qwen2.5-7B agent in ALFWorld, TextWorld, and WebShop environments, the AUROC of step confidence against episode outcome rises from near chance to 0.68–0.85 , and the top-10% of steps the agent is most confident about succeed ∼ \sim 4.3 × \times more often than average. In online RL on the live ALFWorld agent, four calibration rewards improve early-step AUROC, expected calibration error goes from 0.42 to below 0.12 , and selective execution improves over both the untrained agent and a format-only RL control, without degrading task success. We also introduce an evaluation protocol that separates episode depth, task difficulty, and attempt-level foresight, which shows where the gain lies: the trained agent recognizes from its first step which tasks it will fail, while telling a succeeding attempt from a failing one at the same task is harder, with some early-window signal emerging on TextWorld. 
 1 Introduction 
 Acting LLM agents increasingly operate in long-horizon, partially observable environments ( Shridhar et al., 2021 ; Yao et al., 2022 ; Côté et al., 2019 ; Shridhar et al., 2020 ; Hausknecht et al., 2020 ; Wang et al., 2022 ; Zhou et al., 2024 ; Liu et al., 2024 ) , where a single episode spans dozens of actions under compounding uncertainty. Controlling such an agent at runtime requires an estimate of the probability that the current attempt will succeed, so that it can continue with the action, verify with observation, or escalate to a human  ( Kadavath et al., 2022 ) . Such a probability estimate can work as a confidence signal that predicts whether the current rollout will ultimately reach the goal, not whether the current step is locally correct. 
 The natural candidate is a self-reported verbalized confidence of an agent. Without additional focused training, smaller LLMs are poorly calibrated and barely predictive (§ 6 ). Previous works on Reinforcement Learning (RL) for calibration ( Damani et al., 2025 ; Bani-Harouni et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026b ; Xuan et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) focus on training confidence to predict either a single answer or step-level correctness. Such greedy optimization signals train the model to be locally predictive, promising no predictiveness for eventual outcome. 
 We propose a methodology to train a step-wise confidence signal that could predict the eventual outcome of the task. Under group-relative policy optimization (GRPO), the rollouts for a task supply success labels at no extra cost. We define the per-step reward as a proper scoring rule on the step confidence against the realized outcome, with other reward terms. We conduct offline & online RL training. Our evaluation protocol separates what the aggregate score mixes (§ 3.2 ). 
 Our approach reveals that outcome-grounded calibration can turn a weak agent’s confidence into a reliable early signal of eventual outcome, consistently across environments and training regimes. Our evaluation protocol then reveals precisely where this new ability comes from, and where it does not. 
 In summary, our contributions include: (i) Outcome-grounded calibration training under GRPO, (ii) an evaluation protocol for episodic confidence, and (iii) an empirical analysis of what an LLM learns during such a training process. 
 2 Related Work 
 Measuring and eliciting confidence. 
 A broad line of work reads a confidence out of a model without additional training: verbalized probabilities ( Lin et al., 2022 ; Tian et al., 2023 ) , self-consistency and self-evaluation ( Kadavath et al., 2022 ) , and elicitation strategies for black-box models ( Xiong et al., 2024 ) . A related line of work documents that post-hoc calibration of neural classifiers degrades with scale and training ( Guo et al., 2017 ) . These works primarily target synchronous single-answer correctness, where confidence degrades on weak models, precisely the area we target. 
 Reinforcement learning for confidence. 
 The most closely related work trains calibration. RLCR ( Damani et al., 2025 ) optimizes a Brier reward for verbalized confidence, and related efforts tie confidence to reward shaping, uncertainty, or abstention in non-agentic settings ( Bani-Harouni et al., 2026 ; Xu et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ; Leng et al., 2025 ; Yaldiz et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2026a ) . All optimize the calibration of a single synchronous output ; none targets the eventual success of an agent through a long-horizon task. 
 Positioning against concurrent agentic methods. 
 Three concurrent methods are closest. CAR/MSCR ( Xuan et al., 2026 ) and CAPO ( Wang et al., 2026b ) train confidence in tool-use and reasoning settings, and RLCR ( Damani et al., 2025 ) in single-answer QA; all optimize confidence against the synchronous correctness of the current answer or step, whereas we target the predictive success of the eventual rollout. They differ from us in how the signal is produced and rewarded: CAPO reads a perplexity-derived confidence and reshapes the advantage estimator through a consistency-and-regret construction, and MSCR uses a margin reward, while we train an explicit verbalized token under a lightweight per-completion calibration reward. 
 Selective prediction. 
 Our governance mechanism is an instance of selective prediction: we evaluate it with risk-coverage curves ( Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017 ; Kamath et al., 2020 ) and train the signal with classical proper scoring rules ( BRIER, 1950 ; Gneiting and Raftery, 2007 ) . Closest in spirit, belief-uncertainty agents such as Singh et al. (2026) estimate an agent’s internal belief rather than its outcome-predictive confidence; we distinguish the two in § 4 . 
 3 Problem Setup and Evaluation Protocol 
 We first state what a per-step confidence should predict (§ 3.1 ), then define the family of estimands that separates genuine foresight from the depth and difficulty confounds a pooled score mixes together (§ 3.2 ), and finally fix the calibration, selective-execution, and statistical conventions used throughout (§ 3.3 ). 
 3.1 Predictive Confidence Target 
 We model an acting agent as a partially observable decision process with policy π θ \pi_{\theta} . At step t t the policy observes history h t h_{t} (the prompt, prior actions, and environment feedback) and emits an action together with an inline confidence, ( a t , c t ) ∼ π θ ( ⋅ ∣ h t ) (a_{t},c_{t})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{t}) , where c t ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] c_{t}\in[0,1] is parsed from a <conf>NN</conf> tag (an integer 0 0 – 100 100 rescaled to [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] ; a missing or malformed tag defaults to a neutral 0.5 0.5 ). A full rollout τ \tau terminates and receives a binary success label Y ∈ { 0 , 1 } Y\in\{0,1\} ( Y = 1 Y{=}1 iff the task is solved). 
 We define the predictive-calibration target : c t c_{t} should track 
 c t ≈ P ⁡ ( Y = 1 ∣ h t ) , c_{t}\;\approx\;P(Y=1\mid h_{t}), 
 (1) 
 the probability that this episode ultimately succeeds given the history so far. We target eventual success rather than the synchronous P ⁡ ( current step correct ) P(\text{current step correct}) used by prior work. Long-horizon agentic tasks admit many valid solution paths, so multiple actions may be correct at each step; local correctness is thus ill-defined, and only the eventual outcome matters as a label. 
 3.2 Estimands 
 Task s s has attempts i = 1 , … , G i=1,\dots,G with outcomes Y s , i Y_{s,i} and step confidences c s , i , t c_{s,i,t} , every step inheriting its attempt’s outcome. A step’s confidence can rank correctly for three unrelated reasons: (i) the task is easy, (ii) the attempt is going well, or (iii) the episode is near its end. A single pooled metric conflates all three, so we report four estimands , each an AUROC against episode success—the probability that a step of a succeeding attempt is higher than a step of a failing one—differing only in which steps may be compared. We evaluate over all steps, the early window ( k ≤ 2 k\leq 2 , steps 0–2), and step 0 alone. 
 Pooled AUROC compares any success-step with any failure-step and holds none of the confounds fixed. Between-task AUROC collapses each task to its mean confidence, leaving only task difficulty (i). Within-task AUROC compares succeeding against failing attempts of the same task, cancelling difficulty to isolate attempt-level foresight (ii). Depth-matched within-task AUROC compares them only at the same step index, so neither difficulty nor depth contributes and only foresight survives (iii). Against each we report a depth-only model-free baseline scoring every step by − t -t : over all steps it can match or beat a real signal, but the early window and step 0 drive it to chance, so any gain there reflects foresight rather than position. At step 0 every attempt sees the same observation, so a gain can arise only from ranking tasks or from the first sampled action, before any environment feedback. All formal definitions appear in Appendix  A . 
 3.3 Calibration & Selective Execution 
 We report two kinds of metrics. For calibration , how well confidence matches outcome frequency: Expected Calibration Error (ECE) with 10 equal-width bins ( Guo et al., 2017 ) , and the Brier score ( BRIER, 1950 ) . For selective execution , how useful confidence is for gating actions: the success rate among the top- q q % most confident steps in a window ( q = 10 , 25 q=10,25 ), and task-level triage (the success rate when only the top 50% of tasks by mean early confidence are attempted). Online intervals are 95% task-level cluster bootstraps. Offline intervals are 95% bootstraps over steps; steps of one trajectory share a label, so these intervals understate uncertainty. Paired deltas resample the tasks shared by both runs under comparison; within-task deltas use only the tasks that are mixed-outcome in both arms, a small subset, so these intervals rest on few clusters and are correspondingly wide. Tables  4 and 5 and the appendix report ∼ \sim 100 paired deltas. 
 4 Methodology 
 Our method turns the rollouts that GRPO already samples into a training signal for confidence. The G G attempts at a task each end in an outcome that labels every one of their steps, so a step can be scored by how well its stated confidence matches the attempt outcome it belongs to. We assemble this into three pieces: a group-relative advantage that credits a step for both the eventual outcome and the quality of its confidence (§ 4.1 ), a per-step reward built from a proper scoring rule that supplies the confidence term, and an optional spread term that keeps confidence away from the endpoints of the scale (§ 4.2 ). The same signal drives training on recorded offline trajectories and on the live agent alike (§ 4.3 ).

 4.1 Group-Relative Training with Outcome 
 GRPO ( Shao et al., 2024 ; Guo et al., 2025 ) samples G G rollouts of one task and forms advantages relative to the group mean. In an episodic setting the realized outcomes Y i Y_{i} of the group are the labels of Eq. ( 1 ) at no extra cost. Online, the advantage of every step is 
 A s , i , t = ( Y s , i − Y ¯ s ) + ( r s , i , t − r ¯ s ) , A_{s,i,t}=\big(Y_{s,i}-\bar{Y}_{s}\big)+\big(r_{s,i,t}-\bar{r}_{s}\big), 
 (2) 
 where Y ¯ s = 1 G ​ ∑ i Y s , i \bar{Y}_{s}=\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i}Y_{s,i} is the group’s success rate, r ¯ s \bar{r}_{s} the group mean of the per-step rewards, and r s , i , t r_{s,i,t} is the per-step reward below. We also ablate two alternative optimization strategies against this GRPO objective (Section  6.1 ): RLOO ( Ahmadian et al., 2024 ) , a leave-one-out variant that baselines each rollout against the mean of the others rather than the full group, and PPO with a learned value head, a non-group-relative control that replaces the group baseline with a learned critic.

 4.3 Training Strategies 
 We use the standard distinction between offline RL, which trains on a fixed dataset of recorded trajectories without interacting with the environment, and online RL, which trains the agent on the trajectories it generates in the live environment (on-policy). 
 Offline Training. 
 The offline route runs GRPO on (prefix → \to action + <conf> ) rows cut from one recorded untrained Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct ( Yang et al., 2024 ) trajectory per game, each prefix labeled with that trajectory’s outcome. The per-step reward is the weighted composite 
 r off = 0.5 ​ a + 0.2 ​ e + w cal ​ cal ​ ( c , Y ) + w φ ​ φ ​ ( c ) , r^{\text{off}}=0.5\,a+0.2\,e+w_{\text{cal}}\,\mathrm{cal}(c,Y)+w_{\varphi}\,\varphi(c), 
 (5) 
 where a ∈ { 0 , 1 } a\in\{0,1\} indicates that the sampled action matches the recorded one, e e and cal ⁡ ( c , Y ) \mathrm{cal}(c,Y) are the format and calibration terms of Eq. ( 3 ), w cal = 0.3 w_{\text{cal}}=0.3 by default, and the spread weight w φ w_{\varphi} is nonzero only when regularization is enabled (Appendix  I ). The policy never acts on its own outputs. Because all eight generations for a prefix share the recorded outcome as their label, the group supplies no label contrast, the action-match term rewards reproducing the recorded action, and trained confidence is scored on recorded prefixes of the untrained policy. The reward-form arms of Table  2 use w cal = 1.0 w_{\text{cal}}=1.0 in place of the 0.3 default. 
 Online Training. 
 The online route instead trains the live agent on its own rollouts for 120 iterations under Eq. ( 2 ); each iteration samples a batch of 4 tasks from the training pool and performs 6 rollouts per task (the GRPO group, G = 6 G=6 ), with learning rate 10 − 6 10^{-6} , clip 0.2, and KL coefficient 10 − 3 10^{-3} . Because each offline rollout contributes a single trajectory, the within-task estimands can be measured only on the online route. 
 5 Experimental Setup 
 Environments and models. 
 We use ALFWorld, TextWorld, and WebShop, each with binary episode success, and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct ( Yang et al., 2024 ) trained with LoRA ( Hu et al., 2022 ) at rank 32 and α = 64 \alpha=64 . We also perform experiments on a different-sized model Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, with the same training configurations. The prompt asks for one admissible action on the first line and, on the next line, the confidence that this episode will ultimately succeed as <conf>NN</conf> (details in Appendix  I ). 
 Data. 
 Balanced training data hold ALFWorld 1,068 rows from 200 games, TextWorld 943 from 200, and WebShop 1,066 from 372. For comparison, we train the model on each environment independently for 150 steps. 
 Training ablations. 
 We train a format-only baseline ( w cal = w φ = 0 w_{\text{cal}}=w_{\varphi}=0 ) to compare against four calibration experiments: Brier+ φ \varphi ( w cal = 1 w_{\text{cal}}=1 , w φ = 0.10 w_{\varphi}=0.10 ), balanced Brier, Brier+dispersion, and balanced+dispersion. Every calibration experiment carries a spread or dispersion term at w φ = 0.10 w_{\varphi}=0.10 , so there is no pure-Brier online arm, and each online arm uses a single training seed. Three things were fixed in advance: (i) the estimand (within-task AUROC at k ≤ 2 k\leq 2 ), (ii) the checkpoint rule for the balanced, dispersion, and balanced+dispersion arms (validation argmax over checkpoints { 40 , 80 , 120 } \{40,80,120\} ), and (iii) the evaluation criterion. 
 Baselines. 
 We compare against untrained and a format reward-only trained model variants. We also compare against two white-box signals available offline: negative mean token entropy and sequence log-probability. Finally, we compare against a depth-only model-free baseline and a depth + length texture regression, which capture what episode position & output length alone predict.

 6 Results 
 We first establish offline RL experiments, across all three environments, that outcome-grounded training makes verbalized confidence predictive of eventual episode success (§ 6.1 ); we then show that the effect carries over to the live agent trained online in ALFWorld, and transfers to a second live environment, TextWorld (§ 6.2 ). We then analyze the trained signal (§ 7 ). 
 6.1 Offline RL Training 
 Each offline training sample is a single recorded trajectory, so the within and between-task estimands of Section  3.2 cannot be formed; thus we report the pooled estimand with the depth-and-length controls it demands. Outcome-grounded training makes verbalized confidence predict eventual success in every environment (Table  1 ): pooled AUROC rises from 0.517, 0.578, and 0.462 to 0.681, 0.722, and 0.850, and success in the most-confident decile from 1.1–1.6 × \times to about 4.3 × \times the base rate (intervals and per-environment lifts in Appendix  G ). 
 Before training, a free white-box signal is competitive. The untrained model’s negated mean token entropy predicts success more accurately than its verbalized confidence in all three environments, and retains most of this power after residualizing on step depth and output length (Appendix  G ). Training inverts the comparison. After calibration, the verbalized confidence exceeds the token entropy in every environment—most sharply on TextWorld (0.722 vs. 0.399) and WebShop (0.850 vs. 0.430)—because the mean content-token entropy falls roughly sixfold as the policy sharpens and the entropy predictor degrades, where the token distribution grows most confident on the steps that fail. 
 The pooled confidence AUROC is partly explained by trajectory depth. A predictor built from step depth and output length alone, with no access to the confidence token, reaches AUROC 0.787, 0.855, and 0.799, matching or exceeding the trained confidence AUROC on ALFWorld and TextWorld though not on WebShop (Appendix  G ). A depth-independent component of the confidence nonetheless does not replicate the same result: residualizing the confidence on depth and output length leaves AUROC 0.626, 0.677, and 0.811, whereas the same residualization reduces the untrained model’s confidence to 0.534, 0.479, and 0.509, near chance. The depth-independent signal is thus a product of training. Because the pooled estimand cannot separate confidence from depth, we evaluate the depth-matched and within-task estimands in the online setting, which supports them (Section  6.2 ). 
 The presence of a calibration term matters more than its functional form (Table  2 ). Six reward forms—spherical, Brier, ranking, L1, logarithmic, and decision—yield overlapping AUROC between 0.820 and 0.882, statistically indistinguishable, whereas training with the task reward alone reaches only 0.559, close to the untrained 0.502. The choice of advantage estimator, by contrast, is consequential (Table  3 ): under a matched ranking-plus-spread reward, RLOO attains 0.801 and 0.876 against 0.613 and 0.676 for GRPO, and PPO with a cold-start value critic fails to learn (0.500 across two seeds) parsable response format.

 7 Analysis of the Trained Signal 
 Results in § 6 show that training improves confidence. As part of this section, we ask what it changes. We decompose the online gain by estimand to locate where the improvement comes from (§ 7.1 ), trace it back to the first step of each episode (§ 7.2 ), and benchmark it against depth and white-box signals (§ 7.3 ) before drawing out what it means for an agent controller (§ 7.4 ). 
 7.1 Decomposition 
 Training’s gain is specifically between-task: it sharpens which tasks the agent will succeed at, not how it reads individual attempts (Table  5 ; Appendix Figure  6 ). Between-task early-window AUROC rises for every calibration arm (0.825 to 0.848–0.897), and only Brier+dispersion’s + 0.072 +0.072 [.029,.115] is significant. The localization suggests: the within-task estimand does not move (0.606 untrained against 0.585–0.661; paired point estimates − 0.026 -0.026 to − 0.092 -0.092 ), so the measured improvement is concentrated in task ranking, and no experiment met the pre-registered within-task criterion of § 5 . Consistent with this, the within-task all-step estimates match format-only experiment (format-only − 0.077 -0.077 , balanced+dispersion − 0.072 -0.072 ) and similar to the depth-confounded estimand of § 3.2 . Relative to the format-only control, the calibration term does incur gain on early-window within-task discrimination ( + 0.062 +0.062 [-.034,.177] for Brier+ φ \varphi , + 0.127 +0.127 [.062,.189] for Brier+dispersion), while the depth-matched within-task deltas are not significant (Appendix  C ). 
 Pooled and between-task early-window values move together under training (0.689/0.825 untrained to 0.761/0.862 for Brier+ φ \varphi ; details in Appendix  C ), while task-level AUROC, triage deltas, and within-task deltas show no significant gains. One reading consistent with all four estimands is that training sharpens and brings foresight forward to step 0, the task ranking the untrained agent already expresses in the early window (§ 7.2 ). 
 7.2 Foresight at Step 0 
 Training brings this task ranking forward to the very first step. Pooled step-0 AUROC rises from 0.562 [.530,.600] to 0.642–0.700 across the four calibration arms, even though the confidence is emitted after the sampled first action but before any environment feedback. The signal is task-level: within-task step-0 AUROC stays at chance (0.450–0.602, every CI spanning 0.5), so the agent is ranking which tasks it will solve rather than grading its first action . The untrained baseline confirms this is genuine improvement: at greedy decoding it emits exactly 0.50 at step 0 on every task of a 64-task probe (Appendix  B ), so its sampled 0.562 is noise around a near-constant value, and the task ranking it expresses (between-task 0.825) emerges only after the first feedback. The mechanism follows from Eq. ( 2 ): uniform-outcome groups, which still arise by sampling despite mixed-outcome task selection, carry no success/failure contrast, so their gradient moves a task’s confidence toward its shared outcome at every step, step 0 included. 
 7.3 Depth and White-Box Baselines 
 The depth-only baseline beats confidence on pooled all-step AUROC and matches or beats every trained experiment, yet collapses to 0.499–0.500 in the early window, whereas the early-window and depth-matched estimands are essential for a decent controller (Figure  5 ; per-size & per-experiment values in Appendix  B ,  C ). Token entropy is the strongest competitor for offline training; the online experiments comparison has no white-box competitor. 
 7.4 Confidence as a Triage Signal 
 Outcome-grounded RL makes verbalized confidence a calibrated early triage signal: the 10% most confident early steps succeed at 0.56–0.66 against 0.49 untrained and a base rate of 0.20, with ECE 0.05–0.12 (Figure  3 ). The gain is earlier recognition of which tasks the agent will fail (§ 7.2 ); attempt-level foresight does not improve on ALFWorld, though it emerges in the early window on TextWorld (§ 6.2 ). Among the most confident steps (the top 10%), higher confidence does not reliably mean higher success (Figure  3 ), and over all steps the most-confident bin is overconfident (Figure  2 ), so a controller should rely on a band of high confidence rather than the very highest values alone. Finally, the within-task question needs training signals that contrast attempts at the same task. 
 8 Conclusion 
 We introduce a training method that calibrates a language agent’s verbalized confidence by rewarding it against eventual task outcomes under GRPO. On a live multi-turn agent it turns an unreliable confidence token into a usable early-triage signal without hurting task success, and a decomposition shows the gain is between tasks: the agent learns earlier which tasks it will fail, not which attempts. 
 Limitations 
 Our evidence is strongest as a between-task effect: on the live ALFWorld agent, training sharpens which tasks the agent will succeed at, and establishing the same signal at the attempt level more broadly is left to future work, as the within-task estimands move only in the early window on TextWorld and not yet on ALFWorld. Our positive live multi-turn results span two text-based environments (ALFWorld and TextWorld) at the 7B scale, and a larger 14B agent corroborates the pooled effect in Appendix  E . Our live WebShop results are inconclusive: held-out success stays low for both arms, leaving too few informative held-out tasks to assess the signal (Appendix  D ); extending the live study to additional model families is left to future work.

 We conducted experiments within the provisions of the ACL Ethics Policy and relevant research-integrity guidelines. There are, to the best of our knowledge, no remaining ethical risks that have not been addressed.

 Appendix A Estimand Definitions 
 This appendix gives the formal definitions of the four estimands and the depth-only baseline introduced in § 3.2 . All five are the same AUROC statistic applied to different comparison sets and score transforms, so any difference between them is attributable to the confound each one cancels, not to a change of metric. 
 Notation. 
 A task s s is attempted i = 1 , … , G i=1,\dots,G times. Attempt ( s , i ) (s,i) has a binary episode outcome Y s , i ∈ { 0 , 1 } Y_{s,i}\in\{0,1\} (success or failure) and emits a step confidence c s , i , t ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] c_{s,i,t}\in[0,1] at each decision step t = 0 , 1 , … t=0,1,\dots ; every step inherits its attempt’s outcome, so step ( s , i , t ) (s,i,t) carries the label Y s , i Y_{s,i} . A window W ⊆ ℤ ≥ 0 W\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} is a set of step indices that restricts which steps are compared; we report results for three, W all = ℤ ≥ 0 = { 0 , 1 , 2 , … } W_{\mathrm{all}}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}=\{0,1,2,\dots\} (all steps), W k ≤ 2 = { 0 , 1 , 2 } W_{k\leq 2}=\{0,1,2\} (the early window), and W 0 = { 0 } W_{0}=\{0\} (step 0 alone). Write 𝒮 W = { ( s , i , t ) : t ∈ W , c s , i , t  emitted } \mathcal{S}_{W}=\{(s,i,t):t\in W,\ c_{s,i,t}\text{ emitted}\} for the steps in play, and partition them by outcome into positives 𝒫 W = { ( s , i , t ) ∈ 𝒮 W : Y s , i = 1 } \mathcal{P}_{W}=\{(s,i,t)\in\mathcal{S}_{W}:Y_{s,i}=1\} and negatives 𝒩 W = { ( s , i , t ) ∈ 𝒮 W : Y s , i = 0 } \mathcal{N}_{W}=\{(s,i,t)\in\mathcal{S}_{W}:Y_{s,i}=0\} . 
 The AUROC statistic. 
 Fix a set of items each carrying a real-valued score and a binary label. Thresholding the score at τ \tau calls an item positive when its score exceeds τ \tau ; sweeping τ \tau traces the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of the true-positive rate TPR ⁡ ( τ ) = P ⁡ ( score > τ ∣ label = 1 ) \mathrm{TPR}(\tau)=P(\text{score}>\tau\mid\text{label}=1) against the false-positive rate FPR ⁡ ( τ ) = P ⁡ ( score > τ ∣ label = 0 ) \mathrm{FPR}(\tau)=P(\text{score}>\tau\mid\text{label}=0) . The area under this curve (AUROC) summarizes separation across all thresholds at once: it is 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} when scores are independent of the label and 1 1 when every positive outscores every negative. A standard identity turns the area into a pairwise statistic rather than an integral, namely the probability that a uniformly drawn positive outranks a uniformly drawn negative ( Hanley and McNeil, 1982 ) , which for a finite set of positive scores A A and negative scores B B , with ties split evenly, is 
 AUC ⁡ ( A , B ) = 1 | A | ​ | B | ​ ∑ a ∈ A ∑ b ∈ B h ⁡ ( a , b ) , \mathrm{AUC}(A,B)=\frac{1}{|A|\,|B|}\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}h(a,b), 
 (6) 
 where the pairwise kernel h ( a , b ) = 𝟏 [ a > b ] + 1 2 𝟏 [ a = b ] h(a,b)=\mathbf{1}[a>b]+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}[a=b] scores a correctly ranked pair 1 1 , a tie 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} , and a misranked pair 0 0 . Each estimand below instantiates ( 6 ) with a particular score for every step and a particular partition into A A and B B . 
 Pooled AUROC. 
 Score each step by its raw confidence and compare every success-step against every failure-step in the window, holding none of the confounds fixed: 
 AUC pool ​ ( W ) = AUC ⁡ ( c ⁡ ( 𝒫 W ) , c ⁡ ( 𝒩 W ) ) . \mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{pool}}(W)=\mathrm{AUC}\big(c(\mathcal{P}_{W}),\,c(\mathcal{N}_{W})\big). 
 (7) 
 This conflates task difficulty (i), attempt quality (ii), and depth (iii): a success-step may outrank a failure-step merely because its task is easy or its episode is nearly over. 
 Between-task AUROC. 
 Collapse each task to a single number by replacing every step’s confidence with the mean confidence of its task over the window. Writing 𝒮 s , W = { ( i , t ) : ( s , i , t ) ∈ 𝒮 W } \mathcal{S}_{s,W}=\{(i,t):(s,i,t)\in\mathcal{S}_{W}\} for the steps task  s s emits in the window, 
 c ¯ s ​ ( W ) = 1 | 𝒮 s , W | ​ ∑ ( i , t ) ∈ 𝒮 s , W c s , i , t , \bar{c}_{s}(W)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{s,W}|}\sum_{(i,t)\in\mathcal{S}_{s,W}}c_{s,i,t}, 
 (8) 
 and then apply ( 7 ) with c s , i , t c_{s,i,t} replaced by c ¯ s ​ ( W ) \bar{c}_{s}(W) . Because every step of a task now shares one score, all within-task comparisons tie and contribute exactly 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} ; only cross-task comparisons carry signal, so the statistic measures whether easier (higher-mean-confidence) tasks succeed more often, i.e. difficulty alone. 
 Within-task AUROC. 
 Fix the task and compare its succeeding attempts against its failing attempts, which cancels difficulty because both sides share the task. For a task s s with both outcomes present in the window (a mixed-outcome task, written s ∈ ℳ W s\in\mathcal{M}_{W} ), define the per-task score 
 a s ​ ( W ) = AUC ⁡ ( c ⁡ ( 𝒫 W s ) , c ⁡ ( 𝒩 W s ) ) , a_{s}(W)=\mathrm{AUC}\big(c(\mathcal{P}_{W}^{s}),\,c(\mathcal{N}_{W}^{s})\big), 
 (9) 
 and average over the mixed-outcome tasks: 
 AUC within ​ ( W ) = 1 | ℳ W | ​ ∑ s ∈ ℳ W a s ​ ( W ) . \mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{within}}(W)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{W}|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{M}_{W}}a_{s}(W). 
 (10) 
 Only tasks that both succeed and fail in the window are estimable, so | ℳ W | |\mathcal{M}_{W}| (reported as the mixed count in Table  6 ) sets how many clusters the estimate rests on; it shrinks as the window narrows, which is why the step-0 and early-window within-task intervals are wide. The statistic still mixes attempt quality (ii) with depth (iii), since a succeeding attempt’s step may outrank a failing one simply by sitting later in the episode. 
 Depth-matched within-task AUROC. 
 Additionally fix the step index, so only confidences emitted at the same depth of the same task are ever compared. For each task s s and index t t with both outcomes present, let 
 a s , t = AUC ⁡ ( c ⁡ ( 𝒫 W s , t ) , c ⁡ ( 𝒩 W s , t ) ) , a_{s,t}=\mathrm{AUC}\big(c(\mathcal{P}_{W}^{s,t}),\,c(\mathcal{N}_{W}^{s,t})\big), 
 (11) 
 and, writing 𝒞 W = { ( s , t ) : t ∈ W , ∃ i , i ′ Y s , i = 1 , Y s , i ′ = 0 } \mathcal{C}_{W}=\{(s,t):t\in W,\ \exists\,i,i^{\prime}\ Y_{s,i}=1,\,Y_{s,i^{\prime}}=0\} for the qualifying (mixed-outcome, same-step) cells, average over them: 
 AUC dm ​ ( W ) = 1 | 𝒞 W | ​ ∑ ( s , t ) ∈ 𝒞 W a s , t . \mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{dm}}(W)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{W}|}\sum_{(s,t)\in\mathcal{C}_{W}}a_{s,t}. 
 (12) 
 Neither difficulty nor depth can contribute, so what survives is attempt-level foresight alone: among attempts of one task that have reached the identical step, does the one that will ultimately succeed state higher confidence. 
 Depth-only baseline. 
 To quantify how much of any estimand a trivial position signal could explain, we recompute it with each confidence replaced by the negated step index, 
 c ~ s , i , t = − t , \tilde{c}_{s,i,t}=-\,t, 
 (13) 
 feeding c ~ \tilde{c} in place of c c through the same estimand (( 7 ), ( 8 ), ( 10 ), or ( 12 )). This baseline ranks shallow steps above deep ones and knows nothing of the agent. Over all steps it can match or exceed a real signal, because succeeding and failing attempts differ systematically in length. In the step-0 window every c ~ = 0 \tilde{c}=0 , so by ( 6 ) every comparison ties and the baseline is exactly 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} ; in the early window the three admissible depths leave it near chance. A trained signal that beats the depth-only baseline in the early window or at step 0 therefore exhibits foresight that position cannot explain.

 The early-window improvement might instead reflect de-censoring, where training raises the fraction of early steps that emit a confidence tag, rather than the calibration term in isolation. We probe this with a format-only control but cannot cleanly separate the two. Pooled over all emitting steps the three-way comparison is null (base 0.723, full 0.702; flip full − - base − 0.021 -0.021 [-.052,.011] ), so the pooled signal is not a censoring artifact. For the early-window gain we evaluate the control at an emission-matched checkpoint (tag rate 0.967, within 0.012 of both the base and full arms): neither the de-censoring component (format − - base, + 0.006 +0.006 [-.020,.034] ) nor the calibration component (full − - format, − 0.027 -0.027 [-.055,.000] ) is individually resolvable. By the trained endpoint the control’s own format correctness rate falls to 0.744, which confounds a like-for-like comparison. We therefore report the early-window transfer gain (within-task AUROC + 0.265 +0.265 [.107,.428] at step 0 and + 0.070 +0.070 [.010,.130] over steps 0–2) without attributing it to de-censoring or calibration in isolation. 
 WebShop. 
 On a live, on-policy WebShop run with the binary won reward, held-out episode success stays near the floor for both arms (0.9% untrained, 1.6% Brier+ φ \varphi ), leaving too little success variation to estimate a within-task early-window gain: only two to three held-out tasks contain both a successful and a failed seed, so the within-task AUROC is not meaningfully identified. Step-level pooled discrimination is 0.825 [.785,.889] untrained and 0.877 [.784,.951] trained, with overlapping intervals due to insufficient held-out samples. 
 Appendix E Across Model Size 
 We repeat the online ALFWorld comparison on Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, evaluating the untrained agent and a Brier+ φ \varphi checkpoint under the same held-out protocol as the 7B experiments. Episode success is 36.9% untrained and 38.1% trained, with format parsing rate of 1.000 in both experiments. The pooled matched-emission AUROC is 0.697 [.615,.781] untrained and 0.729 [.635,.818] trained; the paired (trained-untrained) Δ \Delta over the shared held-out tasks is + 0.032 +0.032 [-.016,.073] , directionally consistent with the 7B pooled gain. The within-task early-window estimand is not identifiable here, as only six untrained and seven trained held-out tasks contain both a successful and a failed seed, too few for a reliable paired within-task delta. At 14B the trained agent still beats its untrained counterpart on both episode success and pooled AUROC, in the same direction as the 7B gain, so the pooled effect does not vanish with scale. Resolving the within-task estimand at this scale would require more held-out tasks across additional sizes and model families.

 Appendix I Hyperparameters, Prompt & Rewards 
 Prompt (used in both training & evaluation). 
 You are an agent solving a household task in a text world. Each turn you are given the task, a log of what you have already done this episode, what you currently observe, and the list of admissible actions. Choose exactly ONE admissible action that best progresses toward the task. Do not repeat actions that did not help. First output the chosen action text on its own line, copied verbatim from the admissible list, with no explanation. Then on the NEXT line output your confidence that THIS EPISODE will ultimately succeed, as an integer 0-100 wrapped exactly as <conf>NN</conf>. Output nothing else.

 Appendix J Computational Budget and Reproducibility 
 Models and parameters. 
 All training uses Qwen2.5-Instruct backbones at 7B and 14B parameters ( Yang et al., 2024 ) , adapted with LoRA ( Hu et al., 2022 ) at rank 32 and α = 64 \alpha=64 (Appendix  I ); only the adapter parameters are updated. 
 Hardware and compute budget. 
 All models were trained on a single node with 8 × \times NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Each run is a single-GPU LoRA job (adapters only), with experiments scheduled concurrently across the eight GPUs. Representative single-GPU wall-clock is about 8 h for a 7B online ALFWorld experiment, 12 h for the 14B experiment, 5 h for a TextWorld experiment, 2 h for WebShop, and roughly 10 min for an offline GRPO reward-form computation. By category: the offline GRPO reward-form and estimator sweeps total ≈ \approx 2 GPU-hours; the online 7B ALFWorld arms (six reward forms plus four additional-seed runs) ≈ \approx 79 GPU-hours; the TextWorld and WebShop online runs ≈ \approx 18 GPU-hours; and the cross-size experiments ≈ \approx 49 GPU-hours. Including preliminary and failed runs, total training compute is approximately 200 GPU-hours. 
 Software. 
 PyTorch 2.13.0 (CUDA 13.0), Transformers 5.17.0, TRL 1.14.0, PEFT 0.21.0, Accelerate 1.15.0, Datasets 5.0.1, and NumPy 2.5.3. Offline training uses TRL GRPO; the online route uses the in-house on-policy learner (Appendix  I ). 
 Runs and statistical reporting. 
 Each online training experiment uses a single training seed (Section  5 ); the offline reward-form and estimator comparisons use the seed counts stated in Appendix  I and Table  13 . All reported intervals are 95% task-level cluster bootstraps over held-out tasks. Training, validation, and test seeds are disjoint (Appendix  I ); held-out evaluation seeds never appear in training. 
 Reproducibility. 
 Every ingredient needed to reproduce our results is specified in the paper: the on-policy learner configuration and full hyperparameters, the agent prompt template with the inline <conf>NN</conf> format, and the exact per-arm reward definitions (Appendix  I ); the pre-registered estimand (within-task AUROC at k ≤ 2 k\leq 2 ), the checkpoint-selection rule, and the disjoint train/validation/test seed ranges (Section  5 ); and the 95% task-level cluster-bootstrap procedure used for all reported intervals. We provide anonymized training and evaluation code, configurations, and prompts in the supplementary material to reproduce every experiment.
