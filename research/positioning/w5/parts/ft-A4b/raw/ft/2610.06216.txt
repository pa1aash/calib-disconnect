 Abstract 
 Pre-generation success probes estimate response correctness from a language model’s hidden activations before decoding, enabling cost-aware routing. While prior work has demonstrated their utility primarily on English inputs, we study their reliability across languages along three dimensions: (1) whether they preserve the ranking of likely successes and failures ( discrimination ); (2) whether they retain probabilities that match observed success frequencies ( calibration ); and (3) whether they produce scores comparable enough across candidate models for cost-aware multilingual routing ( utility ). Using 3,000 MATH problems in 10 languages and 8 open-weight model configurations, we compare cross-lingual transfer from English-trained probes and equal-budget pooled multilingual probes. English-trained probes retain useful cross-lingual discrimination but become less well calibrated after transfer. Pooled multilingual supervision improves both properties and yields more reliable estimates of success. In routing experiments, the pooled router achieves a 0.7% higher test success rate while reducing modeled cost by 13.0% relative to always selecting the model with the highest average success. These results show that multilingual routing requires success estimates that remain well calibrated and comparable across languages and models. 
 1 Introduction 
 Figure 1:

Curves compare English-trained, recalibrated English-trained, and equal-budget pooled multilingual probes used for routing.
 Large language models (LLMs) do not perform consistently across languages. A model may solve a mathematical problem in English and fail on a semantically equivalent formulation in another language ( Shi et al., 2022 ; Ahuja et al., 2023 ; Ko et al., 2025 ; Son et al., 2025 ) . At the same time, always using the strongest available model is costly and often unnecessary, since a cheaper model may be equally capable of solving the same problem ( Chen et al., 2023 ; Aggarwal et al., 2024 ; Chen et al., 2024 ; Ong et al., 2024 ) . Cost-aware model routing aims to address this trade-off by selecting, for each input, the least expensive model likely to produce a correct response. Such a decision requires estimating, before decoding, how likely each candidate model is to answer correctly. 
 Recent work has shown that a model’s hidden activations contain information about the correctness of its future response ( Azaria and Mitchell, 2023 ; Lugoloobi et al., 2026 ) . Linear probes can extract this information after the prompt has been processed but before the first output token is generated. These pre-generation success probes can therefore support routing without first generating and evaluating a candidate response ( Di Cicco, 2026 ; Lugoloobi et al., 2026 ; Varshney et al., 2026 ) . However, existing evidence is largely limited to English inputs, leaving unclear whether these probes remain reliable across languages and whether their estimates are sufficiently comparable for multilingual routing. We therefore ask: (RQ1) How reliably do pre-generation success probes transfer from English to non-English target languages? (RQ2) How effectively do the resulting success estimates support cost-aware routing? 
 We investigate these questions using 3,000 problems from the MATH dataset ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ) , translated from English into 9 additional languages, and 8 open-weight model configurations. For each problem, language, and model configuration, we collect n = 5 n=5 sampled generations to estimate empirical success and extract residual-stream activations at every model depth before decoding. We train layer-wise ridge-regression probes under monolingual training and equal-budget pooled multilingual training. We then compare English-to-target cross-lingual transfer with pooled multilingual supervision, using a probe trained directly on labelled data in the target language as an oracle reference.
We assess probes’ reliability through three increasingly demanding requirements: (1) probes should preserve the ordering of likely successes and failures ( discrimination ); (2) their predicted probabilities should remain aligned with observed success frequencies ( calibration ); and (3) their scores should be sufficiently comparable across candidate models to support effective cost-aware choices ( utility ). 
 Our results show that English-trained probes retain substantial cross-lingual discrimination, particularly at intermediate layers ( Civelli et al., 2026 ) . Their scores, however, do not retain a consistent probabilistic meaning across languages. Pooled multilingual supervision reduces expected calibration error by 38–69% across models and produces a stronger success–cost frontier in routing (see Figure 1 ). Overall, our findings show that preserving cross-lingual rankings is only one requirement for reliable multilingual routing. Effective routing also requires success probabilities whose scale remains meaningful across languages and sufficiently comparable across candidate models. 
 2 Related Work 
 Pre-generation prediction from activations. 
 Prior work has investigated whether a model’s internal state contains information about properties of its eventual response before generation begins. Early studies identify activation patterns associated with truthfulness and correctness ( Azaria and Mitchell, 2023 ) . Related work shows that human-annotated problem difficulty is linearly decodable from pre-generation representations ( Lugoloobi and Russell, 2025 ; Civelli et al., 2026 ) . Moving from these general response and problem-level properties to model-specific success, Cencerrado et al. (2025) estimate forthcoming answer correctness from question-only activations by constructing correctness directions from the difference between the mean activations of successful and unsuccessful examples. These directions transfer well across factual knowledge tasks, but their predictive performance is weaker on mathematical reasoning. Lugoloobi et al. (2026) extend this line of work by training supervised linear probes directly on labelled successes and failures. They show that these probes can predict model success on mathematical and coding tasks and, crucially, use the resulting estimates for cost-aware model routing. Subsequent approaches similarly exploit prefilling-time signals for self-evaluation ( Kasnavieh et al., 2026 ) or use an open-weight model’s activations to predict the correctness of separate target models for routing ( Varshney et al., 2026 ) . Together, these studies establish that pre-generation representations encode signals correlated with correctness and that these signals can support routing decisions. 
 Cross-lingual transfer of internal signals. 
 Multilingual models have been shown to map semantically equivalent inputs in different languages to partially aligned representation spaces Bernardelle et al. (2026) ; Kim and Lee (2025) ; Li et al. (2025) ; Schut et al. (2025) ; Wendler et al. (2024) , enabling zero-shot cross-lingual transfer ( Pires et al., 2019 ; Conneau et al., 2020 ; Libovický et al., 2020 ) . Nevertheless, multilingual representation differences in tokenisation, pretraining data, and language-specific model competence can produce substantial variation across languages ( Rust et al., 2021 ) . Moreover, representational alignment alone does not guarantee that task-relevant information will transfer. In a controlled multilingual setting, Hua et al. (2024) show that strongly aligned representations can coexist with poor cross-lingual transfer, demonstrating that shared geometry is not sufficient for transferring a learned predictor. This distinction is particularly important for meta-cognitive signals. Concurrent work finds that problem difficulty has a partly shared cross-lingual geometry, especially in intermediate model layers ( Civelli et al., 2026 ) . Similarly, Datta et al. (2026) report that activation-based hallucination detectors trained in English deteriorate under zero-shot cross-lingual transfer and benefit from multilingual supervision. 
 Calibration under distributional shifts. 
 Discrimination and calibration describe complementary properties of a predictor. A predictor may rank successful examples above unsuccessful ones while assigning scores that do not correspond to empirical outcome frequencies ( Guo et al., 2017 ; Zadrozny and Elkan, 2002 ; Naeini et al., 2015 ; Platt, 1999 ) .
Prior work has shown that cross-lingual transfer can substantially degrade the calibration of output-level predictions ( Jiang et al., 2022 ) . More recently, Zhou et al. (2025) study multilingual confidence signals in internal representations and find that intermediate layers can provide better-calibrated confidence estimates than final-layer representations, particularly outside English. This result connects multilingual calibration to representational depth, but does not establish whether supervised pre-generation success probes preserve calibration when transferred between languages or whether multilingual supervision improves their downstream routing utility. 
 Cost-aware LLM routing. 
 LLM routing and cascading seek to reduce inference cost by assigning each query to the least expensive model expected to answer it correctly ( Chen et al., 2023 ; Ong et al., 2024 ; Ding et al., 2024 ; Dekoninck et al., 2025 ; Jitkrittum et al., 2025 ) . Most routing methods estimate model suitability from external signals such as query embeddings, preference data, previously observed outputs, or uncertainty measures. Activation-based routers instead use internal representations computed during prompt prefilling to estimate model-specific success before generation begins ( Lugoloobi et al., 2026 ; Varshney et al., 2026 ) . For routing, however, predictive ranking alone may be insufficient. Model scores must be comparable across candidate models and operating conditions if they are used to set thresholds or optimise expected success under a cost constraint. 
 Existing activation-based routing work has not examined whether success estimates or routing thresholds remain valid under language shift.

 3.2 Data 
 We sample 3,000 English-language problems from the MATH dataset ( Hendrycks et al., 2021 ) and translate each into Arabic, Chinese, French, Italian, Russian, Swahili, Telugu, Thai, and Turkish using DeepL ( DeepL SE, 2026 ) .
To preserve the mathematical content and structure of the source problems, we protect mathematical notation and structured blocks during translation. We apply regular-expression checks to detect malformed notation, and use COMET-Kiwi ( Rei et al., 2022 ) as a reference-free estimate of translation adequacy. For further details on translation quality see Appendix  A . 
 We partition the 3,000 underlying MATH problems into training, validation, and test sets using a 70/15/15 split. We split by underlying problem rather than by translated prompt, so all ten language-specific versions of a problem remain in the same partition, with the resulting assignment shared across all models. The training split is used to fit probe parameters, the validation split is used for regularisation, reporting-layer, recalibration, and routing-policy selection, and the test split is reserved for final evaluation. 
 3.3 Models and Generation 
 Our experiments include 8 configurations of instruction-tuned Ouyang et al. (2022) , open-weight models: Qwen3 at 0.6B , 1.7B , 4B , and 8B parameters ( Yang et al., 2025 ) ; Qwen3.5 at 4B and 9B parameters ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) ; and gpt-oss-20b ( OpenAI, 2025 ) , evaluated under low- and high-reasoning settings, denoted gpt-oss-low and gpt-oss-high , respectively. 
 To simulate a realistic usage setting, each model receives only the mathematical problem, formatted using its native chat template, with no additional instructions. For each model–problem–language tuple, we sample n = 5 n=5 responses at a temperature of 0.7, extract the final answer, and aggregate the resulting binary correctness labels according to Equation  2 .

 3.6 Cross-lingual Probe Reliability 
 To answer RQ1 , we evaluate probe quality on the same held-out examples from each of the nine non-English target languages. Excluding English ensures that all reported comparisons measure performance under language transfer. 
 We compare three regimes: (a) English transfer ( Eng. ) , where a probe trained under monolingual supervision on English is applied to the target language; (b) pooled multilingual supervision ( Pool ) , where a probe trained under pooled multilingual supervision is evaluated separately on each target language; and (c) same-language reference ( Oracle ) , where a probe trained under monolingual supervision in the target language is evaluated on that language. The oracle therefore uses labelled target-language training data and serves as a reference for the other two conditions. 
 Discrimination. 
 We evaluate discrimination using rollout-weighted AUROC. A problem with k i k_{i} successful and n i − k i n_{i}-k_{i} failed generations contributes its score p ^ i \widehat{p}_{i} with weight k i k_{i} to the positive class and weight n i − k i n_{i}-k_{i} to the negative class. AUROC therefore measures the probability that a randomly selected successful rollout receives a higher score than a randomly selected failed rollout. 
 AUROC depends only on the ordering of predictions and is invariant to strictly monotonic transformations of their values. A probe can therefore achieve high AUROC while being systematically overconfident or underconfident. 
 Calibration. 
 Calibration measures whether predicted probabilities agree with observed success frequencies.
We diagnose this using sample-weighted Expected Calibration Error (ECE) with ten equal-width bins and reliability diagrams. ECE summarizes the average absolute difference between predicted and observed success rates across bins, while reliability diagrams show where these differences occur. 
 Calibration does not measure whether a probe meaningfully separates easy and difficult problems. For example, a probe that always predicts the overall success rate may be perfectly calibrated while having no discriminative ability. 
 Probabilistic quality. 
 We evaluate the overall quality of the predicted success probabilities using the rollout-level Brier score in Equation  3 and aggregated binomial negative log-likelihood (NLL). Both are strictly proper scoring rules: in expectation, they are minimized by reporting the true success probability. They jointly reward predictions that are correctly scaled and informative.
Brier score applies a quadratic penalty to probability errors and is bounded, whereas NLL applies a logarithmic penalty and therefore penalizes confidently incorrect predictions more strongly. 
 Neither metric isolates calibration from discrimination: an improvement may result from a better probability scale, a better ordering of examples, or both. We consequently use AUROC to measure discrimination, calibration diagnostics to evaluate probability scale, and Brier score and NLL to summarize their combined effect on probability quality (more details can be found in Appendix  C ).

For Qwen3-4B , the empirical success rate ranges from 0.145 in Swahili to 0.716 in English (refer to Table  1 ), showing that success depends jointly on the mathematical problem, the evaluated model, and the language in which the problem is presented.

 Table  2 shows that the English-trained Qwen3-4B probe retains substantial discrimination when transferred unchanged to the nine non-English languages. It achieves a macro-average rollout AUROC of 0.729, only 2.9 percentage points below the 0.758 obtained by probes trained directly in each evaluation language ( oracle ). Thus, English supervision retains enough success-relevant information to preserve much of the ordering between more and less likely successes after language shift. 
 Equal-budget pooled multilingual supervision further increases AUROC to 0.741, reducing the gap to the same-language reference from 2.9 to 1.7 percentage points. Because the English and pooled conditions both use 2,100 training prompts, this improvement results from the linguistic composition of the supervision rather than a larger annotation budget. The pattern is consistent across all 8 model configurations (refer to Table  10 ). Pooled supervision improves AUROC for every configuration and raises the cross-model mean from 0.697 to 0.724, thereby reducing the average gap to the same-language reference from 4.9 to 2.2 percentage points. Cross-lingual ranking information is therefore broadly transferable from English, although multilingual supervision recovers a substantial portion of the remaining discrimination loss. 
 4.2 Ranking Transfers More Reliably Than Probability Scales 
 As introduced in Section  3.6 , AUROC measures whether a probe orders examples correctly, but not whether its scores match the underlying success probabilities. Table  2 shows that English-trained probes retain useful discrimination after transfer, while their Brier scores and ECE deteriorate substantially relative to the labelled same-language oracles. Equal-budget pooled supervision recovers a considerable portion of this loss: it reduces mean Brier score from 0.171 to 0.154 and lowers ECE for all 8 models (refer to Table 10 ), bringing both metrics closer to the oracle reference. Because Brier score measures overall probabilistic quality, this recovery may reflect improvements in discrimination and calibration; ECE more directly identifies improved alignment between predicted and observed probabilities. These aggregate metrics do not show whether the calibration loss is uniform across languages or driven by particular language shifts. The per-language reliability diagrams in Figure  2 therefore examine how the relationship between predicted and observed success changes for each target language in the Qwen3-4B model. 
 The English-trained probe remains capable of ranking many target-language problems, but the mapping from its scores to observed success rates varies across languages. In particular, the same numerical prediction can correspond to different empirical success frequencies after language shift. Distributing the same supervision budget across languages produces scores that lie closer to the reliability diagonal and are more comparable across the covered language distribution. For reliability diagrams for the other models see Appendix  F . 
 4.3 ETD Success-Prediction Performance 
 Same-model probe performance is not sufficient to characterize routing because the ETD router Varshney et al. (2026) does not use each target model’s own representations.  5 Conclusion 
 This work investigated whether pre-generation success probes can support cost-aware model routing across languages. Using 3,000 mathematical problems in ten languages and 8 open-weight model configurations, we found that English-trained probes retain useful cross-lingual discrimination, meaning they can continue to rank likely successes above failures after language transfer. Their numerical predictions, however, do not preserve the same probabilistic meaning across languages. Equal-budget pooled multilingual supervision improves both discrimination and calibration, producing success estimates that are more reliable across languages and candidate models.
These improvements translate into stronger routing decisions. The pooled multilingual router reaches 86.6% test success while saving 13.0% relative to always using the highest-average-success model, and saves 22.9% at its validation-selected anchor-matching operating point. Monotonic recalibration substantially improves English-trained predictions but recovers only about one quarter of the routing-utility gap, indicating that the benefit of multilingual supervision cannot be explained by calibration alone. Overall, our results demonstrate that transferable rankings are necessary but insufficient for multilingual routing. 
 Limitations 
 Our study is limited to mathematical reasoning, these findings might not extend to other domains. Nine of our ten languages are machine-translated rather than natively authored, so results reflect transfer across translated prompts. We restrict our analysis to linear probes at a single post-prompt position and do not probe during generation, which may have an impact, especially under extended reasoning traces. We evaluate only open-weight models up to 20B parameters, and larger or closed-weight models may behave differently. Finally, our routing costs are estimated under a simplified model rather than measured from a live deployment.

 Appendix F Additional Results 
 F.1 Empirical Success Rates 
 Table  9 reports the empirical success rates of the
evaluated models across all ten languages. Each value is obtained by averaging
the empirical success scores over the test split. Since each problem-level score
is estimated from five sampled generations, these values estimate the
probability that a model solves a problem under the decoding procedure used in
this work, rather than the outcome of a single deterministic generation. 
 Success varies substantially across both models and languages. English has the
highest average success rate across models, followed by Chinese and French,
whereas Swahili and Telugu have the lowest averages. Increasing model capacity
or reasoning effort generally improves success: performance rises across the
 Qwen3 model sizes, from Qwen3.5-4B to
 Qwen3.5-9B , and from gpt-oss-low to
 gpt-oss-high . Nevertheless, the language gaps remain visible even for
the strongest configurations. For example, gpt-oss-high decreases
from 0.925 in English to 0.647 in Swahili. These results reinforce that success
is conditional not only on the underlying problem and target model, but also on
the language in which the problem is presented. 
 F.2 Same-model Probe Discrimination and Calibration 
 The complete same-model probe results (Table 10 ) show that the main findings are consistent
across model configurations. English-trained probes retain useful
discrimination after transfer to the nine non-English languages, obtaining a
mean rollout AUROC of 0.697. Thus, the representations learned
from English supervision preserve a substantial amount of information about
which target-language generations are likely to succeed. 
 Equal-budget pooled multilingual supervision improves mean AUROC to
0.724, bringing discrimination closer to the labelled
same-language reference. The improvement indicates that the pooled probe does
not merely adjust the numerical scale of the predictions: multilingual
supervision also improves the ordering of successful and unsuccessful
generations. 
 The difference between supervision regimes is clearer for probabilistic
quality. English transfer obtains a mean Brier score of
0.171, whereas pooled supervision reduces it to
0.154. Pooled supervision also lowers ECE for all 8
models, moving the predictions toward the same-language reference. English
supervision therefore transfers more reliably as a ranking function than as a
probability estimator. A score learned in English may still identify relatively
easy and difficult target-language problems, while no longer corresponding to
the same empirical success frequency after language shift. 
 The same-language probes generally remain the strongest reference because they
are trained with the full labelled training set from the evaluation language.
Importantly, however, pooled supervision uses the same total number of training
examples as the English-only condition. Its advantage therefore results from
the linguistic composition of the supervision rather than from a larger
annotation budget.

 The ETD results evaluate the predictions that are directly consumed by the
router. Unlike same-model probes, these heads predict the success of every target
model using a shared representation extracted from Qwen3-4B . This is a
more demanding setting because the encoder representation must contain
information not only about its own expected success, but also about the
behaviour of several distinct target models. 
 English-trained ETD heads retain some discriminative information, with mean
rollout AUROC 0.684. Pooled multilingual supervision improves
this value to 0.734 and reduces mean Brier score from
0.181 to 0.152. The improvement in AUROC shows
that the pooled routing advantage cannot be explained solely by better
calibration: pooled supervision also produces more informative target-model
rankings. 
 Monotonic recalibration reduces the English heads’ mean Brier score to
0.169. Because this transformation is strictly increasing
within each target-model head, it preserves the corresponding AUROC while
correcting systematic shifts in probability scale. Its improvement therefore
isolates the contribution of calibration. Nevertheless, the raw pooled heads
retain the lowest mean Brier score, and recalibration does not improve every
calibration diagnostic for every target model. Multilingual supervision
therefore contributes information that cannot be recovered by applying a
single post-hoc monotonic correction to English-trained predictions. 
 This distinction is important for routing. The policy compares predicted
success values across candidate models rather than ranking examples for one
model in isolation. Consequently, even an English-trained head with useful
within-model AUROC can produce poor decisions when its score scale is not
comparable with those of the other target-model heads. 
 F.4 Reliability Diagrams 
 The main text presents the reliability diagrams for Qwen3-4B . The
figures below report the corresponding per-language diagrams for the remaining
seven model configurations. Each pair compares the unchanged English-trained
probe with the equal-budget pooled multilingual probe at the model’s frozen
shared reporting layer. 
 Across models, the English-trained diagrams show that calibration errors are
language dependent: predicted values that are appropriate for one language can
correspond to different observed success frequencies in another. This explains
why English transfer can preserve useful AUROC while obtaining worse ECE and
Brier scores. Pooled supervision generally moves the curves closer to the
diagonal and reduces their variation across languages, although it does not
eliminate all model- and language-specific calibration error. 
 The diagrams also show why a single macro-averaged calibration statistic is
insufficient on its own. Similar aggregate ECE values can arise from different
patterns, including systematic overprediction, systematic underprediction, or
opposing errors across languages that partially cancel when averaged. We
therefore interpret these figures together with AUROC, Brier score, NLL, and the
calibration coefficients reported in the complete tables.

 Appendix G Monotonic Recalibration 
 To isolate the effect of probability scaling, we recalibrate the predictions
of each English-trained ETD target-model head using the pooled multilingual
validation set. We fit a positive-slope Platt-scaling transformation
 ( Platt, 1999 ) : 
 p ^ cal = σ ⁡ ( a m + softplus ⁡ ( b m ) ​ logit ⁡ ( p ^ raw ) ) , \widehat{p}^{\,\mathrm{cal}}=\sigma\left(a_{m}+\operatorname{softplus}(b_{m})\operatorname{logit}\left(\widehat{p}^{\,\mathrm{raw}}\right)\right), 
 where a m a_{m} and b m b_{m} are fitted separately for each target model m m .
Predictions are clipped to
 [ 10 − 7 , 1 − 10 − 7 ] [10^{-7},1-10^{-7}] before applying the logit. 
 The calibration parameters are estimated by minimizing binomial negative
log-likelihood on the nine non-English validation languages, using the observed
numbers of successful and unsuccessful rollouts. They are then frozen and
applied unchanged to the test predictions and routing simulation. 
 Because softplus ⁡ ( b m ) > 0 \operatorname{softplus}(b_{m})>0 , the transformation is strictly
increasing. It therefore preserves the ordering of predictions within each
target-model head and cannot change its AUROC, apart from numerical ties.
However, since a different transformation is fitted for each target model,
recalibration can change the relative probability scales across models and
therefore affect routing decisions.
