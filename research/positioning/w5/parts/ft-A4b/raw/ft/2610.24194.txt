 Abstract 
 Tool-using LLM agents repeatedly face decision points under uncertainty, choosing between autonomous action, additional information gathering, and human escalation. These decisions require balancing task success against interaction cost and the risk of irreversible errors. Learning to make these trade-offs is challenging because existing interaction trajectories record only the outcomes of actions taken, leaving the consequences of alternative actions unobserved. To address this gap, we construct a counterfactual dataset containing 3,115 action outcomes from 1,036 decision points in ToolSandbox. At each decision point, we execute each legal action and evaluate its downstream outcomes based on task completion, interaction cost, and irreversible errors. Using this dataset, we train SURE-Act , a controller that selects actions for a frozen backbone agent at inference time. Our experiments show that learning to rank alternative actions improves offline action-selection utility by 0.0309 over a regression-only baseline. We further show that adding SURE-Act enables end-to-end gains of up to 0.0575 in ToolSandbox’s Overall Score with the Qwen3-4B agent. Moreover, SURE-Act outperforms adapted decision rules from SAGE-Agent and Calibrate-Then-Act by 0.1506 and 0.1438, respectively. This work establishes the first counterfactual dataset for ToolSandbox and provides actionable insights into decision-making under uncertainty for tool-using agents.

 1 Introduction 
 Figure 1: Overview of SURE-Act. Counterfactual rollouts from an environment checkpoint evaluate

 Tool-using large language model (LLM) agents complete tasks through multi-turn interactions with users and external environments ( Yao et al., 2023 , Lu et al., 2025 ) . However, successful task completion alone does not establish that an agent made efficient decisions along the way. An agent may make unnecessary tool calls or ask the user for information already available in the environment. Conversely, acting before resolving uncertainty can lead to irreversible errors. At each turn, an agent must decide whether to act on the available information, gather more evidence, ask for clarification, or escalate to human assistance. Learning to make these decisions requires balancing task completion, interaction cost, and the risk of irreversible errors. 
 Recent work has studied the trade-off between the cost of seeking information and its value for decisions under uncertainty ( Suri et al., 2026 , Ding et al., 2026 , Guo et al., 2026 ) . These efforts motivate a broader question: how should an agent choose among the available actions when each may lead to different outcomes over subsequent turns? Estimating uncertainty in the current state is only part of this problem. The agent must also assess whether additional information would change its decision, what obtaining that information would cost, and what could happen if it acted immediately. This motivates comparing alternative actions by their downstream consequences at the same decision point. 
 Obtaining supervision for this comparison remains challenging. Existing datasets contain trajectories that show the actions taken and their outcomes, but do not directly reveal what would have happened under alternative choices. A successful trajectory therefore does not establish whether another action could have achieved the same result at lower cost. Likewise, a failed trajectory does not identify which alternative action could have prevented the failure. Learning from these trajectories alone leaves the relative value of unchosen actions unexplored. 
 To address this gap, we introduce a framework that uses counterfactual rollouts to supervise action selection. We obtain these labels directly from the environment. At each decision point, we clone the environment state, execute each legal meta-action for one turn, and continue under the same frozen policy (Figure 1 ). These counterfactual branches provide an executable label for every available action at that state. A separate controller then learns to choose among those actions while the underlying policy remains unchanged. For each candidate action, the controller predicts task success, interaction cost, and critical-error risk, combines these predictions using a predefined utility function, and selects the action with the highest predicted utility. However, accurate absolute outcome predictions may still fail to capture the small utility differences that determine action rankings. To address this limitation, we directly supervise pairwise comparisons between actions at the same decision point using a ranking loss. That loss is ordinary learning to rank applied to an unusual source. Although the loss follows standard learning-to-rank principles, its supervision comes from environment-generated counterfactual outcomes. We also establish a theoretical limitation: with only one stochastic rollout per action, observed utility differences may produce rankings that disagree with the expected utilities. We demonstrate this through a bounded counterexample (Appendix B ). 
 Adding the pairwise ranking loss improves action ranking over a matched regression-only objective across hundreds of family-held-out partitions. However, these partitions reuse the same family pool, and the improvement becomes less conclusive under family-level resampling. End-to-end evaluation further highlights the importance of intervention frequency, with performance closely associated with how often the controller overrides the original agent across five decision intervention-control rules. 
 Motivated by this observation, we introduce a simple stopping criterion: the controller remains active until it selects the same action three consecutive times, after which control returns to the original agent for the remainder of the episode. This criterion improves performance across both evaluation pools, with gains assessed against serving noise measured within each job. Among the five intervention-control rules evaluated, it is the only one that maintains a positive effect after reweighting by task family. Positive mean improvements are observed with Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-14B backbones (Table 3 ), and the system outperforms two adapted decision rules evaluated under the same experimental framework. 
 Our main contributions are the following. 
 • 
 Executable Counterfactual Supervision. A procedure that makes the environment the evaluator, branching a live checkpoint across every legal action and scoring each continuation under a frozen policy. On ToolSandbox it yields 1,036 checkpoints from 378 task IDs and 82 families, carrying 3,115 scored action outcomes (Table 2 ). 
 • 
 Theoretical analysis of action ranking. We establish a bound connecting within-decision-point ranking errors to decision regret and characterize how stochastic single-rollout supervision can misalign learned action rankings with expected utility. 
 • 
 SURE-Act with Intervention-Aware Control. We develop a controller that predicts action utility and introduce a preregistered stopping criterion that limits unnecessary interventions. The resulting approach yields positive mean improvements across the evaluated pools and three backbone sizes, and outperforms two adapted decision rules.

 2.1 Agent decision-making under uncertainty 
 AI agents often face uncertainty when interacting with users and invoking tools. Ambiguous user goals and missing task information can lead agents to select the wrong tools or provide incorrect arguments Kirchhof et al. (2025) , Suri et al. (2026) . Existing work uses uncertainty estimates to guide whether agents should invoke tool calls or ask for clarification. Building on broader interest in understanding model-internal representations ( Jin et al., 2025b , Han et al., 2026 , Xu et al., 2026 , Zhu et al., 2026 ) , MICE for CATs uses model-internal signals to estimate whether a tool call is correct and decide whether to execute or abstain ( Subramani et al., 2025 ) . Clarify When Necessary measures uncertainty across plausible user intents to determine when to ask for clarification ( Zhang & Choi, 2025 ) , while Ask-before-Plan combines user clarification with tool-based information gathering to support planning ( Zhang et al., 2024 ) . For question selection, Active Task Disambiguation chooses questions that maximize the expected reduction in task ambiguity ( Kobalczyk et al., 2025 ) . SAGE-Agent models structured uncertainty over tool parameters to select informative questions while penalizing redundancy ( Suri et al., 2026 ) . Existing benchmarks evaluate how agents select actions under uncertainty and use tools to complete user goals. When2Call tests whether agents should call a tool, ask for clarification, answer directly, or acknowledge that they cannot answer ( Ross et al., 2025 ) . FAIL-TALMS evaluates how agents handle ambiguous user requests and unavailable tools ( Treviño et al., 2025 ) . τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench evaluates tasks that require agents and users to coordinate actions in a shared environment ( Barres et al., 2025 ) , while ToolSandbox evaluates multi-turn tool use with persistent environment state and dependencies between tool calls ( Lu et al., 2025 ) . 
 2.2 Learning for multi-turn interactions 
 Improving sequential LLM behavior involves structuring reasoning processes ( Jin et al., 2024 , Jin et al., 2025a ) and learning interaction strategies that improve task outcomes across successive turns. Prior work learns from preferences over complete conversations to optimize response policies ( Shani et al., 2024 ) . Other studies train specific interaction strategies using feedback from simulated conversations. PPDPP trains a dialogue planner using task-oriented rewards ( Deng et al., 2024 ) , while STaR-GATE learns clarification questions that improve subsequent answers ( Andukuri et al., 2024 ) . Rollout-based methods evaluate individual decisions through their downstream outcomes. CollabLLM simulates future conversations to reward responses that improve task outcomes and interaction quality ( Wu et al., 2025 ) . REFUEL samples alternative responses from the same conversation history and updates the policy by regressing on differences in their downstream rewards ( Gao et al., 2025 ) . Table 1 organizes these lines of work along four axes. Frozen indicates whether the underlying policy remains unchanged, Executed distinguishes supervision from actual rollouts from supervision based on model-generated predictions, Declines indicates whether a method can choose not to act, and Signal identifies the information available at each decision point. Our work uses counterfactual rollouts to supervise a separate action selector. We execute each legal meta-action from the same decision point under a frozen continuation policy. The resulting outcomes train the selector to compare task completion, interaction cost, and critical-error risk at inference time.

 3 Method 
 SURE-Act trains a controller to guide a frozen tool-using LLM agent (Figure 1 ). First, we construct counterfactual rollouts to evaluate alternative actions from the same environment state. We then use these outcomes to supervise the controller to predict action utility and learn action rankings based on the observed context. During inference, the controller selects actions until a stopping criterion returns control to the backbone agent. 
 3.1 Problem Formulation 
 At decision point t t , we define x t x_{t} as the saved environment state needed to resume execution and h t h_{t} as the textual interaction context observed by the controller. We define a meta-action as a high-level action category that specifies how the agent should proceed. The controller selects a meta-action from the legal set 𝒜 ⁡ ( x t ) \mathcal{A}(x_{t}) , and the frozen backbone policy π \pi generates the corresponding response or tool call. 
 We define four meta-actions. Ask requests clarification or missing information from the user. Search retrieves information without changing the environment state. Act-Reversible changes the environment state in a way that can be undone. Commit-Irreversible makes a change that cannot be undone. 
 We define Q π ​ ( x t , a ) Q^{\pi}(x_{t},a) as the expected utility of executing meta-action a a for one turn at decision point t t , then following the frozen backbone policy π \pi : 
 Q π ​ ( x t , a ) \displaystyle Q^{\pi}(x_{t},a) 
 = p success π ​ ( x t , a ) − λ ​ c π ​ ( x t , a ) \displaystyle=p^{\pi}_{\mathrm{success}}(x_{t},a)-\lambda c^{\pi}(x_{t},a) 
 − η ​ p critical π ​ ( x t , a ) . \displaystyle-\eta p^{\pi}_{\mathrm{critical}}(x_{t},a). 
 Here, p success π p^{\pi}_{\mathrm{success}} is the probability of task success according to the environment evaluator. c π c^{\pi} is the expected interaction cost, calculated using a fixed cost profile that assigns a cost to each type of interaction. The term p critical π p^{\pi}_{\mathrm{critical}} is the probability that the environment accepts an irreversible action without the required authorization. 
 The nonnegative weights λ \lambda and η \eta control the penalties for interaction cost and critical errors. Our objective is to select the legal meta-action with the highest expected utility. 
 3.2 Counterfactual Data Collection 
 We collect decision points from agent trajectories and save the environment state at each point. For every legal meta-action, we create a separate copy of this state and restrict the backbone’s next turn to that action category. For tool-based actions, we restrict the available tools to the selected action category and require a tool call. For Ask , the backbone generates a question to the user. 
 After this turn, we remove the restriction and let the backbone agent complete the episode. We record the resulting task success, interaction cost, and critical-error occurrence as 
 𝐳 t , a = ( s t , a , c t , a , r t , a ) , \mathbf{z}_{t,a}=(s_{t,a},c_{t,a},r_{t,a}), 
 and compute the observed utility as 
 y t , a = s t , a − λ ​ c t , a − η ​ r t , a . y_{t,a}=s_{t,a}-\lambda c_{t,a}-\eta r_{t,a}. 
 Here, s t , a s_{t,a} denotes task success according to the environment evaluator, c t , a c_{t,a} is the interaction cost, and r t , a r_{t,a} indicates whether a critical error occurred. Each training record contains the interaction context, candidate action, outcome vector, and observed utility. 
 The final dataset contains 1,036 decision points across 378 task IDs and 82 task families, with 3,115 action outcomes (Table 2 ). We combine records generated using Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B and record which backbone model generated each example. Decision points collected with different backbone models remain separate, and all action branches at a given decision point use the same frozen backbone model. 
 We generate a rollout per action at temperature zero. Thus, each label represents a single observed outcome, rather than the exact expected utility.

 3.3 Utility Prediction and Ranking 
 The controller takes the environment identifier, legal-action set, last twelve interaction turns, and a marker identifying the candidate action as input. We use a Qwen3-1.7B encoder ( Qwen Team, 2025 ) with LoRA adapters ( Hu et al., 2022 ) , mean pooling, and a linear head to predict success, interaction cost, and critical-error scores: 
 f θ ​ ( h , a ) = ( s ^ , c ^ , r ^ ) , Q ^ θ ​ ( h , a ) = s ^ − λ ​ c ^ − η ​ r ^ . f_{\theta}(h,a)=(\hat{s},\hat{c},\hat{r}),\qquad\hat{Q}_{\theta}(h,a)=\hat{s}-\lambda\hat{c}-\eta\hat{r}. 
 Here, θ \theta denotes the trainable controller parameters, while the backbone model remains frozen. We use Q ^ θ \hat{Q}_{\theta} to rank actions without assuming that the success and critical-error predictions are calibrated probabilities. 
 Then, we train the controller to predict observed outcomes using masked squared error:

 where j j indexes the three outcome components and ℳ \mathcal{M} contains the context-action-component triples with evaluable targets. Missing outcomes do not contribute to the loss. 
 Accurate outcome predictions alone do not ensure the correct action ranking. Therefore, we add a pairwise ranking loss to directly supervise utility differences.  The combined objective is 
 ℒ = ℒ out + α ​ ℒ rank , \mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}}, 
 where α \alpha controls the contribution of ranking supervision. Setting α = 0 \alpha=0 gives the regression-only ablation, with all other training settings unchanged. We select the trained controller based on action-selection utility on validation task families. 
 3.4 Inference with Intervention Control 
 At each decision point, the controller proposes the legal action with the highest predicted utility: 
 a ^ t ∈ arg ⁡ max a ∈ 𝒜 ⁡ ( x t ) ​ Q ^ θ ​ ( h t , a ) . \hat{a}_{t}\in\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}(x_{t})}\hat{Q}_{\theta}(h_{t},a). 
 The backbone executes the proposal using the same one-turn constraints as in data collection. The controller is consulted again at the next decision point. 
 Training labels evaluate one intervention followed by the original policy. Repeated overrides change this continuation behavior, so their cumulative effect is not directly captured by the labels. We therefore use a stopping rule based on consecutive identical meta-action proposals. 
 The controller maintains the previous proposal p p and a repetition counter n n . A matching proposal increments n n . A different proposal resets it to one. 
 When n n reaches the preregistered threshold k = 3 k=3 , the controller stops before executing that proposal. The unmodified backbone then completes the episode. Returning control is a stopping decision, not a fifth meta-action. Continuous intervention without this rule is evaluated as an ablation. 
 Algorithm 1 summarizes the procedure. Clone copies a checkpoint, Force executes the selected meta-action for one turn, Roll continues under the unconstrained backbone, and Grade evaluates the resulting trajectory. 
 3.5 Theoretical Analysis 
 We distinguish guarantees about expected utilities from those about observed rollout labels. Fix a checkpoint and continuation policy, and write Q a = Q π ​ ( x , a ) Q_{a}=Q^{\pi}(x,a) . Let a ∗ a^{*} maximize Q a Q_{a} and a ^ \hat{a} maximize Q ^ a \hat{Q}_{a} . If 
 | ( Q ^ a ∗ − Q ^ a ) − ( Q a ∗ − Q a ) | ≤ ϵ for all legal  ​ a , \left|(\hat{Q}_{a^{*}}-\hat{Q}_{a})-(Q_{a^{*}}-Q_{a})\right|\leq\epsilon\quad\text{for all legal }a, 
 then Q a ∗ − Q a ^ ≤ ϵ Q_{a^{*}}-Q_{\hat{a}}\leq\epsilon . This motivates learning utility differences, but the bound is conditional on an error assumption that we do not establish for the trained controller. 
 For observed labels, decision regret relative to the best realized action is bounded by the sum of pairwise hinge losses. This does not imply the same guarantee for expected utility. 
 For a bounded random utility difference D D , the population ranking loss can favor the sign that occurs more frequently, while 𝔼 ⁡ [ D ] \mathbb{E}[D] also depends on outcome magnitudes. The preferred ordering can therefore disagree with the expected-utility ordering. Appendix B provides the formal results, proofs, and repeated-rollout analysis. 
 4 Experiments 
 4.1 Experimental Setup 
 We evaluate SURE-Act on ToolSandbox using offline action selection and end-to-end task execution. End-to-end experiments evaluate Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-14B backbones, including a separate pool of 89 unseen task families. 
 For controller training, we split data by task family into 60/20/20 training, validation, and test sets. All decision points and task variants from a family remain in the same split. We select models using validation action-selection utility and use identical splits for paired comparisons. 
 Offline evaluation measures the observed utility of the selected actions. End-to-end evaluation uses ToolSandbox’s Overall Score. We compare SURE-Act with the unmodified backbone, controller ablations, and adapted decision rules from SAGE-Agent ( Suri et al., 2026 ) and Calibrate-Then-Act ( Ding et al., 2026 ) . The latter comparisons evaluate the adapted rules within our framework, rather than the complete published systems. 
 We report paired performance differences and distinguish split-level standard errors from family-clustered confidence intervals. Repeated splits reuse the same family pool, so their standard errors describe sensitivity to the split rather than uncertainty over new task families. The details can be found in Appendix A . 
 4.2 End-to-End Performance 
 We first evaluate SURE-Act’s performance on complete task execution. On test splits drawn from the 82-family development pool, SURE-Act improves ToolSandbox’s Overall Score by 0.0344 with the frozen Qwen3-8B backbone (SE 0.0082), with positive improvements on 20 of 22 evaluation partitions. A repeat of the same experiment yields a similar gain of 0.0386 (SE 0.0093). 
 Table 3 reports results on 89 unseen task families across three backbone sizes. SURE-Act achieves mean improvements of 0.0575, 0.0216, and 0.0252 with Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-14B, respectively. These results show positive average effects across the evaluated backbones. However, the family-clustered confidence intervals for the 4B and 8B comparisons include zero, so the gains remain uncertain across task families. These score improvements do not imply improved safety: critical-error occurrence increases in every repaired evaluation for which component-level statistics are reported. Additional statistics, including family-clustered intervals, critical-error outcomes, and no-stopping comparisons, appear in Appendix D.8 .

 4.3 Effectiveness of Controller Training 
 We evaluate the ranking objective through a matched offline ablation. Across 330 family-held-out partitions, adding the ranking loss improves action-selection utility by 0.0309 over the regression-only controller (SE 0.0028), with positive differences on 250 partitions. Both controllers use the same data splits and training settings apart from the ranking-loss weight. Predicting success, cost, and critical-error outcomes separately also improves utility by 0.0082 over predicting a single utility score (SE 0.0016). These comparisons support the two components of the training objective. Detailed ablations are in Appendix D.6 , Table 9 . 
 In a separate evaluation over 120 partitions, the controller exceeds the best fixed-action baseline by 0.0092 (SE 0.0016) and the strongest prompted baseline by 0.0107 (SE 0.0022). These standard errors describe sensitivity to partitions of the same family pool. The family-clustered intervals for the controller-versus-baseline comparisons include zero, and the controller does not establish an offline selection advantage on the 89 unseen families. The offline results therefore support improved selection within the evaluated development pool, rather than a general improvement on new task families. Appendix D.6 , Tables 7 and 10 , reports the detailed comparisons. 
 4.4 Interaction Cost 
 Improving the task score is only useful if the agent does not become more expensive to interact with. We therefore measure what each decision rule costs the agent it modifies, paired per scenario within one job. SURE-Act overrides at 0.192 of decision points, adds 0.133 turns, and reduces the discounted interaction cost by 0.068. Running the unmodified backbone twice moves the cost by less than 0.001, which bounds the serving variability of this measurement. SURE-Act is therefore the only rule in this comparison that reduces discounted interaction cost, despite slightly increasing the episode length by 0.133 turns on average, and it does so at roughly a fifth of the override rate of the adapted rules.  4.7 Effect of Intervention Control 
 We isolate the stopping rule by comparing SURE-Act with the same controller applied throughout the episode. Without the rule, the intervention rate reaches 0.9273 and Overall Score falls by 0.0707 relative to the unmodified backbone (SE 0.0154). With intervention control, the rate falls to 0.2256 and the score improves by 0.0344. Thus, better offline action selection alone does not ensure better end-to-end performance. 
 Figure 2 summarizes both aspects of this result. The upper panel compares the Overall Score of each arm against the unmodified agent, while the lower panel compares intervention frequency and task performance across nine decision rules. Their correlation is − 0.77 -0.77 : methods that intervene more frequently tend to perform worse in this evaluation. This association, together with the stopping-rule ablation, supports limiting repeated overrides. It does not establish intervention frequency as the sole explanation for performance.

 5 Conclusion 
 Interaction logs reveal only the action taken, so we generate counterfactual supervision by branching the environment, forcing each legal intervention, and scoring its continuation under a frozen policy. This supervision improves offline action selection over a matched regression objective within the evaluated family pool. End to end, however, continuous intervention lowers the benchmark score. A preregistered stopping rule returns control after three repeated choices, yielding positive partition-level mean gains across both evaluated pools. Under the same execution framework, SURE-Act also outperforms adapted implementations of the SAGE-Agent and Calibrate-Then-Act decision rules. These results suggest that, for capable tool-using agents, deciding when not to intervene matters as much as choosing which intervention to make. 
 Limitations 
 Our evidence comes from one benchmark, one pool of 82 task families and 89 families held out from it. The offline margins hold within that pool and we report them as such. The family-clustered intervals are reported in full in Appendix D , and the scope they imply is stated where each number appears. The end-to-end finding is about a decision rule and not about the learned value model, and the Results give the two controls that bound that claim. The labels inherit the branching procedure. An intervention lasts one turn, later behaviour can undo it, each label is a single temperature-zero rollout, and the best action changed on 345 of 725 shared checkpoints across three repeated labelling runs. Finally, the environments are simulated and the repaired controller trips more critical-error conditions than the agent it improves on, so this is a task-competence result and not a safety one.

 Appendix A Experimental Setup Details 
 A.1 Data and Evaluation Splits 
 The primary ranking-loss ablation uses the counterfactual dataset described in Table 2 . It contains 1,036 decision points from 378 task IDs and 82 task families, with 3,115 scored action outcomes. Records are generated using Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B. Decision points associated with different backbone models remain separate, while all action branches at a given decision point use the same frozen backbone. 
 We partition task families into 60% training, 20% validation, and 20% test sets. All task variants, decision points, and backbone-specific records belonging to a family remain in the same split. This prevents variants of the same family from appearing in both training and evaluation. Each split seed determines a partition by shuffling the sorted family list. Paired methods use identical seeds and therefore identical family splits. 
 We train a separate controller for each split and training configuration. Model selection uses validation families only. The primary ranking comparison uses 330 paired partitions of the 82-family pool. The separate offline controller-versus-baseline comparison uses 120 partitions of its development pool. These are distinct experiments, and their partition counts should not be interpreted as independent collections of task families. 
 End-to-end evaluation includes the development pool and a separate pool of 89 task families excluded from development. We evaluate the latter with Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-14B backbones. Scenario coverage and the number of completed partitions are reported for each experiment.

 A.3 Baselines and Ablations 
 End-to-end comparisons include the unmodified backbone, SURE-Act, and the controller without the stopping rule. The latter continues selecting meta-actions throughout the episode and isolates the effect of intervention control. 
 We also adapt the clarification decision rule from SAGE-Agent ( Suri et al., 2026 ) and the cost-sensitive decision rule from Calibrate-Then-Act ( Ding et al., 2026 ) . These rules operate within our execution framework and are evaluated on the same scenarios as SURE-Act. Each adaptation implements a selected decision rule rather than the complete system described in the original paper. The results therefore characterize these implementations under our evaluation conditions. 
 Offline comparisons include fixed-action rules, prompted selectors, uncertainty-based selectors, and learned controllers. The fixed-action reference is the best fixed-action baseline evaluated on each test split. Prompted selectors choose an action directly from the interaction context. Uncertainty-based selectors use signals such as verbalized confidence, token entropy, or self-consistency. 
 The principal training ablations remove the ranking loss or replace the three outcome predictions with a single utility prediction. Additional analyses vary the controller encoder, batch size, utility weights, and interaction-cost profile. Intervention-control analyses vary the stopping threshold and compare the learned controller with a fixed-action schedule. Each paired comparison uses the same evaluation cases and scoring convention. 
 A.4 Offline Action-Selection Evaluation 
 Offline evaluation measures whether the controller selects actions with higher observed utility at held-out decision points. For test set 𝒟 test \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}} , we compute 
 U = 1 | 𝒟 test | ​ ∑ i ∈ 𝒟 test y i , a ^ i , U=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}}y_{i,\hat{a}_{i}},

 We report gains over the best fixed-action baseline on the same test split. This reference is chosen in hindsight and is not a deployable selection strategy. However, it is shared across paired methods and cancels when computing their performance difference. 
 Offline evaluation uses stored counterfactual outcomes and does not require new environment execution. It measures action selection at fixed decision points, but does not capture changes to the subsequent trajectory caused by repeated controller interventions. 
 A.5 End-to-End Evaluation 
 End-to-end evaluation runs each method in ToolSandbox and measures its effect on complete task execution. The primary metric is ToolSandbox’s Overall Score, which multiplies the milestone score by an indicator that no critical-error condition was triggered. This differs from the additive utility used to train the controller. 
 We compare methods on matched scenarios and report score differences relative to the unmodified backbone. We also examine milestone scores, critical-error occurrence, interaction cost, episode length, and intervention rate. Intervention rate measures how often the controller overrides the backbone’s action selection. 
 SURE-Act uses the preregistered stopping threshold k = 3 k=3 . The controller returns control to the backbone when it proposes the same meta-action three consecutive times, before executing the third proposal. Control remains with the backbone for the rest of the episode. Threshold analyses separately evaluate k ∈ { 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 } k\in\{2,3,4,5\} . 
 Each end-to-end comparison includes repeated runs of the unmodified backbone within the same evaluation job. Their score difference provides a reference for serving variability. This reference helps interpret the magnitude of an observed effect, but does not replace confidence intervals or paired comparisons. 
 A.6 Statistical Reporting 
 For paired comparisons, we report the mean performance difference, split-level standard error, median difference, and number of partitions with a positive difference. Compared methods use the same family partitions or matched scenarios, as appropriate for the experiment. 
 Repeated partitions reuse a fixed pool of task families. Their standard errors therefore describe sensitivity to partition composition and should not be interpreted as uncertainty over independent samples of new families. We additionally report family-clustered estimates and 95% confidence intervals obtained by resampling task families. These analyses preserve dependence among observations from the same family. 
 Results from different evaluation jobs retain their own serving-variability references and scenario coverage. Evaluation criteria and thresholds were specified before the corresponding results were inspected. Full baseline results, sensitivity analyses, and additional controls are reported in Appendix D .

 Appendix C Supplementary evaluation on tau-squared-bench 
 This appendix reports a domain-specific evaluation we ran on a second environment and then found we could not interpret. We report it because the measurement is instructive, not because it bounds the method. 
 C.1 What was run, and what it returned 
 Separately trained controllers were fitted on each domain and evaluated against the per-split best fixed action. On retail, the evaluation used 736 checkpoints, 40 splits and 5,681 decisions, with a best-fixed headroom of +0.2096. On airline, it used 137 checkpoints, 40 splits and 1,090 decisions, with a headroom of +0.0967. In both domains, the controller chose ABSTAIN_HANDOFF at every evaluated decision, with zero deviations and +0.0000 gain on every split. Across the two domains, there were 6,771 decisions and no deviation from the fitted fixed action. 
 C.2 Why that result is about the grader, not the controller 
 A uniform choice of one action across 6,771 decisions is a strong pattern, and the obvious reading is that the controller learned nothing. That reading is wrong, and the reason is in the labelling path rather than in the model. 
 The counterfactual labels for this environment are produced by forking an episode, forcing one action, and continuing for a fixed number of turns. The environment’s own evaluator scores a simulation only when it terminates because the agent or the user stopped. Any other termination returns zero with the note that the simulation ended prematurely. A branch we truncate after a fixed number of turns never terminates naturally, so it is scored zero by construction. The single exception is the handoff action, which ends the episode by itself and therefore carries the agent-stop reason that the evaluator requires. 
 So in this label set, every action except handoff is assigned zero, and handoff is the only action that can score above it. A controller fitted on those labels will prefer handoff everywhere, which is exactly what we observe. The pattern is a property of how we truncated the branches, and it tells us nothing about whether the controller can select actions in this environment. 
 C.3 What we are not doing about it 
 We are not relabelling the branches as agent-stop to make the evaluator grade them. That would tell the grader the agent chose to stop when it did not, converting our own truncation into a claim about the agent’s behaviour. That is a fabricated label rather than a measurement. 
 Two honest repairs exist. Each branch can be run to natural termination, paying the cost that the truncation was introduced to avoid. Or truncated branches can be graded directly on the database and action checks, and reported as partial credit rather than as the environment’s reward. Neither is a small change and neither is implemented here. 
 C.4 What this appendix therefore supports 
 It supports a statement about our instrumentation on a second environment and nothing about the method. In particular, it is not evidence that the controller fails to transfer, and the main text does not use it as such. The transfer question on this environment remains open, and the measurement that would settle it is named above.

These are differences from the unmodified backbone, not differences from SURE-Act. They extend the stopping-rule ablation to the unseen-family evaluations. No corresponding no-stopping result is available here for 14B. 
 The medians and standard errors summarize variation across partitions, whereas the clustered intervals come from the task-family analysis. In particular, the development 8B family-weighted mean is 0.0296, with interval [ − 0.0189 , 0.0804 ] [-0.0189,0.0804] , compared with the partition-weighted mean of 0.0344. The clustered intervals should therefore not be interpreted as intervals computed from the partition-level standard errors. Backbone evaluations are separate jobs, and these results do not establish a monotonic relationship between backbone size and improvement. 
 D.9 Stopping-threshold comparisons 
 The stopping rule returns control permanently to the unmodified backbone after the controller proposes the same action three times consecutively. Table 6 varies this threshold from k = 2 k=2 to k = 5 k=5 in one matched job per pool. On both the development pool and the 89 unseen families, intervention rates increase and mean score gains decrease as k k grows. The default k = 3 k=3 was fixed before these comparisons. These jobs are separate from the primary end-to-end evaluation, so their gains and intervention rates should be read within each job. 
 Across the five intervention-control rules, the correlation between intervention rate and Overall Score is − 0.8693 -0.8693 . Across all nine decision rules, it is − 0.7749 -0.7749 . These descriptive associations do not isolate the causal contribution of intervention frequency. In the primary development evaluation, the unmodified backbone run twice differs by 0.0068 (SE 0.0037), providing a serving-variability reference for the SURE-Act gain of 0.0344. An independent repeat yields a gain of 0.0386 (SE 0.0093). 
 D.10 Fixed-action schedule and outcome trade-offs 
 A fixed-action schedule intervenes at a rate of 0.2308, close to the learned controller’s rate of 0.2256, but uses the split’s fitted fixed action. In the schedule-comparison job, the learned-controller rule improves Overall Score by 0.0279 (interval [ 0.0076 , 0.0481 ] [0.0076,0.0481] ), while the fixed-action schedule improves it by 0.0082 with an interval containing zero. A difference in whether two individual intervals include zero does not establish a difference between the methods. The direct paired comparisons below address that question. 
 The head’s increment over the bare schedule is not resolved. It reads +0.0197 ([-0.0043, +0.0437], 13 of 22) and, in a preregistered replication, +0.0136 ([-0.0143, +0.0415], 10 of 20). Both point positive and neither clears zero. The quantity is a difference between two variants that are identical except at roughly one decision point in five. Its standard error is about the size of the effect, so more partitions would not settle it. The Results therefore claim the conjunction and no share for either part. 
 The mechanism is not improved safety. Every repaired job trips more critical-error conditions than the agent it improves on (+0.0220 development, +0.0037 unseen, +0.0115 on 4B) while winning on milestone (+0.0564 and +0.0253), and every losing rule trips fewer. The rule preserves task competence rather than safety. 
 We ran the same four variants on 89 ToolSandbox task families none of them has seen, verified disjoint from the development families, all 222 scenarios per partition. 
 One registered prediction is refuted. The reading that the mechanism is about intervention rate rather than the policy fires on the 4B job, so the account survives the policy change. But the pre-registration predicted a smaller gain on a less capable policy and the gain is larger, which it states would put the stated mechanism in trouble. What survives is the rate account rather than the preserve-a-capable-agent story.

 D.13 What the family-clustered intervals imply 
 The family-clustered intervals for the offline controller-versus-baseline comparisons include zero, as do the end-to-end SURE-Act-versus-backbone intervals reported for the development 8B, unseen 8B, and unseen 4B evaluations. This qualification does not apply to every contrast: the matched comparisons against the adapted SAGE-Agent and Calibrate-Then-Act rules exclude zero. Repeated partitions reuse the same family pools, so partition-level standard errors describe split sensitivity and do not establish generalization to new families. The partition-level thresholds were fixed before the runs and are interpreted at that level. 
 Appendix E Reproducibility details 
 E.1 Evidence reported in the paper 
 The primary quantitative result is a paired comparison of ranking-loss-on and ranking-loss-off controllers across 330 family-held-out partitions. Both variants use the same partition seeds and the same scoring convention, so the shared best-fixed reference cancels in their paired difference. The 330 partitions reuse 82 task families, and the reported standard error measures sensitivity to partition composition, not uncertainty over new families. 
 An earlier controller-versus-baseline comparison was withdrawn. Its scorer used a 25% validation fraction while the controllers were trained with 20%, producing reporting-family sets that did not match. A preregistered split-aligned reanalysis replaced it. The corrected comparison (+0.0092 over the per-split best fixed action, 120 partitions, three guards checked before any value was read) is reported in the Results. 
 E.2 Primary ablation configuration 
 Table 8 gives the centered-target configuration for the separate 120-partition controller-versus-baseline evaluation. The primary 330-partition ranking-loss ablation uses the uncentered-target comparison described in Appendix A . Counterfactual labels combine Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B continuation policies. The utility weights are λ = 1 \lambda=1 and η = 1 \eta=1 , and task families are partitioned 60/20/20 into train, validation, and test sets. Each legal forced action has one continuation rollout at temperature zero. The merged pool contains 1,036 source-specific checkpoints from 378 task IDs and 82 families, and 138 task IDs occur under both label sources. The two ablation variants differ only in ranking-loss weight (1 versus 0) and both use uncentred targets. Figure 3 shows the within-run correlation between loss and decision utility over all 120 runs, median -0.082 and negative on 70, with 36 runs improving their decision while their loss rose. Selecting a checkpoint on loss would therefore pick the wrong one on a large minority of runs, which is why the trainer selects on validation decision utility. 
 The commands and thresholds for every comparison were fixed in a pre-registration before the results were read. 
 E.3 Reproduction scope 
 Offline scoring can be recomputed from the stored counterfactual outcomes without rerunning any environment branch. Closed-loop results use ToolSandbox’s native Overall Score and are a separate experiment, and reproducing them requires the environment and a served policy. 
 Policies are served with vLLM ( Kwon et al., 2023 ) , and the labels are produced by the same served policy that executes the episode.
