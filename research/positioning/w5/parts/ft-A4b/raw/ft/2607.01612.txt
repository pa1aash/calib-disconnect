 Abstract 
 Training large language models (LLMs) with reinforcement learning (RL) has significantly advanced their performance on reasoning and question-answering tasks. However, prevailing RL reward designs typically prioritize response correctness, neglecting to incentivize models to express their confidence accurately. This leads to a critical problem: performance gains are often accompanied by poor calibration between confidence and accuracy, misleading models to overconfidently hallucinate when uncertain.
To address this limitation, we propose C orrectness and C onfidence C alibration R einforcement L earning ( C3RL ), a novel RL algorithm integrating correctness, calibration and dataset-informed reference accuracy rewards together. Comprehensive evaluation across 8 text and multimodal datasets demonstrates that C3RL enhances calibration without sacrificing accuracy, outperforming the current state-of-the-art method in both performance and calibration metrics.
Utilizing the well-calibrated verbalized confidence from C3RL, we further introduce C onfidence-based A daptive Test Time S caling ( CAS ), an adjustable inference-time strategy that allocates computational resources based on response confidence. Experiments show that CAS surpasses majority voting on both in-domain and out-of-domain datasets while reducing the inference budget by up to 12.33 times. We believe the synergy of C3RL and CAS paves the way for deploying more reliable and resource-efficient LLMs.
The code, data and models will be released. 
 1 Introduction 
 Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable performance in reasoning, question answering, and code generation ( brown2020language ; achiam2023gpt ) .
However, their impressive capabilities are accompanied by a critical failure mode: when faced with uncertainty, LLMs frequently produce overconfident but incorrect outputs—a phenomenon widely referred to as hallucination ( ji2023survey ) . Such behavior is not merely an academic curiosity, but
can lead to serious consequences, including misinformation propagation and decision failures in safety-critical domains. 
 To mitigate these risks, it is desirable for LLMs not only to generate correct outputs but also to reliably express their epistemic uncertainty. This property, known as confidence calibration , refers to the ability of a model to produce probability estimates that faithfully reflect the likelihood of correctness ( guo2017calibration ) . Well-calibrated confidence serves as a foundation
to abstain from uncertain answering, defer to human oversight, or dynamically adjust computational budget when confidence is low. 
 Despite its importance, confidence calibration for LLMs remains underexplored and faces several limitations.
First, they typically reduce calibration to a binary classification task (certain vs. uncertain), which is too coarse to capture the nuanced uncertainty of LLM predictions. Second, they often improve calibration at the expense of accuracy, since models may learn to produce deliberately incorrect low-confidence answers to minimize calibration loss. Moreover, they rarely explore the potential of calibrated confidence for downstream decision-making, such as adaptive inference and resource allocation ( zhao2024fact ) . 
 Our work addresses these gaps by jointly optimizing correctness and calibration within a reinforcement learning framework and by exploiting calibrated confidence for adaptive test-time scaling. Specifically, we design an approach that explicitly teaches LLMs to signal low confidence when they are uncertain
. Moreover, we leverage the resulting calibrated confidence to guide early stopping in test time scaling, adjusting more computation to low-confidence samples. To the best of our knowledge, our method is the first to integrate confidence calibration and test-time scaling in a unified framework, thereby improving both the reliability and efficiency of inference. 
 Our contributions are summarized as follows: 
 1. 
 Correctness and Confidence Calibration Reinforcement Learning(C3RL): We propose C3RL, a novel RL algorithm that simultaneously improves correctness and calibration, preserving overall performance while reducing overconfidence. 
 2. 
 Confidence-based Adaptive Test Time Scaling(CAS): We introduce an early-stopping mechanism for test time scaling that leverages calibrated confidence to dynamically allocate inference budget. This approach dedicates more computation to low-confidence ones, striking an improved efficiency–accuracy balance. 
 3. 
 Unified Framework: Together, C3RL and CAS form a coherent pipeline that enables LLMs to express calibrated confidence and to adaptively control inference cost, paving the way for more trustworthy and efficient LLMs. 
 2 Related Work 
 Recent studies reveal that, LLMs often exhibit overconfidence  ( xiong2023can ; mei2025reasoning ) and hallucination  ( zhang2025siren ; liang2024learning ) , which undermines their reliability. Existing efforts to improve confidence estimation and calibration can be broadly grouped into two major lines: (i) prompt and sampling-based calibration and (ii) training and RL-based calibration. We further discuss related work on
adaptive test time scaling. 
 2.1 Prompt and Sampling based Calibration 
 Prompt-based approaches guide LLMs to explicitly verbalize their confidence or self-assess their reliability after producing an answer.
  ( tian2023just ; xiong2023can ; yang2024verbalized ) show that verbalized confidence is often poorly calibrated
though calibration improves with model scale and reasoning capability. More recent reasoning-oriented studies  ( mei2025reasoning ; yoon2025reasoning ) demonstrate that introspective or “slow thinking” chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting can yield better alignment between confidence and correctness. However, these prompting-based techniques
depend heavily on prompt wording, limiting robustness and scalability  zhao2021calibrate . 
 Another line of research estimates confidence implicitly from response consistency. These sampling-based approaches rely on response diversity or majority voting  ( kuhn2023semantic ; aichberger2025improving ; kang2025scalable ) , where agreement across multiple generations serves as uncertainty. While these methods correlate well with empirical accuracy, they are computationally expensive. 
 Figure 1: Pipeline for the framework of training set construction, Correctness and Confidence Calibration Reinforcement Learning(C3RL) and Confidence-based Adaptive Test Time Scaling(CAS). The base model first generates 10 responses for each sample and categorizes the samples into “all correct", “partially correct" and “all incorrect". In C3RL, R correctness R_{\mathrm{correctness}} encourages correct answers, R reference R_{\mathrm{reference}} rewards/penalizes correctness change based on the assigned categories, and R calibration R_{\mathrm{calibration}} incentivizes calibration of confidence. In CAS, the stopping probability (Eq. 1 ) is calculated from the most confident answer and the second most confident answer. If the most confident answer dominates existing generations ( c 1 c_{1} is much larger than c 2 c_{2} ), the inference will stop to save budget. 
 2.2 Training and RL based Calibration 
 Training-based methods aim to learn calibrated confidence distributions through supervised or reinforcement objectives. lin2022teaching introduces dataset-level supervision by correlating response types with empirical accuracy, while R-Tuning  ( zhang2023r ) augments supervised fine-tuning data with binary “I am sure/unsure” labels. Although effective for coarse uncertainty, such designs fail to capture fine-grained, context-dependent confidence. 
 More principled formulations employ reinforcement learning (RL) to jointly optimize correctness and calibration. SaySelf   ( xu2024sayself ) and stangel2025rewarding train LMs with proper scoring rules (e.g., Brier or log loss) as rewards, directly aligning verbalized confidence with output correctness. However, these objectives often prioritize calibration alone, potentially encouraging low-confidence incorrect answers to minimize loss. RLCR  ( damani2025RLCR ) incorporates calibrated reward functions
to balance accuracy and calibration, though applications remain limited to question-answering or Maths tasks. Instead, our model trained with C3RL on reasoning-intensive tasks maintains accuracy and reaches optimal improvement in calibration. 
 2.3 Adaptive Test Time Scaling 
 Adaptive sampling has emerged as a powerful approach to reduce the computational cost of test time scaling. Adaptive-Consistency  ( aggarwal2023AC ) introduces a model-agnostic technique that dynamically adjusts the number of samples per question using lightweight stopping criteria based on the frequency of answers. li2023ESC takes an alternative approach by dividing the sampling process into sequential windows and stopping when all samples within a window are the same. However, these
adaptive sampling methods rely on frequency or entropy to approximate confidence, failing to utilize the confidence signal itself directly.

 3.2 C3RL 
 Previous RL training methods for calibration simply incentivize calibration and pose potential risks that models learn to give incorrect answers deliberately with low confidence. To prevent such a “calibrated but wrong" shortcut, we design a novel Correctness and Confidence Calibration Reinforcement Learning ( C3RL ).
For each question q ∈ 𝒟 t ​ r ​ a ​ i ​ n q\in\mathcal{D}_{train} , we prompt the base model to generate an answer a q a_{q} with an associated verbalized confidence c a q ∈ { 1 , 2 , … , 10 } c_{a_{q}}\in\{1,2,...,10\} , where c a q = 1 c_{a_{q}}=1 means the model is totally uncertain and c a q = 10 c_{a_{q}}=10 means the model is totally certain.
We train the model (same as the base model) utilizing the following reward:

 The three parts in the reward are as follows: 
 Correctness Reward: The traditional RLVR  RLVR reward R correctness ​ ( a q ) R_{\mathrm{{correctness}}}(a_{q}) = 𝕀 ⁡ ( a q = a ∗ ) ∈ { 0 , 1 } \mathbb{I}(a_{q}=a^{*})\in\{0,1\} . This part ensures the model will update in the “correct” direction instead of “calibrated but wrong” way. 
 Calibration Reward: 
We set a confidence threshold t t =5 to classify the answer a q a_{q} into “certain” ( c a q > t c_{a_{q}}>t ) and “uncertain” ( c a q ≤ t c_{a_{q}}\leq t ) categories. We encourage “certain correct” and “uncertain incorrect” answers with R calibration = + β R_{\mathrm{calibration}}=+\beta while penalizing “certain incorrect” and “uncertain correct” responses with R calibration = − β R_{\mathrm{calibration}}=-\beta . The ablation process to determine β \beta is detailed in  A.5 . 
 Reference Accuracy Reward: To utilize the prior accuracy information in data construction process, we customize a reward for question q q based on reference accuracy tag T q T_{q} . When the model answers correctly from formerly “all incorrect" questions, we incentivize it with R reference = + α R_{\mathrm{reference}}=+\alpha . On the contrary, if the model hallucinates with incorrect answers from formerly “all correct" questions, we punish it with R reference = − α R_{\mathrm{reference}}=-\alpha .
This design forces the model to remain correct if it can originally give correct answers and further stops the model from falling into “calibrated but wrong" risks. The ablation process to select α \alpha is elaborated in  A.5 . 
 3.3 Confidence-based Adaptive Test Time Scaling (CAS) 
 Prior adaptive self-consistency methods typically rely on frequency or entropy to determine early-stopping criteria. However, such signals are often uncalibrated and fail to accurately reflect the model’s true confidence. To address this limitation, we propose a Confidence-based Adaptive test-time Scaling (CAS) strategy, which integrates calibrated verbalized confidence into the stopping criterion. After generating each batch of responses, CAS applies a well-calibrated stopping criterion based on the verbalized confidence of model outputs to decide whether to (1) continue sampling or; (2) halt and return the current highest confidence-weighted answer. 
 Inspired by the idea that a high-confidence answer which is dominant over all the other responses suggests a reliable stopping criteria, we design CAS as follows:

 4.1.3 Baselines 
 We compare the following methods: 
 Non-RL Methods (1) Base: We directly ask the untrained model to output both answer and associated confidence ; (2) Self Consistency(SC)  ( wang2023self-consistency ) : Each question is sampled 10 times and the final answer is obtained from majority voting; (3) Supervised Finetuning with reference accuracy(SFT+Ref)  sftref : The model is supervised fine-tuned to calibrate with reference accuracy calculated in 10-time sampling. 
 RL Methods (1) RLVR: The off-the-shelf method using the correctness reward ; (2) SaySelf  ( xu2024sayself ) :The reward simply contains calibration Brier score; (3) RLCR  ( damani2025RLCR ) : Based on SaySelf, RLCR adds the correctness score to ensure the performance, which is current state-of-the-art RL method to tradeoff between performance and calibration; (4) C3RL (Ours) and variants: C3RL integrates the correctness reward, calibration reward and reference accuracy reward together. We exclude the reference accuracy reward (C3RL w/o Ref) to conduct ablation study.

 Ablation Study 
When the reference accuracy reward is ablated (C3RL w/o Ref), the model remains competitive accuracy but suffers a consistent degradation in calibration. This clearly displays the specific contribution of the reference accuracy reward to enhancing calibration capability. 
 4.1.5 Analysis: C3RL Advantage 
 The advantage of C3RL over RLCR in balancing performance and calibration is due to the following two reasons:
(1) Reference accuracy information: The ablation study has demonstrated the effectiveness of reference accuracy reward in advancing calibration;
(2) Importance of learning confidence threshold: RLCR, driven by the Brier Score (see A.2.3 ), tends to train LLMs to indicate totally confident ( c c =10) or totally inconfident ( c c =1) responses, which degrades to a binary case. On the contrary, C3RL guides the model to learn the confidence threshold t t =5 and gives fine-grained confidence to distinguish between certainty and uncertainty. The difference in confidence distribution between C3RL and RLCR is demonstrated in  A.4 . 
 4.2 Can CAS Improve Test Time Scaling? 
 Given the impressive results of self-consistency in Table  1 , it is natural to combine verbalized confidence and self-consistency together.
Although there have been test time scaling methods leveraging confidence to enhance accuracy (e.g. confidence-weighted majority voting), they fail to apply calibrated confidence to saving inference budget. We conduct experiments to demonstrate the inference efficiency of Confidence-based Adaptive Test Time Scaling (CAS). 
 4.2.1 Experimental Setup for CAS 
 To evaluate the performance and inference efficiency of CAS, we employ the following settings: 
 Benchmarks 
We select 6 benchmarks of reasonable sizes (less than 2000 samples): (1) Text : MMLU validation set   mmlu , FOLIO  ( folio ) and GSM8K  ( gsm8k ) ; (2) Multimodal :
The same as CAS evaluation multimodal sets.
The details of the benchmarks are in  A.1.2 . 
 Baselines 
 (1)
 Majority Voting (MV)   ( wang2023self-consistency ) : Each question is generated N N times and the most frequent answer is voted.
(2) Adaptive-Consistency (AC)   ( aggarwal2023AC ) : A strategy dynamically adjusts the number of samples based on a stopping criteria to detect dominant answers. The final answer is voted from the truncated samples. 
 Settings 
To evaluate all the methods equally, we compare the inference budget needed to achieve the same accuracy as Majority Voting( N N =64). It’s guaranteed by controlling threhold P stop P_{\mathrm{stop}} for AC and CAS separately. We use the Qwen2.5VL-2.5-7B-Instruct model trained after C3RL. 
 4.2.2 Results 
 As shown in Table  3 , our proposed CAS method demonstrates remarkable efficiency gains while maintaining competitive accuracy across all evaluation settings. 
 In terms of sampling efficiency, CAS achieves outstanding cost reduction compared to baseline methods.
Notably, CAS consistently outperforms the strongest baseline Adaptive-Consistency. The performance gap is particularly striking on OOD (Text) tasks, where CAS uses less than half of the sampling budget of Adaptive-Consistency (5.19 vs. 12.87 samples).

This demonstrates that CAS can precisely identify high-quality reasoning paths early in the generation process, eliminating unnecessary computations without sacrificing final performance. 
 4.2.3 Analysis: Imbalanced Budget Allocation 
 CAS adjusts the inference budget from high-confidence sampling traces to low-confidence traces with early-stopping strategy. To verify the rationality of this imbalanced mechanism, we compare the benefits from test time scaling for high-confidence and low-confidence samples. 
 We use the C3RL model for generation and choose the three text benchmarks in Sec 4.2.1 for evaluation. We divide the samples into two sets: High-confidence Set and Low-confidence Set.
For each question, if the CAS sampling time n ≤ 4 n\leq 4 (which means CAS stops early because the final answer reaches high confidence quickly), we categorize this question to High-confidence Set. Otherwise, we assign the sample to Low-confidence Set. We study how these two sets benefit differently from majority voting. 
 As shown in Figure 2 , when test time N N increases to 64, the High-confidence Set remains unchanged in performance (<0.2%), while the Low-confidence Set jumps dramatically from 49.0% to 54.5%. The fact that the Low-confidence Set benefits much more from test time scaling than its counterpart rationalizes the CAS strategy of shifting limited computing resource from high-confidence samples to low-confidence samples. 
 4.3 Can C3RL contribute to CAS? 
 To explore the influence of calibrated confidence in test time scaling, we compare two models: The base model and the model trained with C3RL. The Confidence-weighted Majority Voting (CMV) method is also evaluated to display the contribution of calibrated confidence. 
 As shown in Figure  3 , C3RL+CAS outperforms all baseline methods when number of samples N N is sufficiently large ( ≥ \geq 8), demonstrating the scalable effectiveness of our approach. A key observation is the stratification of test-time scaling strategies in the C3RL-trained model. In comparison, the base model benefits little from CAS than AC. This stark contrast underscores that confidence calibration through C3RL is prerequisite for CAS to unlock its full potential. The performance leap from base+CAS to C3RL+CAS demonstrates their synergistic relationship: C3RL provides well-calibrated verbalized confidence, and CAS efficiently leverages it for test-time scaling. 
 5 Conclusion 
 This work presents Correctness and Confidence Calibration Reinforcement Learning (C3RL) to support downstream Confidence-based Adaptive test time Scaling (CAS). By integrating correctness, calibration and prior reference information together, C3RL achieves the best trade-off between performance and calibration, providing credible verbalized confidence. Based on well-calibrated confidence, CAS takes inference most efficiently among test time scaling methods by adjusting computing resources to low-confidence samples. Our results highlight a unified pipeline for reliable LLMs and efficient inference under limited resources. 
 Limitations 
 There are several limitations for our C3RL-CAS framework. First, the high-quality training data in science domain is not sufficient compared to Maths and logic reasoning domains, which may result in training bias. Second, although C3RL achieves best accuracy-calibration trade-off, its calibration ability is still inferior to SaySelf, which means if we want to enhance the model accuracy, we have to sacrifice calibration. Third, the advantage of CAS over Adaptive-Consistency in small generation cases ( N N <8) is not considerable, which requires more reasonable stopping criteria and better-calibrated confidence from C3RL.

 A.4 Confidence Distribution Comparison of C3RL and RLCR 
 As shown in Figure  5 , RLCR exhibits a highly polarized confidence distribution concentrated at the extremes ( c = 1 c=1 and c = 10 c=10 ), effectively degenerating confidence estimation into a binary indicator. Although Brier-score-based training can align models on the semantic meaning of extreme confidence values (e.g., “confidence=10” denotes total certainty and “confidence=1” denotes total uncertainty), it does not provide a clear confidence boundary separating certain and uncertain cases. As a result, intermediate confidence levels are underutilized. 
 In contrast, C3RL explicitly introduces a confidence threshold ( t = 5 t=5 ) through threshold-guided calibration reward, encouraging the model to learn a clearer distinction between certainty and uncertainty while still preserving fine-grained confidence expression. Consequently, C3RL produces a substantially smoother confidence distribution with richer intermediate confidence values. This comparison highlights the importance of learning an explicit confidence threshold instead of relying solely on binary confidence extremes.

 A.7 Advantage of Verbalized Confidence 
 We select verbalized confidence instead of internal logits or probablities to express uncertainty due to the following three reasons. First, verbalized confidence can express epistemic uncertainty of the responses, while the internal logits only represent uncertainty over tokens instead of the claim itself  lin2022teaching . Second, verbalized confidence itself is typically better-calibrated than conditional probabilities  tian2023just . Third, we want to explore a model-agnostic method, which should be applicable to all kinds of LLMs without relying on the internal state of LLMs  yang2024verbalized . 
 We test the efficiency of different uncertainty quantification methods in the task of mitigating CHOKE (Certain Hallucinations Overriding Known Evidence) examples   trustmeimwrong . As mentioned in  trustmeimwrong , a CHOKE example needs to meet three requirements: (1) the LLM can originally answer the question correctly; (2) after modifying with the natural prompt, the LLM hallucinates; and (3) the LLM assigns a high certainty to the wrong answer. We conduct an evaluation experiment on the "all correct" + "partially correct" samples on the in-domain test set (808 among 1,073 samples). We use the model trained with C3RL to generate new responses following the identical settings in  A.3.1 . The CHOKE examples are those with wrong answers after the prompt is modified (we follow the "prompt _ \_ v7" modification in  trustmeimwrong ) and assigned a high confidence. We use probability difference as a baseline. The detailed results are shown in Table  9 .

 A.8 Selective-Answering Evaluation 
 To demonstrate the effectiveness of confidence calibration in calibration-critical scenarios, we additionally evaluate our method on the selective-answering benchmark ALCUNA  alcuna . ALCUNA contains both answerable and unanswerable questions. Specifically, the benchmark includes 88 answerable questions, where sufficient information exists to infer the correct answer, and 158 unanswerable questions, where entities are fabricated and no relevant knowledge exists. 
 For answerable questions, we report the same metrics as in the main experiments, including Accuracy, AUROC, and ECE. For unanswerable questions, we focus on the Abstain Rate, defined as: 
 Abstain Rate = # ​ abstentions # ​ samples , \text{Abstain Rate}=\frac{\#\text{abstentions}}{\#\text{samples}}, 
 where responses with confidence ≤ 5 \leq 5 are regarded as abstentions. 
 Table  10 reports the evaluation results. As shown in the table, SaySelf achieves the highest abstention rate on unanswerable questions by aggressively suppressing confidence. However, this comes at the cost of severe degradation in both accuracy and calibration on answerable questions, indicating over-abstention even when answering is appropriate. 
 In contrast, C3RL achieves the best trade-off between accuracy and calibration on answerable samples while still maintaining a strong abstention capability on unanswerable samples. These results demonstrate that C3RL does not simply optimize abstention behavior, but instead learns a more reliable confidence boundary that distinguishes when to answer versus abstain.

 To evaluate the generalization capability of C3RL beyond reasoning-intensive tasks, we additionally conduct experiments on two canonical question-answering benchmarks: CommonSenseQA  commonsenseqa and StrategyQA  strategyqa . These datasets mainly focus on commonsense reasoning and implicit world knowledge, which are substantially different from our training corpus constructed from mathematics, science, and logical reasoning datasets. 
 As shown in Table  11 , C3RL consistently achieves the best accuracy-calibration trade-off across both benchmarks.  A.10 Ablation Experiment Evaluating RLCR + Reference Accuracy Reward 
 We additionally conduct an ablation experiment by incorporating the reference accuracy reward into RLCR, denoted as RLCR w.t. Ref . This experiment aims to verify whether the improvement of C3RL mainly comes from introducing additional reward terms or from the threshold-guided calibration formulation itself. 
 Table  12 reports the evaluation results on AGIEval and MMLU. Compared with RLCR, directly adding the reference accuracy reward to RLCR does not improve calibration performance and even slightly degrades AUROC and ECE on both benchmarks. In contrast, C3RL w/o Ref consistently outperforms RLCR and RLCR w.t. ref in calibration metrics, while the full C3RL achieves the best overall accuracy-calibration trade-off. 
 These results demonstrate that the improvement of C3RL does not simply arise from adding extra reward signals. Instead, the threshold-guided calibration objective plays the dominant role in preventing confidence collapse and enabling more fine-grained uncertainty estimation.
