 Abstract 
 Long-horizon agents often struggle to recover from failures whose causes lie several steps earlier, beyond what local reflection or backtracking can resolve.
We introduce BeliefWeaver , a framework that combines persistent evidence across attempts with prospective reasoning over repair directions.
It maintains a relational belief state over frontiers, attempts, observations, and checkpoints. Execution evidence updates related frontier priorities across attempts and rollback, while diagnostic evidence gates execution.
An implicit world model (IWM) evaluates repair hypotheses, predicts progress, and identifies relations among frontiers to guide exploration.
We further distill these reasoning components into a 9B student through supervised fine-tuning and on-policy distillation (OPD).
Across three terminal-based benchmarks, BeliefWeaver improves aggregate final score by 5.1 percentage points over native Terminus-2 continuation, with positive trends in exploration efficiency.
The distilled 9B student improves final score by 9.8 percentage points over the reported native baseline while reducing estimated teacher API cost by 54.8% relative to the matched teacher.
These results show that persistent evidence and prospective reasoning can improve recovery in long-horizon agent tasks while substantially reducing reliance on teacher-model inference.

 1 Introduction 
 Long-horizon agents tackle tasks such as autonomous research  Yamada et al. (2025) , computer use  Xie et al. (2024) , and sequential decision-making  Yao et al. (2022) . However, early mistakes can propagate through long trajectories, trapping agents in repetitive attempts or leading to premature commitments that are difficult to reverse  Wang et al. (2026) ; Liu et al. (2026b) ; Li et al. (2026) . The evidence needed to identify these mistakes often emerges only after many subsequent actions. Recovering from such failures requires agents to revisit earlier assumptions in light of new evidence and use what they learn to guide subsequent exploration. 
 Reflection allows agents to revise their behavior after failures or new evidence  Shinn et al. (2023) ; Madaan et al. (2023) . Recovery may involve backtracking and trying another frontier , a testable hypothesis for repairing the current failure. Existing approaches often organize such alternatives as a tree to support branching and backtracking  Zhu et al. (2026) ; Chen et al. (2026) ; Jin et al. (2026) .
Long-horizon failures, however, can arise from earlier decisions or assumptions shared across branches  Zhuang et al. (2026) ; Rafi et al. (2026) . Although rollback and persistent memory can preserve what happened, recovery still requires deciding how new verifier-grounded evidence should change the priorities of related repair hypotheses. When frontiers share prerequisites or interact across steps, evidence from one attempt can make another direction more or less promising  Zhang et al. (2026d) ; Liang et al. (2026) . This motivates a persistent exploration state that carries evidence across rollback and uses it to reprioritize future exploration. 
 We represent exploration as a persistent relational state over frontiers, attempts, observations, and checkpoints. Recorded outcomes and candidate relations determine which unary evidence factors update each frontier’s priority  Mishra et al. (2024) ; Cao and Chen (2026) . This lets evidence from one attempt re-rank related alternatives, while diagnostic evidence separately controls execution eligibility. The state is preserved across rollback.
Belief updates capture what has been learned, but recovery also requires deciding what to try next. Recent agentic world models  Zuo et al. (2026) use learned dynamics or outcome prediction to guide decisions  Zhou et al. (2024) ; Zhao et al. (2026) . We use an implicit world model (IWM) to evaluate repair hypotheses, estimate prospective progress, and infer frontier relations, guiding their generation, refinement, and reconsideration. These repeated assessments can then be distilled into a lightweight student to reduce teacher inference cost  Yang et al. (2026) . 
 In this paper, we introduce BeliefWeaver , a framework for long-horizon problem solving that combines a persistent relational exploration state with an implicit world model (IWM).
The exploration state tracks frontiers, attempts, observations, and checkpoints, preserving evidence across rollback and using new outcomes to revise related hypotheses.
The IWM evaluates repair hypotheses, estimates prospective progress, and infers relations among frontiers to guide what to explore next.
Execution outcomes are then added back to the persistent state, so later decisions can build on earlier attempts.
Because these assessments are used repeatedly during exploration, we distill them into a lightweight 9B student through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and on-policy distillation (OPD)  Agarwal et al. (2024) , improving task performance while reducing teacher API costs  Jolicoeur-Martineau (2025) .
This makes recovery cumulative: failed attempts provide evidence for subsequent exploration rather than simply triggering another retry.
Our contributions are: 
 • 
 We introduce a recovery controller that uses retained execution evidence and relations among repair hypotheses to update frontier priorities. It can reprioritize candidates beyond the latest attempt, while a separate diagnostic gate controls execution eligibility. 
 • 
 We develop an implicit world model that reasons over repair hypotheses, expected progress, and their relations, and distill this reasoning into a smaller student for efficient execution. 
 • 
 Across three terminal-based benchmarks, BeliefWeaver improves aggregate final score by 5.1 points over native Terminus-2 continuation and also improves exploration efficiency. The distilled 9B student gains 9.8 points over the reported native baseline while reducing estimated teacher API cost by 54.8%. 
 2 Related Work 
 World Models for Agentic Reasoning. 
World models let agents reason about possible future outcomes before acting. Prior work has explored both implicit predictive models and explicit executable ones. FLARE  Zheng et al. (2025) predicts latent future observations for robot control, while Qwen-AgentWorld  Zuo et al. (2026) models agent–environment transitions for general-purpose agents. RAP  Hao et al. (2023) and WebDreamer  Gu et al. (2024) use LLMs to predict outcomes during planning, and WKM  Qiao et al. (2024) combines global task knowledge with local state. Other work builds executable models for internal simulation, including WorldCoder  Tang et al. (2024) and Executable World Models  Rodionov (2026) ; PatchWorld  Bai et al. (2026) , OPINE-World  Courtis et al. (2026) , and Tycho  Lehmann et al. (2026) further support inspection, replay, and revision. Our work instead uses consequence predictions and verifier feedback to maintain a persistent belief over unresolved frontiers and guide exploration across them. 
 Long-horizon and Recursive Agents. 
Long-horizon agents face context growth, evidence loss, error propagation, and difficulty recovering from earlier decisions  Chen et al. (2026) ; Zhu et al. (2026) ; Sun et al. (2025) ; Liu et al. (2026a) . Recent work addresses these issues through persistent state, structured search, recursion, and recovery. Argus  Zhang et al. (2026e) uses a shared evidence graph to coordinate search. HTR  Jin et al. (2026) maintains a persistent tree of hypotheses, artifacts, evidence, and insights, propagating experimental findings to refine and guide later search. AgentRewind  Zhuang et al. (2026) restores earlier checkpoints while retaining memories from failed attempts. Recursive Language Models  Zhang et al. (2025) recurse over subproblems, while Recursive Agent Harnesses  Lumer et al. (2026) extend this to tool-using subagents. SciNav  Zhang and Sun (2026) frames scientific coding as search over alternative solution branches.
Our controller turns retained outcomes and frontier relations into explicit priority updates through unary evidence factors, including for candidates not executed in the latest attempt. A separate diagnostic gate determines which frontiers are eligible for execution. 
 Self-Improving and Uncertainty-Guided Agents. 
Recent agents increasingly learn from accumulated experience or use uncertainty to decide what to explore next. SkillRL  Xia et al. (2026) jointly evolves a hierarchical skill library and agent policy, while COS-PLAY  Wu et al. (2026) discovers and refines reusable skills from interaction trajectories. Darwin Gödel Machine  Zhang et al. (2026a) , Gödel Agent  Yin et al. (2025) , and Hyperagents  Zhang et al. (2026b) go further by iteratively modifying the agent’s code, logic, or improvement process. MLEvolve  Du et al. (2026) combines cross-branch search with retrospective memory, while Uncertainty-Guided Tree Search  Mhammedi and Cohan (2026) and Agentic Uncertainty  Zhang et al. (2026c) use epistemic uncertainty to guide exploration and reflection. Our work instead uses verifier-grounded evidence to update priorities over related frontiers, allowing the agent to revisit promising directions without changing the underlying agent. 
 3 Problem Formulation 
 Long-Horizon Problem Solving as Frontier Exploration. 
We consider an agent operating in a partially observable long-horizon environment, where decisions are conditioned on the interaction history h t h_{t} . Instead of modeling individual low-level actions, we formulate exploration over a time-varying set of problem-solving frontiers, 
 ℱ t = { f 1 , … , f N t } . \displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{N_{t}}\}. 
 Each frontier f i f_{i} is a testable repair hypothesis that specifies a possible failure mechanism, the evidence needed to assess it, and the checks used to evaluate the repair. At step t t , the agent either explores an eligible frontier or continues execution to gather evidence that can support, refine, or introduce new frontiers. The set ℱ t \mathcal{F}_{t} evolves over time, while i i identifies the same frontier across repeated attempts. 
 Persistent Exploration State. 
We maintain a relational graph 
 G t = ( V t , ℛ t ) , \displaystyle G_{t}=(V_{t},\mathcal{R}_{t}), 
 where V t V_{t} is the node set and ℛ t \mathcal{R}_{t} is the relation edge set. The graph connects frontiers with their attempts, observations, and checkpoints, and links frontiers that share or refine failure mechanisms. For each frontier f i f_{i} , we maintain a belief 
 b t i = q t i ​ ( z i = 1 ) , \displaystyle b_{t}^{i}=q_{t}^{i}(z_{i}=1), 
 where z i ∈ { 0 , 1 } z_{i}\in\{0,1\} indicates whether the frontier remains worth pursuing and q t i q_{t}^{i} is the corresponding belief distribution. We summarize the exploration state as 
 B t = ( G t , ℰ t , 𝐛 t ) , 𝐛 t = ( b t i ) f i ∈ ℱ t , \displaystyle B_{t}=(G_{t},\mathcal{E}_{t},\mathbf{b}_{t}),\qquad\mathbf{b}_{t}=(b_{t}^{i})_{f_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}, 
 where ℰ t \mathcal{E}_{t} contains accumulated diagnostic observations and execution feedback. Relations in G t G_{t} determine which observations activate unary evidence factors for each frontier, allowing new evidence to update the priority of related hypotheses beyond the latest attempt. New evidence e t + 1 e_{t+1} is incorporated through 
 B t + 1 = Update ⁡ ( B t , e t + 1 ) . \displaystyle B_{t+1}=\operatorname{Update}(B_{t},e_{t+1}). 
 The exploration state persists across environment rollback, so evidence from earlier attempts remains available for subsequent decisions. 
 Implicit World Modeling of Exploration. 
Belief updates summarize what has already been learned from exploration, while choosing how to proceed requires reasoning about unexplored directions. We model this prospective reasoning with an implicit world model (IWM), 
 ψ θ ​ ( B t , f i ) = ( d ^ t i , p ^ t i , r ^ t i ) , \displaystyle\psi_{\theta}(B_{t},f_{i})=\big(\hat{d}_{t}^{i},\hat{p}_{t}^{i},\hat{r}_{t}^{i}\big), 
 which assesses a candidate frontier f i f_{i} under the current exploration state B t B_{t} . Here, d ^ t i \hat{d}_{t}^{i} evaluates the hypothesis against available evidence, p ^ t i \hat{p}_{t}^{i} estimates its potential for repair progress, and r ^ t i \hat{r}_{t}^{i} captures its relations to other frontiers. These assessments guide frontier eligibility and belief revision, connect evidence across related hypotheses, and support frontier generation, refinement, and reopening. The model is implicit because it reasons directly from the exploration context rather than learning an explicit transition model of the environment. 
 Exploration Objective. 
The goal of exploration is to make sustained progress toward task completion under a limited execution budget. At each step, the agent uses the accumulated evidence and frontier beliefs in B t B_{t} to maintain and prioritize plausible frontiers, while allowing earlier hypotheses to be revisited as new evidence emerges. When no frontier is ready to pursue, execution continues to gather evidence and expose new repair directions. The resulting feedback supplies new evidence e t + 1 e_{t+1} for updating the exploration state. The concrete gating and ranking rules are introduced in the next section. 
 4 Methodology 
 Figure 1: Overview of BeliefWeaver . 
 BeliefWeaver combines evidence from past attempts with prospective reasoning over candidate repairs.
Given the task and current context ( Blue Box ), the implicit world model ( Yellow Box ) proposes repair hypotheses, gathers diagnostic evidence, and estimates their outcomes.
The persistent exploration graph ( Gray Dashed Box ) retains hypotheses, attempts, observations, and relations across rollback.
Belief-guided selection ( Purple Box ) uses this evidence to update frontier priorities, while diagnostic evidence determines execution eligibility.
The agent harness and verifier ( Pink Box ) execute the selected frontier and feed the outcome back into the graph. 
 We propose BeliefWeaver , a framework for long-horizon problem solving that organizes exploration around testable repair hypotheses and evidence-grounded beliefs over which directions remain worth pursuing (Figure  1 ). An Implicit World Model proposes and evaluates candidate frontiers using targeted diagnostics and estimates of repair progress, while a Persistent Global Belief State tracks their evidence and relations across attempts and rollback. Diagnostic evidence determines which frontiers are eligible for execution, and belief scores rank the remaining candidates. Execution outcomes are then added back to the state, allowing failed attempts to reshape related hypotheses and reconsider previously deprioritized alternatives. To reduce repeated teacher inference, we distill four recurring IWM roles into a 9B student while keeping the recovery controller and executor unchanged. 
 4.1 Implicit World Model 
 The implicit world model (IWM) represents unresolved failures as structured hypotheses over possible mechanisms and repairs. For each hypothesis, it asks three questions: what may be causing the failure, whether the available evidence supports that explanation, and what progress is expected from pursuing the repair. 
 Candidate Hypothesis Generation. 
For each unresolved failure, a language model receives the task requirements, line-indexed source context, current diagnostics, and previous attempts, and proposes a testable repair hypothesis . Each proposal describes a mismatch between the implementation and specification, a possible underlying mechanism, relevant source locations, evidence to collect, and a target check. Valid proposals are added to the frontier catalog for later evaluation. 
 Diagnostic Probing and Evidence Judgment. 
For each candidate, the controller generates a diagnostic command and runs it in a temporary copy of the current environment. An evidence judge compares the observation with the candidate’s predicted current behavior and returns 
 v t i ∈ { consistent , insufficient , contradicted } . v_{t}^{i}\in\{\texttt{consistent},\texttt{insufficient},\texttt{contradicted}\}. 
 The verdict determines execution eligibility, while contradictions also add refutation evidence to the belief state. 
 Prospective Evaluation. 
Two prompt-based experts estimate the consequences of pursuing each hypothesis. local_code traces the relevant control and data flow to estimate mechanism validity and progress under a bounded repair attempt, while contract_judge checks whether the current implementation violates the cited task requirement. Their outputs are combined into an estimate of strict progress using the mechanism selection and outcome composition in Appendix  D.3 . Relation queries also identify refinements and shared mechanisms among candidates, connecting unsuccessful attempts to related hypotheses. 
 Evidence verdicts determine execution eligibility and update beliefs, while candidate relations determine which relational evidence factors apply. Prospective estimates are used for candidate regeneration and conditional reopening. Selectable frontiers are ultimately ordered by the belief score in Section  4.2 . Prompt templates, thresholds, and update rules are given in Appendix  A.1 . 
 4.2 Persistent Global Belief State 
 Long-horizon recovery often returns to earlier checkpoints after unsuccessful interventions. We maintain a persistent belief state over repair hypotheses and their evidence so that outcomes from previous attempts can shape what to explore next. 
 We represent this state as an exploration graph G t G_{t} over frontiers, attempts, observations, and checkpoints. Each attempt is linked to the frontier it pursues and its starting checkpoint, while frontier–frontier relations capture shared mechanisms and hypothesis refinements. The graph and its evidence persist across rollback. 
 For each frontier f i f_{i} , we introduce a binary variable z i z_{i} indicating whether it remains active for exploration. Starting from a neutral prior, we aggregate the evidence associated with f i f_{i} as 
 q t i ​ ( z i ) = 1 Z t i ​ ϕ 0 i ​ ( z i ) ​ ∏ a ∈ 𝒜 t i ψ a ​ ( z i , ℰ t , G t ) , q_{t}^{i}(z_{i})=\frac{1}{Z_{t}^{i}}\phi_{0}^{i}(z_{i})\prod_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}^{i}}\psi_{a}(z_{i};\mathcal{E}_{t},G_{t}), 
 (1) 
 where ℰ t \mathcal{E}_{t} is the accumulated evidence, 𝒜 t i \mathcal{A}_{t}^{i} indexes the factors applicable to frontier i i , and Z t i Z_{t}^{i} is the normalization constant. We use b t i = q t i ​ ( z i = 1 ) b_{t}^{i}=q_{t}^{i}(z_{i}=1) as the frontier priority for subsequent exploration. 
 Relations in G t G_{t} determine which unary evidence factors apply to each frontier. This lets an outcome from one attempt change the priority of a related frontier, while each belief is computed independently by local normalization. 
 We use four types of evidence: repeated attempts, refinements, historical target feedback, and probe-based refutation. Repeated failures lower the priority of the same frontier, while an untried refinement can remain promising. Historical target feedback downweights candidates whose targets have not improved in earlier attempts, and contradictory probe observations provide direct refutation evidence. Full factor definitions and parameter settings are given in Appendix  A.2 . 
 4.3 Evidence-Gated Execution 
 The controller uses the persistent belief state to decide which frontier should guide the next execution. Open or reopened frontiers are ranked by 
 S t ​ ( f i ) = b t i − λ fail ​ ℓ t i , S_{t}(f_{i})=b_{t}^{i}-\lambda_{\mathrm{fail}}\,\ell_{t}^{i}, 
 (2) 
 where ℓ t i \ell_{t}^{i} indicates that the frontier’s most recent queue outcome is failure. 
 Ranking and execution eligibility are handled separately. Before execution, a frontier must receive a consistent evidence verdict and have a target that is not already satisfied. The highest-ranked eligible frontier is passed to the agent harness with its supporting evidence and execution context. If none qualifies, the agent continues without a frontier-specific focus. 
 The verifier then scores the resulting state. Improving checkpoints are retained, while unsuccessful updates are rolled back. In either case, the new observations and attempt history are added to the persistent state.  4.4 Small-Model IWM Distillation 
 The IWM relies on four recurring model-assisted roles: probe generation, evidence judgment, frontier-relation inference, and task-clause assessment. These roles use structured inputs and outputs and account for much of the repeated teacher inference, so we distill them into a smaller student model. 
 We first train the student with SFT using LoRA  Hu et al. (2022) on teacher-generated, role-specific request–response pairs. This gives the student the basic behavior and output structure needed to participate in exploration. 
 SFT covers states collected under teacher-guided exploration. We therefore use on-policy distillation (OPD) to train on contexts induced by the student’s own decisions. During collection, the student handles the four distilled roles, while a shadow teacher answers the same requests for supervision without replacing the student’s outputs. These teacher-labeled examples are combined with the SFT data and earlier OPD rounds, and training continues from the latest accepted adapter. After validation, the updated student is used for the next collection round. 
 Only these recurring IWM roles are transferred to the student; the recovery controller, executor, belief-update rules, and prospective evaluation remain unchanged. Training details and validation criteria are provided in Appendix  B . 
 5 Results 
 5.1 Experimental Setup 
 Experiment Design. 
We conduct two sets of experiments.
First, using DeepSeek V4 Pro  Xu et al. (2026) , we evaluate the contributions of structured continuation, world-model grounding, and persistent relational beliefs through controlled ablations.
Second, we distill these capabilities into Qwen3.5-9B  Qwen Team (2026) , comparing different distillation strategies in terms of task performance and teacher inference cost. 
 Benchmarks. 
We evaluate on 113 tasks from three terminal-based benchmarks: 65 from Terminal-Bench 2.0 (TB2), 23 from Terminal-Bench 3.0 (TB3)  Merrill et al. (2026) , and 25 from Long-Horizon Terminal-Bench (LHTB)  Li et al. (2026) . TB2 and TB3 cover diverse terminal tasks with test-based feedback, while LHTB emphasizes longer-horizon execution and provides score-only feedback. Together, they test recovery across different task lengths and levels of diagnostic information. Full task and evaluation details are given in Appendix  C .

We compare four configurations that progressively add components of BeliefWeaver :
(1) Terminus-2 , the native continuation baseline;

All configurations start from the same checkpoint with the same remaining execution budget. 
 Distillation. 
We evaluate three variants using Qwen3.5-9B  Qwen Team (2026) . SFT is trained on teacher responses, SFT + OPD adds teacher supervision collected during student-guided exploration, and SFT + OPD + Gen also lets the student generate repair hypotheses. 
 The student handles probe generation, evidence judgment, candidate-relation inference, and task-clause assessment in all three settings. Repair hypotheses remain teacher-generated for SFT and SFT + OPD , while SFT + OPD + Gen generates them with the student. Prospective evaluation remains teacher-based. 
 Both SFT and OPD train LoRA adapters with response-token cross-entropy while keeping the base model frozen. Each OPD round adds new teacher-labeled examples to the SFT data and earlier OPD data. All variants use the same executor and belief-update rules. API savings are measured against the matched teacher run. Training data and hyperparameters are given in Appendix  B . 
 Evaluation protocol and metrics. 
For each paired comparison, methods start from the same checkpoint and receive the same remaining execution budget. We report Final as the test-pass fraction or graded reward of the final retained checkpoint (%). Step-AUC and Time-AUC summarize performance over agent steps and executor time, using a shared per-task step horizon and the remaining execution-time budget, respectively. Both are normalized and scaled by 100. We also track executor usage, execution time, and controller or teacher API usage during continuation. All metrics are averaged equally across tasks, with Overall pooling tasks across benchmarks. Additional accounting and evaluation details are provided in Appendix  C . 
 5.2 Main Results 
 Improved task performance and exploration efficiency. 
Compared with native Terminus-2 continuation, BeliefWeaver improves Overall Final performance by 5.1 percentage points, with the largest gain on TB3 (+10.1 points). It also improves Overall Step-AUC by 2.8 points and Time-AUC by 1.6 points, achieving the strongest aggregate results among the teacher configurations. The gains in Final and Step-AUC are significant relative to the native baseline (Table  2 ). Together, these results show that BeliefWeaver not only reaches better retained checkpoints, but also makes useful progress earlier during continuation. This pattern is consistent with more effective recovery rather than gains appearing only at the end of execution. 
 Component ablations support the combined design. 
We use component ablations to examine the role of each stage in the full framework. Structured Continuation improves Overall Final, Step-AUC, and Time-AUC over Terminus-2 by 2.9, 0.9, and 1.1 points, respectively. Adding WM Grounding alone reduces Overall Final by 1.7 points, with small declines in both AUC metrics. Adding persistent relational beliefs and evidence-gated execution on top of grounding improves Overall Final by 3.9 points, along with gains of 2.4 in Step-AUC and 1.0 in Time-AUC.
The best results come from using grounding together with the full recovery controller, which updates frontier priorities from retained evidence and separately gates execution with diagnostic evidence. Since these two components are introduced together, their individual effects are not isolated. 
 Executor savings come with greater controller overhead.

Compared with Terminus-2 , BeliefWeaver reduces executor steps by 13.2%, execution time by 17.8%, and executor cost by 16.6%, while achieving a higher Final score. The full system uses slightly more executor resources than the intermediate ablations but attains the best task performance. It also requires more controller calls, with 33.4 reservations per task compared with 9.1 or fewer for the other configurations.  5.3 Distillation Results 
 Distillation transfers the recurring IWM decisions, while OPD yields a higher final score. 
We next ask whether the model-assisted IWM roles can be moved to a smaller student without losing the benefits of evidence-guided recovery. SFT already improves substantially over native Terminus-2 , suggesting that these structured decisions can be learned from teacher supervision rather than requiring the full teacher at every step.
OPD then trains the student on contexts produced by its own exploration. SFT + OPD reaches 58.4% Overall Final, 9.8 points above the native baseline, with a significant Final gain (Table  2 ). Its Final point estimate is also 4.7 points above the matched teacher. This suggests that on-policy supervision can do more than reproduce teacher outputs: it can adapt the distilled IWM roles to the states the student actually encounters during recovery.
Overall, the results support a simple division of labor: SFT provides the structured IWM behavior, while OPD adapts it to the student’s exploration distribution. The recovery controller and executor remain unchanged, so the gains come from replacing and refining the recurring model-assisted decisions rather than distilling the entire agent. 
 Student-generated hypotheses yield the largest teacher savings. 
We assess distillation efficiency using executor time, teacher API requests, and estimated API cost from recorded token usage at $1/$4 per million input/output tokens. Across the tasks in Table  4 , both SFT and SFT + OPD cut teacher requests and API cost by about 55% relative to the matched teacher. Moving repair-hypothesis generation to the student yields larger savings: SFT + OPD + Gen reduces teacher requests by 74.5% and API cost by 81.9%, lowering the average teacher cost from $0.550 to $0.100 per task. It also reaches 57.5% Final, 3.8 points above the matched teacher. Teacher token usage follows the same pattern, decreasing by 55.4%, 55.1%, and 81.1% for SFT , SFT + OPD , and SFT + OPD + Gen , respectively.
Overall, distillation substantially reduces teacher inference while maintaining strong task performance, with the largest savings when hypothesis generation is also moved to the student. These savings refer to teacher API usage rather than total end-to-end inference cost. 
 6 Conclusion 
 We presented BeliefWeaver , a framework for long-horizon failure recovery built around an evidence-guided recovery controller. The controller carries verifier-grounded evidence across attempts and rollback, uses relations among repair hypotheses to update frontier priorities, and separates execution eligibility from belief-based ranking. An implicit world model supports this process by generating and evaluating repair hypotheses, estimating prospective progress, and identifying relations among frontiers.
Across three terminal-based benchmarks, BeliefWeaver improves task performance and exploration efficiency over native continuation. Distilling the recurring controller roles into a smaller student substantially reduces teacher API usage and cost while preserving strong task performance.
Future work will study learned and uncertainty-aware belief updates, finer attribution among controller components, and recovery under sparse feedback or imperfect rollback. 
 Limitations 
 Our framework has several limitations. Errors in IWM predictions or inferred relations can mis-rank frontiers and waste exploration effort. The current belief state also models only a small set of relations between repair hypotheses, which may be too limited when several failure causes interact. Finally, our experiments focus on terminal-based tasks with verifier feedback and reliable checkpoint restoration. It remains unclear how well the framework transfers to settings where feedback is sparse or rollback is noisy or unavailable.

 Appendix A Controller Details 
 Table  5 summarizes the controller settings used throughout the experiments. The following subsections describe how these settings enter belief updates, frontier selection, and execution. 
 A.1 Implicit World Model 
 Candidate generation. 
The generator receives the task requirements, line-indexed source context, current diagnostics, existing candidates, and previous attempts. It returns structured repair hypotheses containing a proposed mechanism, the observed and required behavior, supporting source references, evidence to collect, a target check, and an optional parent candidate. The controller validates the required fields and references before adding them to the frontier catalog. 
 Probes and evidence verdicts. 
For each candidate, a separate model call generates a diagnostic command from the hypothesis and requested evidence. The command is executed in a temporary checkpoint copy, subject to the timeout and output-retention limits in Table  5 . The evidence judge compares the observed output and return code against the candidate’s claimed current behavior: consistent indicates supporting evidence, contradicted indicates conflicting evidence, and insufficient is used when the probe does not resolve the claim. These verdicts are used by the evidence factors and execution gate described in Appendices  A.2 and  A.3 . 
 Prospective evaluation and relations. 
The local_code expert estimates mechanism validity and expected repair progress from the relevant control and data flow, while contract_judge checks the current implementation against the cited task requirement. A separate relation query identifies links such as same_mechanism and supports between candidates. These signals guide candidate regeneration and frontier reopening as exploration progresses, using the thresholds in Table  5 .

 A.3 Evidence-Gated Execution 
 Selection and gating. 
 Only open or reopened frontiers are considered for execution. Candidates are ranked by 
 S t ​ ( f i ) = b t i − λ fail ​ ℓ t i , S_{t}(f_{i})=b_{t}^{i}-\lambda_{\mathrm{fail}}\ell_{t}^{i},

 where ℓ t i \ell_{t}^{i} indicates whether the most recent attempt on f i f_{i} failed and λ fail \lambda_{\mathrm{fail}} is given in Table  5 . The controller selects the highest-ranked candidate with a current consistent evidence verdict whose target is not already passing. Blocking verifier failures take precedence and trigger continuation aimed at restoring execution or producing the required deliverable. If no frontier passes the evidence gate, or exploration stagnates, the controller falls back to plain continuation. 
 Execution. 
 For a selected frontier, the agent receives the task, current verification state, repair hypothesis, supporting evidence, and relevant attempt history. Plain continuation uses the same executor without frontier-specific context. After verification, improved checkpoints are retained and otherwise the environment is rolled back, while accumulated evidence and exploration history persist for subsequent belief updates.

 Distillation setup. 
We distill four roles into a shared Qwen3.5-9B student: probe generation, evidence judgment, candidate-relation inference, and task-clause assessment.
SFT and SFT + OPD retain teacher-based hypothesis generation, while SFT + OPD + Gen adds a separate generation adapter trained from the SFT checkpoint alongside the OPD adapter.
Prospective evaluation remains teacher-based, with belief updates, execution gating, and task execution unchanged.
Final evaluation tasks are excluded from SFT and OPD data collection, hypothesis-generation training, and development-based model selection.

 B.2 On-Policy Distillation 
 Collection and weighting. 
OPD starts from the SFT student and follows the round and task allocation in Table  6 . For each task, teacher-reference and student-assisted runs start from the same initial checkpoint. The student serves the four distilled roles, while a shadow teacher labels the same requests; failed student calls may fall back to the teacher. We retain valid teacher-labeled examples from student-induced contexts, teacher-reference runs, and fallback calls, and deduplicate identical request–target pairs within each batch. Examples are upweighted when student and teacher disagree on evidence verdicts or on same_mechanism / supports relations, in addition to the task–role balancing used for SFT. 
 Iterative updates and evaluation. 
At round k k , we train on the original SFT data together with the retained OPD data from rounds 1 , … , k 1,\ldots,k , starting from the latest accepted adapter. Training uses the same response-token objective as SFT, with checkpoint selection on the combined development set. After each round, we evaluate the adapter on the fixed SFT development set. We retain it if parser acceptance is at least 95% for every distilled role and its evidence-verdict agreement with the teacher is within 2 percentage points of the previous accepted adapter, where A prev A_{\mathrm{prev}} denotes the latter agreement (Table  6 ). The retained adapter is then used for the next round of data collection. The final student is the adapter retained after the last round, and we evaluate it from fresh initial checkpoints on the full downstream benchmarks using the same controller and execution protocol. 
 Hypothesis-generation extension. 
SFT + OPD + Gen combines the final OPD adapter for the four controller roles with a separate hypothesis-generation adapter initialized from SFT.
The generator replaces teacher-based candidate generation, proposing testable failure mechanisms and repair directions based on task requirements, code context, and execution feedback.
It is trained on teacher request–hypothesis pairs using response-token cross-entropy.
After filtering sequences longer than 65,536 tokens, the dataset contains 609 training and 150 validation examples.
Training runs for one epoch at a learning rate of 10 − 5 10^{-5} (Table  6 ). 
 Downstream evaluation. 
We evaluate the matched teacher and student configurations from the same initial checkpoints under identical execution budgets and verification procedures.
All configurations share the same executor, controller logic, and belief-update rules, differing only in model-role assignments.
Shadow-teacher labeling and teacher fallback are disabled during evaluation.
Task coverage and metrics are detailed in Appendix  C . 
 Appendix C Evaluation Protocol and Metrics 
 C.1 Evaluation Procedure 
 Tasks and shared settings. 
We evaluate 113 tasks: 65 from TB2, 23 from TB3, and 25 from LHTB.
For each task, we run the executor until its first stop or half the total execution-time budget, then initialize all continuation methods from the same checkpoint.
All methods share the executor, environment, verification procedure, and remaining budget.
TB2 and TB3 provide scores and failing-test names, while LHTB provides scores only.
For student evaluation, we rerun the matched teacher alongside SFT, SFT + OPD, and SFT + OPD + Gen from shared checkpoints, with shadow-teacher labeling and teacher fallback disabled.
To ensure task separation, we exclude all evaluation tasks from SFT and OPD data collection, hypothesis-generation training, and development-based model selection. 
 Execution budgets. 
Each task i i has a total executor-time budget:

1200\ \mathrm{s},&\text{TB3},\\
720\ \mathrm{s},&\text{LHTB}.\end{cases} 
 For TB2, budgets range from 600 to 1,800 seconds, depending on the task.

 C.2 Metric Computation 
 Final score. 
Final measures task performance at the last retained checkpoint, excluding rejected trials.
For checkpoints with multiple tests, we compute the score as max ⁡ ( r , p / ( p + f ) ) \max(r,p/(p+f)) , where r r is the official reward and p p and f f are the numbers of passed and failed tests.
For single-score tasks, including LHTB, or checkpoints without test counts, we use the official graded reward.
Final scores are reported as percentages.
