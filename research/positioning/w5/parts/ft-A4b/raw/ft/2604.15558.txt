 Preregistered Belief Revision Contracts 
 Saad Alqithami
 Email:  alqithami@gmail.com 
 Abstract 
 Multi-agent language-model systems can change their answers in response to social cues without acquiring new externally validated evidence. We study PBRC ( Preregistered Belief Revision Contracts ), which associate public evidence conditions with permitted updates to an explicit belief state. A router enforces the selected contract branch and records a certificate for each accepted non-fallback transition. Under evidence gating and an argmax-preserving fallback, token-empty interaction preserves the top-hypothesis set; a nonamplifying fallback also prevents growth of the specified confidence functional. We characterize these social-only properties by local transition conditions and distinguish behavior-preserving normal forms from safety-enforcing restrictions. For token-invariant contracts, identical validated-token exposure traces induce identical enforced trajectories under the specified routing assumptions. Under synchronous flooding, accumulated token knowledge follows ordinary distance reachability, whereas incoming exposure follows positive-length walks; this distinction yields exact trace characterizations and the standard tight diameter bound for universal knowledge closure. We give source-labelled accountability conditions for finite execution logs, prove soundness of a companion KD45/PDL specification system, and establish bounded assignment certificates for a guarded conjunctive trigger fragment. Controlled simulations and a matched synthetic study illustrate the conditional guarantees and vulnerability to correlated label errors. KAIROS experiments demonstrate token-empty answer retention on 3,000 rows. A separate 200-claim SciFact study records evidence-enabled admission: PBRC achieves 53.0% accuracy, versus 49.0% for RAW and 61.5% for clean RAG; its gain over RAW is statistically inconclusive, with 12 harmful and 20 beneficial answer changes. The framework governs external protocol state and accepted outputs; it does not by itself establish semantic truth, calibrated probabilities, or improved task accuracy.

 1 Introduction 
 Deliberation can improve the information available to an agent, but it can also make agreement, confidence, prestige, or majority size substitutes for independently checkable reasons. Recent LLM studies report conformity and peer-pressure effects [ Weng et al.(2025) , Song et al.(2025) ] , including sensitivity to topology and self–social weighting [ Han et al.(2026) ] . Emergent conventions and collective biases are related but distinct phenomena [ Ashery et al.(2025) ] . These results motivate a protocol question: which changes to a public belief state should a deliberation system permit, and what evidence should accompany them? 
 We introduce PBRC , a public contract associating first-order event predicates with permitted update operators, a deterministic priority order, and a fallback. A triggered update requires a nonempty validated witness, full-event satisfaction of the selected trigger, and faithful execution of its operator. Fallback remains an explicit transition and may reduce confidence without changing the top set. Thus the framework controls the admissibility of externally represented transitions rather than asserting that every numerical change must carry new evidence. 
 The separation between communication and admissible revision is conditional. An evidential trigger merely requires the presence of a validated token; it can still depend on message metadata unless token invariance is imposed. A signature authenticates an artifact, but does not make its semantic label correct. A state-holding router enforces the public state, but does not directly constrain an LLM’s private reasoning. These distinctions delimit all claims below. 
 1.1 Contributions 
 We develop five connected results. 
 1. 
 Contracts, witnesses, and enforcement. Section  4 separates full-event trigger selection, restricted witness checking, and numerical operator compliance. The default semantics uses an authoritative state-holding router. 
 2. 
 Conditional social-only guarantees. Sections  6 – 7 prove top-set stability and maximum-confidence nonamplification under conservative fallback, and characterize these properties by the actually selected one-step transition (Theorem  6 ). Evidence gating is a sufficient design discipline, not a necessary syntactic form for every robust protocol. 
 3. 
 Normal forms and licensed transitions. Section  8 gives behavior-preserving and enforcement-based translations under explicit witness and routing hypotheses. The former may absorb unsafe behavior into fallback; it does not create safety. Under faithful enforcement, each top-set change has an accepted certificate licensing that transition (Theorem  17 ). 
 4. 
 Token-trace factorization and flooding. Token-invariant contracts separate rhetorical presentation from transition inputs under token-determined or complete routing. Incoming exposure is characterized by positive-length walk relations (Theorems  12 and 15 ); accumulated knowledge has the standard tight diameter bound (Corollary  6 ). Universal trace equivalence includes adjacency equality and is not claimed as a nontrivial graph compression. 
 5. 
 Threat assumptions, specification, and checking. Sections  9 – 12 separate provenance, semantic correctness, replay age, and validator compromise. A KD45/PDL specification system is sound, and its belief-free fragment is checkable on faithful finite logs. Guarded existential triggers admit small assignment certificates with linear logical verification cost after lookup indexing; search and validation costs remain separate. Section  13 evaluates protocol behavior through controlled simulations, a matched synthetic study, and KAIROS and SciFact experiments. 
 Scope. The guarantees concern fixed finite hypothesis sets, public transition inputs, conservative fallback, and faithful enforcement. Truth preservation, statistical calibration, secure evidence generation, dynamic hypothesis expansion, and empirical benefit under new evidence require additional assumptions or evaluation. The logical results establish soundness and finite-model monitoring. 
 1.2 Relation to underlying revision operators 
 PBRC leaves the choice of numerical update operator to the contract designer. It is compatible with evidence aggregation but does not establish AGM rationality postulates for probability vectors, select a universally optimal inference rule, or make social consensus intrinsically evidential. It constrains a recorded transition according to public rules. The relation to runtime enforcement, evidence logics, and agent protocols is discussed next. 
 The operational model and contract semantics appear in Sections  3 – 4 , followed by a worked example and the formal guarantees. Sections  9 – 12 address threats, monitoring, implementation, and cost. Section  13 presents the experimental methods and results. Appendix  A gives the proofs of logical soundness and finite-model monitoring. 
 2 Related work 
 Empirical studies of LLM multi-agent systems (MAS) document systematic conformity and peer-pressure effects whose strength depends on the interaction protocol, peer reliability, rapport, and network structure. BenchForm provides a benchmarked characterization of conformity across interaction protocols [ Weng et al.(2025) ] . KAIROS studies peer-pressure under heterogeneous peer reliability and rapport [ Song et al.(2025) ] . Recent results also emphasize that topology and self–social weighting can modulate conformity and “wrong-but-sure” cascades [ Han et al.(2026) ] . Complementary work adapts classic social-psychology paradigms and reports that AI agents exhibit conformity patterns aligned with Social Impact Theory [ Bellina et al.(2026) ] , connecting to foundational human-group evidence on conformity and social impact [ Asch(1951) , Latané(1981) ] . Our contribution concerns admissibility rather than measuring conformity: PBRC specifies when a public state transition is licensed. Rhetoric independence additionally requires token-invariant predicates and operators. 
 Repeated interaction among LLM agents can also yield emergent conventions and collective biases [ Ashery et al.(2025) ] ; the earlier preprint is a version of the same study [ Ashery et al.(2024) ] . These phenomena motivate mechanisms that distinguish coordination signals from epistemic justification. PBRC targets the epistemic side: contracts restrict which belief transitions are admissible and require certificates with validated evidence tokens, so convention formation cannot by itself justify epistemic flips absent evidence. 
 Belief revision and belief-change formalisms provide the canonical language for rational change of epistemic states. AGM belief revision axiomatizes rational postulates for theory change [ Alchourrón et al.(1985) , Gärdenfors(1988) , Hansson(2026) ] . Belief update distinguishes revising by new information from updating after world change [ Katsuno and Mendelzon(1991) ] , and iterated revision studies sequential change [ Darwiche and Pearl(1997) ] . Ranking-theoretic approaches provide alternative representations of epistemic states and revision dynamics [ Spohn(2012) ] . Dynamic epistemic logic (DEL) models informational actions and announcements [ Baltag and Renne(2026) , van Ditmarsch et al.(2007) ] , and dynamic logics of belief change connect AGM-style ideas with dynamic and plausibility semantics in both single- and multi-agent settings [ van Benthem and Smets(2015) ] . PBRC does not propose a new underlying revision operator; instead it constrains which transitions are admissible under social interaction via preregistered, token-witnessable triggers, and it supports auditing via checkable certificates. Relatedly, belief merging and judgment aggregation study how to combine information from multiple sources under inconsistency [ Konieczny and Pérez(2002) , Konieczny and Pérez(2011) ] ; PBRC is compatible with such operators but adds an enforceable evidence gate for when they may be applied. 
 Runtime enforcement already studies monitors that restrict or transform executions according to specified policies [ Schneider(2000) , Ligatti et al.(2005) ] . PBRC uses that established architecture for evidence-labelled transitions of a public belief state; the presence of a trusted gate is not itself a new enforcement theorem. Evidence-based dynamic logics also distinguish evidence structures from resulting beliefs [ van Benthem and Pacuit(2011) ] . Our contribution lies in the explicit combination of contract selection, validated token witnesses, router-held numerical state, and exposure-trace analysis, under the assumptions stated in each result. 
 Combining belief modalities with dynamic/program operators has a substantial history in dynamic doxastic logic and related systems [ Leitgeb and Segerberg(2007) , Schmidt and Tishkovsky(2008) ] ; see [ Fagin et al.(1995) , van Benthem(2011) ] for broader background on multi-agent epistemic/doxastic logic and logical dynamics. Our novelty is not the presence of KD45 + PDL per se, but the use of program structure to encode PBRC contracts and to specify invariants over enforced, certificate-carrying belief transitions (Section  10 ). 
 Compliance monitoring in MAS is often framed in terms of commitment-based protocols and social/interactional commitments [ Singh(1999) , Yolum and Singh(2002) ] . PBRC enables compliance monitoring for epistemic transitions: accepted non-fallback transitions require verifiable evidence tokens and witness sets that an external router or auditor can check; fallback can still change numerical confidence. This certificate discipline is reminiscent of proof-carrying mechanisms, where untrusted producers attach checkable artifacts enabling efficient validation by a consumer [ Necula(1997) ] . Tamper-evident audit logs are a standard accountability primitive [ Schneier and Kelsey(1999) ] ; PBRC certificates are designed to be stored in such append-only logs. If one additionally wants to bind correct execution of updates (not just admissibility), verifiable computation and succinct proof systems provide relevant primitives [ Gennaro et al.(2010) , Parno et al.(2013) ] (Section  4.4 ). 
 Trust and reputation models are another common response to unreliable peers in MAS [ Sabater and Sierra(2005) ] . PBRC takes a complementary stance: rather than granting epistemic force to socially mediated reputation, it treats reliability as an evidence problem to be expressed through verifiable artifacts (tool provenance, signed attestations, audited verifier judgments). Socially persuasive claims remain non-binding absent checkable evidence. 
 Our notion of “wrong-but-sure” cascades connects to classic informational cascades and herding in economics and social learning, where agents update based on others’ actions and can converge on incorrect beliefs [ Banerjee(1992) , Bikhchandani et al.(1992) ] . Networked aggregation and Bayesian social learning further show how topology shapes convergence and error propagation [ DeGroot(1974) , Bala and Goyal(1998) , Golub and Jackson(2010) , Acemoglu et al.(2011) ] . PBRC differs in that it does not prescribe a specific update rule; instead, it constrains which belief transitions are admissible via preregistered, checkable evidence triggers, yielding a factorization through token dissemination when the contract and router satisfy token invariance. 
 Recent LLM MAS often use debate/critique/reflection patterns to improve reasoning [ Liang et al.(2023) , Li et al.(2023) , Shinn et al.(2023) , Madaan et al.(2023) ] . Practical orchestration frameworks operationalize such protocols in agentic programming settings [ Wu et al.(2023) , Hong et al.(2023) ] . These approaches are typically implemented as prompting patterns without externally checkable admissibility constraints; PBRC is complementary, providing enforceable constraints that can prevent purely social persuasion steps absent admissible evidence. 
 Evidence tokens can be instantiated using retrieval-augmented and tool-augmented LLM pipelines, where snippets, citations, and tool outputs provide structured artifacts that a verifier can validate [ Lewis et al.(2020) , Yao et al.(2022) , Nakano et al.(2021) , Menick et al.(2022) , Schick et al.(2023) ] . Retrieval-and-revise methods and hallucination detectors can further serve as candidate verifiers or token labelers [ Asai et al.(2024) , Manakul et al.(2023) ] . On the systems side, provenance standards such as W3C PROV provide a natural representation for evidence lineage [ W3C(2013) ] . PBRC abstracts these implementation details into a token interface ( Valid \mathrm{Valid} / Supports \mathrm{Supports} / Contradicts \mathrm{Contradicts} / Type \mathrm{Type} / Fresh \mathrm{Fresh} ) and studies protocol-level guarantees when admissibility is enforced via checkable certificates. 
 Taken together, these lines of work show that social interaction can induce conformity, cascades, and convention formation, while belief-change logics and systems mechanisms provide tools for specifying and enforcing rational updates. PBRC connects these perspectives by making social robustness and auditability enforceable at the protocol level: preregistered contracts specify which evidence patterns license which updates; routers enforce admissibility by routing token-empty steps to fallback; and auditors can inspect the accepted license alongside full transition inputs and outcomes. 
 3 Operational model: evidence-annotated deliberation 
 We formalize a deliberation run among agents who exchange messages containing evidence tokens . Tokens represent checkable artifacts (tool outputs, verified citations, signed sensor readings, etc.). The model separates persuasive language (unvalidated message content) from validated evidence (tokens that satisfy Valid \mathrm{Valid} under a router-side validity layer).

 3.2 Belief state as a router-side artifact (LLM instantiation) 
 The formal object b i t ∈ Δ ⁡ ( ℋ ) b_{i}^{t}\in\Delta(\mathcal{H}) is a protocol state used for deliberation, enforcement, and auditing. In deployed LLM-agent systems it is often unreasonable to assume that agents maintain stable, calibrated internal Bayesian beliefs, or that self-reported probabilities are trustworthy. PBRC does not require these assumptions: a router/orchestrator can maintain an authoritative belief state and compute contract updates itself (Section  4.4 ), while agents contribute candidate hypotheses, arguments, and evidence tokens.

 Open-ended tasks and dynamic hypothesis sets. 
 For open-ended tasks, a practical pattern is to maintain a dynamic hypothesis set ℋ t \mathcal{H}_{t} produced by candidate generation and pruning. A proposed extension would treat hypothesis introduction as a contract-governed operation: new hypotheses may enter ℋ t \mathcal{H}_{t} only when an evidential trigger fires (e.g., a validated retrieval token that introduces a new entity/answer), while social-only rounds cannot expand ℋ t \mathcal{H}_{t} . Formally, one can model each episode as a sequence of stages , each with a fixed finite hypothesis set. A stage-transition operator (triggered by evidence) expands ℋ \mathcal{H} and reinitializes b b over the expanded set. The theorems below apply within each fixed stage. Correctness of an expansion or reinitialization operator requires a separate specification and is not established here. 
 Takeaway.

 4.4 Enforcement semantics, trust boundary, and operator compliance 
 Certificates make belief revision auditable as an admissibility claim.
A distinct trust boundary concerns operator compliance : even with a valid witness, can an agent output an arbitrary next belief/decision while claiming it applied U i , j ⋆ U_{i,j^{\star}} ?
We therefore distinguish three enforcement architectures and state explicitly which results depend on which. 
 (A) Gate-only admissibility checking. 
 A minimal output gate verifies admissibility (e.g., W ≠ ∅ W\neq\emptyset , W ⊆ 𝒯 ⁡ ( E ) W\subseteq\mathcal{T}(E) , and E ⊧ W φ i , ℓ E\models_{W}\varphi_{i,\ell} ) and then accepts or rejects an agent-supplied update.
This supports checking admissibility of the certificate, but the numerical router-projection/normal-form results in Section  8.5 additionally require honest or verified operator execution. The gate does not, by itself, guarantee that an accepted numerical transition equals applying U i , ℓ U_{i,\ell} to the authoritative prior state unless agents are assumed honest with respect to operators. 
 (B) Proof-carrying updates (verifiable computation). 
 To enforce operator compliance without executing U i , j U_{i,j} inside the router, the agent can attach a proof that its output equals
 U i , j ⋆ ​ ( b i t , E i t ) U_{i,j^{\star}}(b_{i}^{t},E_{i}^{t}) (or equals the contractually specified decision rule), in the spirit of proof-carrying mechanisms [ Necula(1997) ] .
We treat this as an optional deployment path; our logical development does not depend on any specific proof system. 
 (C) State-holding routers (router-executed dynamics; default). 
 A practically common and conceptually clean architecture is a state-holding router that maintains the authoritative protocol state and executes the contract update itself.
Concretely, the router stores b i t b_{i}^{t} , validates tokens, checks triggers, extracts a witness, and then sets 
 b i t + 1 := { U i , j ⋆ ​ ( b i t , E i t ) , if a trigger  j ⋆  fires with a nonempty witness , δ i ​ ( b i t , E i t ) , otherwise , b_{i}^{t+1}:=\begin{cases}U_{i,j^{\star}}(b_{i}^{t},E_{i}^{t}),&\text{if a trigger $j^{\star}$ fires with a nonempty witness},\\
\delta_{i}(b_{i}^{t},E_{i}^{t}),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases} 
 while recording the corresponding certificate π i t \pi_{i}^{t} .
Unless stated otherwise, all results that reason about belief trajectories b i t b_{i}^{t} are interpreted under this router-executed semantics.
Gate-only checking (A) supports certificate-admissibility statements. Audit equivalence as defined below includes numerical beliefs and therefore also requires operator compliance. Rejections record the proposed certificate and rejection reason separately from the executed fallback label.

 5 Running example: “peer pressure” misinformation triage 
 We use a minimal running example to illustrate three distinct phenomena that PBRC separates by construction:
(i) purely social persuasion (rhetoric without validated evidence),
(ii) evidence-gated belief revision (admissible changes of mind justified by tokens and witnesses), and
(iii) the distinction between replay (stale-but-valid evidence) and forgery (manufactured “valid” evidence). 
 We will return to this example throughout the paper to ground the formal results. Specifically, we will show how the social-only guarantees (Section  6 ) prevent the cascade described in Section  5.2 , how the normal form representation (Section  8 ) canonicalizes the evidence-gated revision in Section  5.3 , and how the adversary models (Section  9 ) formalize the replay and forgery attacks in Section  5.4 .

 5.4 Replay versus forgery (preview) 
 This example also clarifies two orthogonal integrity threats. 
 Replay. A peer may retransmit an old token τ ⋆ \tau^{\star} that was once valid but is no longer contextually applicable (e.g., wrong claim instance, stale timestamp, or mismatched retrieval context).
If Valid ⁡ ( τ ) \mathrm{Valid}(\tau) does not bind tokens to an instance identifier and a freshness condition, then replayed evidence can satisfy φ 1 \varphi_{1} despite being irrelevant.
This is addressed by freshness/context predicates inside triggers (Section  9 ), e.g., requiring Fresh Δ \mathrm{Fresh}_{\Delta} and/or an instance hash match as part of admissibility. 
 Forgery. If an adversary can fabricate tokens that pass Valid \mathrm{Valid} (e.g., by compromising validators or the verification channel), then it can manufacture admissible “evidence” for arbitrary conclusions.
If the forged certificate passes all full-event and priority checks, is accepted, and its selected operator produces an adverse output at the current belief, that output occurs (Theorem  19 ). Forgeability alone does not imply that any chosen contract can be forced to flip. Integrity, applicability, operator design, and faithful execution all remain relevant. 
 6 Main guarantees (theorem-centric) 
 This section establishes the core social-dynamics guarantees of PBRC .
The central theme is that, under evidential contracts and conservative fallback, purely social interaction cannot (i) increase confidence or (ii) induce a new high-confidence wrong consensus. 
 To orient the reader, we proceed in four steps. First, we define the necessary properties of fallback operators (Section  6.1 ). Second, we prove that in token-empty (social-only) rounds, confidence cannot increase (Section  6.2 ). Third, we establish the central impossibility result: purely conformity-driven cascades cannot occur under PBRC (Section  6.3 ). Finally, we provide a minimal topology statement showing why identical admitted evidence yields identical beliefs under an evidence-additive policy (Section  6.4 ). These formal results directly explain the cascade suppression observed in the misinformation triage example (Section  5 ).

 7 Minimality and necessity results 
 This section formalizes a class of auditable trigger protocols and characterizes their behavior under evidence-free (social-only) interaction. Fallback constraints apply only where fallback can be selected; trigger constraints apply to the actual selected transition. The results are intentionally elementary: they are “one-step” necessity statements that isolate which part of a protocol must carry the burden of social robustness. 
 We structure this analysis as follows. First, we define the general class of auditable trigger protocols (Section  7.1 ). Second, we formalize evidence-free robustness properties (Section  7.2 ). Third, we prove that fallback operators must be conservative to satisfy these properties (Section  7.3 ). Finally, we characterize robustness through every selected social-only transition and show why syntactic evidence gating is sufficient but not necessary (Section  7.4 ).

 Example 1 (Why evidence gating is not a necessary syntactic condition) . 
 A sole always-true trigger with operator V ⁡ ( b , E ) = ( 1 − λ ) ​ b + λ ​ u V(b,E)=(1-\lambda)b+\lambda u , 0 < λ < 1 0<\lambda<1 , preserves the full top set and never increases maximum confidence, including on token-empty events. A lower-priority trigger that is never selected is likewise unconstrained by an immunity property of realized transitions. 
 Remark 5 (Stability, confidence, and calibration) . 
 Theorems  4 – 6 constrain actual selected transitions on social-only inputs. Evidence gating plus conservative fallback is one sufficient implementation of these conditions. Maximum-confidence nonamplification is not statistical calibration, and does not establish improved accuracy or a better proper scoring rule. 
 8 PBRC normal form, equivalence notions, and representation 
 The local characterization in Section  7 constrains behavior, not a unique syntax. We now distinguish two transformations. A behavior-preserving normal form moves the original social-only behavior into fallback; it preserves trajectories but need not make an unsafe protocol safe. Evidence-gated enforcement instead retains the original fallback and can suppress social-only triggered behavior. The results below assume the specified witness extractors exist and that original trigger predicates are expressible in the target contract language. For FO contracts, the original triggers are therefore assumed FO-definable. 
 We compare numerical trajectories and projected certificate traces. Canonicalizing an empty witness to the fallback label is a mathematical projection of the audit record, not an instruction to delete an agent’s original proposal or to certify that an arbitrary ungated social transition was conservative. Complete logs retain proposals, decisions, and actual updates.

 8.5 Routers, enforcement, and router-level normal form 
 PBRC is designed to be enforceable : a system-level router (or “output gate”) can reject belief changes that are not justified by a verifiable, nonempty evidence witness. The audit-equivalence notion in Section  8 compares canonicalized certificates, but it does not yet capture the operational effect of enforcement, where the gate may override a proposed update and force fallback. We now formalize this enforcement semantics and show that it induces a canonical evidence-gated normal form.

 8.5.2 Token-sufficiency and persuasion separation 
 Router-level enforcement projects protocols onto evidence-gated dynamics (Theorems  8 – 9 ). The next result sharpens this into a separation principle : under natural invariance assumptions, the validated token set carried by an event is a sufficient statistic for the enforced update. Consequently, altering rhetoric, prestige cues, confidence posturing, or majority claims—without changing validated tokens—cannot affect accepted belief change.

 8.5.4 Topology-to-trace structural conditions under token flooding 
 Theorems  10 and 11 reduce dependence, with contracts, public context, and initial states fixed, on the communication structure to a single question: which validated tokens reach which agents at which times . We now give a closed-form characterization of token exposure traces under a minimal dissemination layer: token flooding , where agents forward validated tokens verbatim along the communication graph. 
 Definition 31 (Communication graph and flooding) .

at each round t ≥ 0 t\geq 0 , each agent j j sends a message containing all tokens in 𝒦 j t \mathcal{K}_{j}^{t} to every out-neighbor.

 Remark 10 (Interpretation) . 
 Theorem  16 formalizes a sharp contrast with conformity-driven failures: increasing connectivity can accelerate the propagation of validated evidence (tokens), but it cannot create epistemic pressure in the absence of evidence because social-only events are forced into fallback behavior under evidence-gated enforcement.

 Remark 12 (Enforcement as semantic projection) . 
 The router normal-form results (Theorems  8 and 9 ) make the “output gate” semantics precise: once enforcement rejects unauditable (empty-witness) steps, any auditable trigger protocol behaves as if all of its non-fallback behavior were explicitly evidence-gated. In this sense, external enforcement projects arbitrary deliberation protocols down to the evidential PBRC shape. 
 Remark 13 (Why audit equivalence matters) . 
 Belief equivalence alone says two protocols behave the same internally. Audit equivalence strengthens this: an external compliance gate that only accepts nonempty-witness certificates will accept/reject the same updates under P i P_{i} and under NF ⁡ ( P i ) \mathrm{NF}(P_{i}) . Thus NF ⁡ ( P i ) \mathrm{NF}(P_{i}) is a canonical “PBRC-shaped” realization of the same auditable behavior. 
 Remark 14 (Interaction with minimality theorems) . 
 If P i P_{i} additionally satisfies evidence-free peer-pressure immunity and evidence-free non-amplification, then the social-only behavior absorbed into δ i NF \delta_{i}^{\mathrm{NF}} must be argmax-preserving and non-amplifying on social-only events. Combined with Theorems  4 – 6 , the compiled fallback retains these behavioral properties. This supports the chosen representation, but does not imply that robust protocols must originally have evidence-gated trigger syntax. 
 9 Adversary models and conditional robustness bounds 
 Evidence gating controls admissibility, not the truth or representativeness of evidence. The results below separate assumptions about evidence availability, authentication, semantic accuracy, context, and numerical execution. Language is inert under the token-invariance assumptions of Theorem  10 ; sound routing alone does not make every evidence-bearing contract insensitive to language. 
 9.1 Threat taxonomy 
 Definition 34 (Token appearance history) . 
 Seen < t ​ ( τ ) \mathrm{Seen}_{<t}(\tau) holds when some message transmitted strictly before round t t contained τ \tau . Initial possession is recorded separately. 
 Definition 35 (No-new-valid-evidence adversary) . 
 An adversary has no new valid evidence if every valid token it transmits has either been recorded initially or appeared earlier in the run. This is an availability restriction, stronger than ordinary signature unforgeability: an adversary may obtain and transmit a genuinely signed output for the first time without forging anything. 
 Definition 36 (Evidence-interface forgery) . 
 A forgery is an adversarial artifact accepted as authentic by the validity layer although it lacks the required authentic origin or authorization. An authentic but false tool result is a semantic integrity failure, not necessarily a signature forgery. 
 Definition 37 (Replay adversary) . 
 A replay adversary retransmits issued tokens without modifying their authenticated contents, potentially outside their intended time, instance, or use count. 
 Definition 38 (Colluding adversary) . 
 A colluding adversary coordinates controlled agents or validators, including their evidence selection and attestations. 
 Definition 39 (Omission/delay adversary) . 
 An omission/delay adversary withholds, delays, or selectively forwards evidence. This can obstruct correction or cause biased evidence-bearing updates; it is not exclusively a liveness attack. 
 9.2 Evidence-generation steering and omission attacks 
 Tokens are exogenous inputs to the present operational model. In an application, social messages can influence retrieval queries and tool inputs and thereby change which authentic tokens are generated. Selectively retaining supporting results can also produce a misleading evidence stream. The token-empty invariants survive omission, but correctness under evidence-bearing events needs additional assumptions. Authentication alone establishes neither balanced acquisition nor semantic truth. 
 A possible extension binds tokens to tool inputs and instance identifiers and adds a QueryOK \mathrm{QueryOK} predicate for a preregistered acquisition policy. Mandatory counter-evidence queries require an auditable record of required queries, returned results, and omissions. This is a proposed extension; the present experiments do not implement or validate it. 
 9.3 No-evidence robustness 
 Theorem 18 (Immunity in an initially token-empty system) . 
 Suppose the initial system contains no valid tokens, neither honest agents nor the environment generate new valid tokens, and adversaries satisfy Definition  35 . If honest agents use evidential contracts, sound enforcement, faithful numerical execution, and argmax-preserving fallback, their top sets remain unchanged. If fallback is also confidence-nonincreasing, their maximal confidence cannot increase. 
 Proof. 
 Induction on rounds shows that no transmitted token is valid: none exists initially, none is generated honestly, and an adversary cannot introduce a first valid token. Every exposure is therefore social-only. Evidential triggers are false, sound enforcement selects fallback, and the two conclusions follow by induction using the fallback properties.
∎ 
 9.4 Limits under evidence-interface compromise 
 Theorem 19 (Conditional effect of an accepted forged witness) .

Suppose branch j j is the full-event priority-selected branch, the adversary can supply a certificate passing every required check, the router accepts it, and numerical execution applies U j ​ ( b , E ) U_{j}(b,E) . If U j ​ ( b , E ) U_{j}(b,E) has a specified adverse property (for example, a false unique maximizer), that property holds after the step even if the certificate’s accepted evidence was forged. 
 Proof. 
 Acceptance and faithful numerical execution give b ′ = U j ​ ( b , E ) b^{\prime}=U_{j}(b,E) . Substitute this equality into the assumed adverse property.

 This conditional boundary result does not imply that forgeability alone forces a false belief. Rejection, a higher-priority branch, incomplete routing, or an operator that preserves correctness may prevent the hypothesized transition. Cryptographic integrity and semantic accuracy must be assessed separately.

 Theorem 22 (First-error classification) . 
 Assume an initially correct state, an evidential contract, argmax-preserving fallback, and a gate rejecting empty accepted witnesses. At a first transition from a correct state to an incorrect one, at least one of the following holds: 
 1. 
 numerical execution differs from the specified accepted operator or fallback; 
 2. 
 an accepted certificate fails full-event truth, priority, witness correctness, or valid-token membership; 
 3. 
 an accepted witness token fails freshness or context matching; 
 4. 
 an applicable accepted witness token is not truth-sound; 
 5. 
 the selected operator fails contract soundness on the actual event and input state.

In the remaining case all witness tokens are applicable and truth-sound, yet the selected specified operator maps a correct belief to an incorrect belief. This is precisely case 5.
∎ 
 This is an exhaustive conditional classification, not an algorithm that identifies a unique cause from a certificate. The cases can overlap; evaluating truth-soundness requires external ground truth, and omitted evidence may be causally relevant.

 If execution is faithful, routing is sound, all accepted witnesses are applicable, evidence integrity holds, the contract is ( h ⋆ , Δ ) (h^{\star},\Delta) -sound, and fallback preserves argmax, an initially correct run remains correct. 
 Proof. 
 Each possible first-error case in Theorem  22 is excluded.
∎

Consequently the first loss of correctness, if any, has the same index and canonical certificate in both. If the applicability annotations also agree, the classification above agrees as a truth-relative statement. 
 Proof.

 11 Design and implementation guidelines 
 PBRC is intentionally protocol-level : it specifies which public artifacts (contracts, tokens, certificates, and audit traces) must exist for belief change to be admissible and verifiable, while remaining agnostic to how agents generate messages. The goal is to preserve open communication while making changes of mind evidence-gated, auditable, and (under a state-holding router) enforceable. 
 This section distills implementation guidance that preserves the paper’s formal guarantees in deployed multi-agent systems. The recurring theme is to (i) keep triggers witnessable by small token sets, (ii) separate cryptographic validity from semantic applicability, and (iii) choose conservative operators and fallbacks that make safety robust to missing or delayed evidence. 
 11.1 Contract patterns (templates) 
 In practice, many contracts can be assembled from a small library of reusable patterns. For predictability and efficiency, these are best restricted to token-witnessable fragments such as FO ∃ ⁣ ∧ 𝖳𝗈𝗄 \mathrm{FO}^{\mathsf{Tok}}_{\exists\wedge} (Definition  46 ), where admissibility can be certified by a small witness set and checked locally (Propositions  8 – 9 ). 
 Verified contradiction (falsifier pattern). 
A conservative default is to permit decreases in belief mass (or argmax flips away from a hypothesis) only when a validated token contradicts that hypothesis, e.g., 
 ∃ τ ⁡ ( Valid ⁡ ( τ ) ∧ Contradicts ⁡ ( τ , h ) ) . \exists\tau\;\big(\mathrm{Valid}(\tau)\wedge\mathrm{Contradicts}(\tau,h)\big). 
 Operationally, contradiction triggers are the primary safeguard against “wrong-but-sure” cascades driven by social pressure: if no validated contradictor exists, social exchange cannot justify moving probability mass aggressively. 
 Verified support (bounded confirmation). 
When positive evidence is available, include support triggers of the form 
 ∃ τ ⁡ ( Valid ⁡ ( τ ) ∧ Supports ⁡ ( τ , h ) ) , \exists\tau\;\big(\mathrm{Valid}(\tau)\wedge\mathrm{Supports}(\tau,h)\big), 
 but couple them with bounded operators (e.g., capped log-odds steps or margin-based updates) to avoid runaway overconfidence from a single token or noisy verifier. In many settings, it is appropriate to require stronger conditions for increasing confidence than for decreasing it (e.g., support requires k k independent attestations; contradiction requires one strong falsifier). 
 Freshness gating (replay resistance). 
To control replay and context drift, augment evidence triggers with freshness predicates: 
 ∃ τ ⁡ ( Valid ⁡ ( τ ) ∧ Supports ⁡ ( τ , h ) ∧ Fresh Δ ​ ( τ , t ) ) , \exists\tau\;\big(\mathrm{Valid}(\tau)\wedge\mathrm{Supports}(\tau,h)\wedge\mathrm{Fresh}_{\Delta}(\tau,t)\big), 
 and analogously for contradiction. This implements the theoretical replay bounds (Theorem  20 ) and is typically cheap in the logical layer (constant-time arithmetic checks per token). 
 Multi-attestation (robustness to validator compromise). 
When the validity layer admits attestations, replace single-token conditions by quorum-like requirements (“ k k -of- K K ”) at the validity layer (Section  9 ). This reduces the impact of any single compromised validator or verifier and yields an explicit robustness–liveness trade-off: larger k k improves integrity but may delay admissible revision when evidence is sparse. 
 Surprise clauses (novel or unmodeled evidence). 
Because preregistration can be brittle, include a low-priority clause that activates when a validated token is present but does not match existing templates, e.g., 
 ∃ τ ⁡ ( Valid ⁡ ( τ ) ∧ Unmodeled ⁡ ( τ ) ) . \exists\tau\;\big(\mathrm{Valid}(\tau)\wedge\mathrm{Unmodeled}(\tau)\big). 
 Within the fixed-state model the corresponding operator should conservatively hold or dilute belief. Deferral actions, tool calls, and hypothesis expansion require extensions to the state and action semantics (Section  3.2 ). Importantly, surprise clauses do not weaken the anti-conformity story: in the token-empty regime they never fire, and social-only exchange remains routed to fallback. 
 Budget clauses (availability and DoS resilience). 
A practical attack surface is flooding events with many tokens (valid but irrelevant) to inflate validation latency. Two complementary mitigations are recommended:
(i) use positive witnessable triggers so checking can stop once the highest-priority branch is established; lower-priority branches also require ruling out all higher-priority triggers (Section  13.7 ); and
(ii) enforce explicit per-round validation budgets with a safe defer/hold fallback when budgets are exceeded. Budget predicates are naturally router-side (policy-level) and can be treated as part of the enforcement environment rather than the agent’s private computation. 
 11.2 Token validity and semantic applicability 
 A common deployment failure is to conflate cryptographic validity with semantic applicability . In PBRC , the validity layer is the boundary at which evidence becomes admissible; if this boundary is underspecified, the system becomes vulnerable to “citation laundering” (valid provenance, irrelevant content) or to entailment mislabeling (valid tokens, incorrect Supports/Contradicts mapping). 
 A robust token schema should therefore bind evidence to task context and support auditing. Concretely, tokens should (at minimum) include:
(i) a provenance binding (signatures/attestations),
(ii) a timestamp (for freshness checks),
(iii) a context binding (instance identifier, prompt hash, or task context hash),
and (iv) typed content (tool output schema, retrieval snippet hash, verifier judgment metadata).
At the predicate level, the router should be able to expose at least: 
 Valid ⁡ ( τ ) , Fresh Δ ​ ( τ , t ) , Type ⁡ ( τ , ⋅ ) , \mathrm{Valid}(\tau),\quad\mathrm{Fresh}_{\Delta}(\tau,t),\quad\mathrm{Type}(\tau,\cdot), 
 and, when feasible, a separate predicate capturing applicability (e.g., “the snippet is about the claim”), distinct from semantic entailment.
When entailment-based labeling is used, auditability improves substantially if tokens record the verifier identity/version and the exact inputs used to compute the label (so that the Supports/Contradicts relation is reproducible). 
 Finally, because social influence can act indirectly by steering evidence generation (biased queries, selective tool calls), the validity layer should, where possible, bind tokens to the inputs that generated them (query hashes, tool-call arguments). This supports query-policy compliance mechanisms (Section  9.2 ) and helps prevent conformity-by-proxy.

 11.4 Supplementary finite token-set implementation 
 We implement a state-holding reference router for finite token-set predicates. It selects the highest-priority true predicate on the full validated set, requires a nonempty valid witness satisfying the selected predicate on restriction, executes the registered operator, and checks the numerical output before committing state. The default witness is the full validated set; this is sound for the implemented token-set semantics but does not promise a small certificate. Custom extractors are checked independently. An incomplete decision rejects the selected branch and uses fallback; it does not discard tokens and then accept a lower-priority branch. Coverage is reported as unobserved if the supplied horizon ends before closure. 
 The validator and its version identifier are mandatory, and tokens with conflicting contents under one identifier are rejected. Logs retain full events, validation decisions, proposed and executed certificates, failures, and before/after beliefs. Deterministic replay additionally requires immutable callback code, parameters, and context. The 27 regression tests cover witness sufficiency, full-event truth, priority, validation, contextual freshness, numerical failures, state integrity, random-state separation, and knowledge/exposure distinctions. An invalid fallback raises without committing a numerical state. 
 The implementation assumes well-typed input objects and trusted local callbacks; it implements neither signatures nor arbitrary first-order satisfaction, and its logs are not tamper-evident. The probability-vector check permits a 10 − 12 10^{-12} rounding tolerance in normalization. Thus these finite checks support the implementation within its stated scope, not a floating-point proof of the real-arithmetic theorems or a production-security claim. 
 11.5 Contract verification and synthesis 
 Contracts can be verified against global safety invariants expressed in CDDL (Section  10 ) and audited post hoc using full inputs, decisions, certificates, and numerical state transitions (Proposition  6 ). A finite log check establishes only that recorded execution; a global guarantee requires a proof or an explicit transition model covering all allowed executions. Beyond manual design, an important research direction is contract synthesis : propose candidate trigger/operator sets from logs and desired invariants (e.g., “no top-hypothesis flips without evidence”), then validate candidates by model checking against the contract logic and by empirical stress tests under verifier noise, incomplete routers, and adversarial token floods. Even in the absence of fully automated synthesis, the template library above provides a practical starting point for systematic contract engineering.

 12.1 Separate costs and complete checks 
 Let n = | 𝒯 ⁡ ( E ) | n=|\mathcal{T}(E)| , let k k count quantified token variables, and let L L denote formula encoding size. The cost of reading the event and authenticating its tokens is separate from logical checking. A complete accepted decision must establish event membership and validity of witness tokens, restricted witness satisfaction, full-event trigger truth, priority selection, and correct numerical execution. A schematic accounting is 
 C step = C parse + C validate + C witness + C full / priority + C update + C log . C_{\rm step}=C_{\rm parse}+C_{\rm validate}+C_{\rm witness}+C_{\rm full/priority}+C_{\rm update}+C_{\rm log}. 
 For monotone positive triggers a supplied witness can establish full-event truth. It does not by itself show that all higher-priority triggers are false. With nonmonotone formulas, even full-event truth of the named trigger needs an additional check. Signature checking and semantic labeling costs depend on the chosen implementation; they are not constant by assumption here. 
 12.2 A guarded existential conjunctive fragment 
 Definition 46 (Guarded token-existential conjunctive triggers) . 
 A trigger in FO ∃ ⁣ ∧ 𝖳𝗈𝗄 \mathrm{FO}^{\mathsf{Tok}}_{\exists\wedge} has the form 
 χ = ∃ τ 1 ⋯ ∃ τ k ( ⋀ r = 1 k Valid ( τ r ) ∧ ⋀ m = 1 M R m ( τ ¯ m , c ¯ m ) ) , k ≥ 1 . \chi=\exists\tau_{1}\cdots\exists\tau_{k}\left(\bigwedge_{r=1}^{k}\mathrm{Valid}(\tau_{r})\land\bigwedge_{m=1}^{M}R_{m}(\bar{\tau}_{m},\bar{c}_{m})\right),\qquad k\geq 1. 
 The R m R_{m} are positive atomic predicates, with equality and disequality permitted, over token variables and fixed public parameters.  If E ⊧ χ E\models\chi , a satisfying assignment σ = ( τ 1 , … , τ k ) \sigma=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{k}) has image W ⊆ 𝒯 ⁡ ( E ) W\subseteq\mathcal{T}(E) with 1 ≤ | W | ≤ k 1\leq|W|\leq k , and W W witnesses χ \chi .  Proof. 
 Validity guards place each assigned value in 𝒯 ⁡ ( E ) \mathcal{T}(E) ; k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 makes its image nonempty. Reading the assignment and checking every conjunct once gives the stated cost.
∎ 
 A witness set alone need not identify which token fills which variable. Reconstructing an assignment may require search; enumerating n k n^{k} tuples gives a simple O ​ ( n k ​ ( k + L ) ) O(n^{k}(k+L)) bound. Thus inexpensive verification of a supplied assignment must not be confused with uniformly efficient witness search when k k is part of the input. 
 Proposition 9 (Canonical assignment witnesses) . 
 Fix a public total order on token identifiers. On a satisfying finite event, choose the lexicographically least satisfying k k -tuple and return its image and assignment payload. On an unsatisfying event return the empty result. This defines a sound nonvacuous witness extractor; it is token-invariant when the underlying predicates and parameters are token-invariant. 
 Proof. 
 There are finitely many tuples, so a nonempty set of satisfying tuples has a unique least member. Proposition  8 proves witness correctness and nonemptiness. Equal annotated token inputs yield the same satisfying tuples, public order, least tuple, and image. Social-only inputs have no tuple satisfying the validity guards.
∎ 
 Canonical selection can require more work than finding any witness. The existence theorem does not assert an efficient canonicalization algorithm for variable-size queries. 
 12.3 General first-order triggers and caching 
 A fixed first-order query on a finite explicitly represented structure can be evaluated by recursively enumerating quantified variables, in polynomial data complexity with an exponent depending on the query. This does not give a uniform polynomial bound in query size or guarantee small token witnesses for unrestricted formulas. Full-event negation, universal conditions, and priority checks may require examining data outside an accepted witness. 
 Under the full assumptions of token-trace equivalence, replay can use complete annotated token exposures, the initial belief, contracts, deterministic operator semantics, and router decisions (or deterministic complete selection). Proposition  6 explains why accepted witnesses alone are insufficient. Cache keys must include the relevant context, current time, validator/schema version, and revocation state. Authentication of immutable contents may be reusable while freshness and applicability still require reevaluation. 
 Budget exhaustion should cause a logged fallback or deferral decision. Early stopping is justified for a positive highest-priority trigger after a sufficient assignment is found; accepting a lower-priority trigger additionally requires certifying the absence of a higher-priority match. Arbitrary first-order contracts do not inherit the short-circuit cost of the simple toy experiment.

 13.10 SciFact: evidence-enabled claim verification 
 The SciFact [ Wadden et al.(2020) ] experiment evaluates answer admission using retrieved scientific passages and an imperfect NLI validator. It measures categorical answer-level behavior under unverified persuasive messages, complementing the token-empty KAIROS analysis. This instantiation does not implement the full numerical-belief model or KD45/PDL monitor. 
 Data, protocol, and models. 
 The primary main200 profile selects the first 200 of the 300 public development claims under the SHA-256 ordering of 20260730:claim_id . Selection uses no labels. Their gold classes are 82 SUPPORT, 41 CONTRADICT, and 77 NOT_ENOUGH_INFO (NEI). All 809 training claims are reserved for calibration and have disjoint IDs. Texts, labels, and evidence-document IDs come from the official SciFact archive identified in the protocol. These are development-set claim classifications; they are not official test-set or joint rationale-retrieval leaderboard scores. 
 The subject, treatment generator, and query generator use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [ Qwen Team(2024) ] through vLLM on the RunPod BF16 backend, at temperature zero and request seed zero. Maximum output lengths are 180, 260, and 64 tokens, respectively. The model revision is a09a35458c702b33eeacc393d103063234e8bc28 . The NLI model is MoritzLaurer/DeBERTa-v3-base-mnli-fever-anli [ Laurer(n.d.) ] , revision 6f5cf0a2b59cabb106aca4c287eed12e357e90eb , with a 384-token input limit. Hardware and library differences can prevent bit-identical fresh inference even with these settings. 
 The protocol fixes model revisions, configuration, selected IDs, and calibration before held-out generation; predictions are finalized before scoring. Generation reads unlabeled claims and the corpus, with a software guard blocking access to the score-only gold directory. Hashes record the local configuration; this procedure is not an independent public preregistration or a security boundary against administrator access. Held-out claims are excluded from tuning in this study; their absence from model pretraining is not established.

 BM25 searches all 5,183 corpus abstracts using the claim alone, with k 1 = 1.5 k_{1}=1.5 and b = 0.75 b=0.75 . It retrieves eight documents and selects two sentences per document by lexical score, supplying up to 16 title-plus-sentence passages. A separate acquisition condition uses a model-generated query influenced by the majority messages. NLI treats each passage as premise and the claim as hypothesis. Let s s and c c be the maximum entailment and contradiction scores across retrieved passages. It abstains when max ⁡ ( s , c ) < θ \max(s,c)<\theta or | s − c | < m |s-c|<m ; otherwise it selects SUPPORT if s > c s>c , and CONTRADICT otherwise. There were no equal-maxima ties in either held-out stream. 
 Training IDs are partitioned deterministically into 561 tuning and 248 internal-validation claims. The 45-point grid combines θ ∈ { 0.50 , 0.55 , … , 0.90 } \theta\in\{0.50,0.55,\ldots,0.90\} and m ∈ { 0 , 0.05 , … , 0.20 } m\in\{0,0.05,\ldots,0.20\} . No setting met the 0.8 target. The selected ( θ , m ) = ( 0.90 , 0 ) (\theta,m)=(0.90,0) has tuning precision 0.560 and internal-validation precision 0.508.  Parsing, checks, and scope. 
 All four subject parse errors and seven failed peer parses remain in the analysis; query parsing has no failures. Subject parse failure maps to NEI, which is also a scored class, rather than automatically counting as incorrect. A successful parse does not guarantee prompt compliance. For example, RAW supplies nonempty citations despite receiving no passages in 120/200 outputs. Clean RAG, clean RAG with majority, and steered RAG cite at least one document absent from their prompts on 13, six, and nine claims, respectively. The full gate requires the token’s document to be cited but does not reject every additional spurious citation. All scores use this same fixed parsing and admission policy. 
 The 108 non-abstaining clean tokens support controlled context, stale-token, and payload-mutation checks: full PBRC rejects every mutated case while the corresponding check-removal ablation accepts all; intact controls are accepted. These are secondary mechanism tests with constructed matching candidates, not naturally occurring attack rates. The token integrity field is an unkeyed SHA-256 content hash, not a digital signature: an attacker able to replace the token can recompute it. Freshness uses the fixed protocol clock 1700000000 + 1 1700000000+1 and a seven-day expiry interval, not observed wall-clock replay protection. The study therefore tests trusted in-process admission and syntactic consistency, not adversarial issuer authentication or semantic correctness. 
 The results quantify a conditional reduction in admitted social-pressure changes alongside retained errors and missed corrections. Generalization is limited by the use of one model, one selected development subset, one imperfect validator, and one fixed pressure protocol. 
 14 Limitations and open problems 
 The guarantees concern a fixed finite hypothesis space, explicit numerical beliefs, specified event semantics, and a faithful enforcement boundary. They do not show that an LLM internally represents these probabilities or reasons according to the contract. Hypothesis generation, changing ontologies, asynchronous delivery, expiry, revocation, and stateful spent-token tracking require additional formal treatment. 
 Evidence gating preserves the chosen fallback invariants on token-empty rounds. It does not guarantee truth, calibrated probabilities, useful learning, balanced evidence acquisition, or eventual correction. An overly restrictive contract can permanently retain an incorrect initial top hypothesis. Omission and query steering can bias evidence-bearing updates. An accepted witness establishes a contractual license; it is neither a complete numerical replay record nor proof of a unique causal explanation. 
 Token invariance is conditional on fixed context and annotations. General contracts may depend on message metadata, and sound-but-incomplete routers may make different acceptance decisions on token-equivalent events. Exact trajectory equivalence therefore needs the stated operator, fallback, and router assumptions. The normal-form construction needs suitable witnesses and first-order definability; it is not a representation theorem for arbitrary computational policies. Syntactic evidence gating is sufficient but not necessary for top preservation and confidence non-amplification. 
 Freshness limits the age of usable evidence but does not prevent repeated use inside a window. Authentication proves origin under a threat model, not semantic truth. Multi-attestation bounds require distinct authenticated identities and strong assumptions about honest validators and compromise dependence. Numerical claims must be enforced by a state-holding router or a correct operator-verification mechanism. 
 The logical results here establish soundness and exact monitoring of explicitly supplied finite models. All-model completeness and the finite-model property remain open questions for the combined system. Finite-prefix monitoring does not prove unobserved future behavior, and the belief modality needs semantics beyond a numerical log. 
 The simulations use idealized dynamics, with simulations I–V evaluated at one seed. The matched study uses fixed evidence weights, informative synthetic source claims, controlled label errors, and one pooling comparator. In KAIROS, token-empty gating preserves RAW by construction, while incomplete generation metadata limits exact replication. In SciFact, PBRC has no statistically established improvement over RAW and lower observed accuracy than clean RAG. The validator fails to meet the calibration precision target and admits incorrect answers; unsupported empirical assertions in peer messages preclude a pure-rhetoric interpretation. Claim dependence, one model/backend, a single selected development subset, and unadjusted comparisons limit generalization. Unkeyed token hashes and a synthetic protocol clock do not implement authenticated evidence or real-time replay security. Neither the simulation code nor the finite token-set reference router supplies a production semantic validator. 
 15 Conclusion 
 PBRC makes admissible belief changes a public contract: a selected evidence-bearing branch requires a checked witness and faithfully executed operator, while other steps use a declared fallback. Argmax-preserving, non-amplifying fallback yields precise token-empty invariants. A local transition characterization explains why those invariants do not require every safe protocol to use syntactic evidence gates. 
 Under the stated definability and witness assumptions, evidence splitting yields a behavior-preserving normal form; sound enforcement and token invariance support conditional trace equivalences. Flooding separates accumulated knowledge, governed by shortest-path distance, from incoming exposures, governed by positive-length walks. The logic supplies sound specifications and finite-log monitoring with an explicit operational bridge. 
 These results make admissibility and selected failure modes inspectable while leaving evidence truth, acquisition bias, numerical compliance, and practical utility as explicit obligations. The matched synthetic study shows beneficial revision but no final-accuracy gain over its evidence-only updater at the lower thresholds, and correlated errors defeat the benefit of distinct issuer identities. KAIROS experiments demonstrate the benefits and costs of retaining an initial answer. In SciFact, evidence-enabled PBRC attenuates the degradation associated with the specified majority-pressure condition, but admits wrong answers, has an inconclusive gain over RAW, and falls below clean RAG accuracy. This supports an auditable admission mechanism with explicit assumptions, not a general claim that stricter evidence gating improves reasoning.

 A.2 Finite-model algorithm and correctness 
 Let W W be the supplied finite state set. Represent a formula by its truth set and a program by a binary relation on W W . Atoms use the supplied valuation; negation uses complement in W W ; conjunction uses intersection. For B i ​ φ B_{i}\varphi , retain exactly states whose R i R_{i} successors all belong to the already computed truth set of φ \varphi . For [ α ] ​ φ [\alpha]\varphi , use the same universal-successor operation with the computed R α R_{\alpha} .

 Mutual induction on the finite syntax trees of formulas and programs now proves each computed set and relation equals its semantic interpretation. Tests contain strictly smaller formula subtrees, and modalities contain strictly smaller program and formula subtrees, so the recursion is well founded. Using Boolean matrices and a Floyd–Warshall transitive-closure computation, an implementation takes at most O ⁡ ( L ​ | W | 3 ) O(L|W|^{3}) time and O ⁡ ( L ​ | W | 2 ) O(L|W|^{2}) storage, where L L is total formula/program syntax size; this deliberately coarse bound includes closure and composition. Event parsing, token validation, and first-order trigger evaluation are separate preprocessing costs. This proves Theorem  24 . 
 For a finite run, Lemma  6 supplies the action relation. Enumerating reachable source indices then checking their actual successors returns the earliest violation of either displayed PBRC invariant. No doxastic accessibility relation is inferred from numerical beliefs.
